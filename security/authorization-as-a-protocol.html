Authorization as a Protocol

Authorization is the layer that resists tooling hardest, and the reason is worth stating exactly: an authorization bug is usually a valid program implementing the wrong relation. Nothing is malformed. The types check, the tests pass, the scanner is quiet, and the code does precisely what it says — which is the wrong thing.

That makes it the residue left over after designing-out-defect-classes (which removes representation errors) and after ambient-authority-and-blast-radius (which bounds consequence). This note is about the relation itself.

Confidence legend: ◐ = corroborated across independent secondary sources, primary not obtained in this pass · ○ = mechanism argued, not a measured result.

Three properties that defeat static tooling

○ Authorization defects have a shape that is hostile to every tool that works on one artifact at a time:

  • Cross-file and cross-service. Identity is established in one place, resource ownership in another, policy in a third, and the effect in a fourth. No single file contains the bug; the bug is the relation between four files.
  • Temporal. Roles, consent, suspension, delegation, and object state all change. “User A may edit this” is true until a revocation lands, and a cached decision, a group snapshot, or an in-flight retry can carry the old answer past it.
  • Contextual. The same action may be permitted only below a threshold, within a region, during an incident, or subject to separation of duties.

What each tool actually sees: SAST sees code; DAST sees the executions its crawler happened to generate; an LLM sees whatever was retrieved into its context. ○ None of them possesses the authoritative business relation, because in most systems that relation is not written down anywhere — it lives in a product manager’s head, a Jira thread, and four handlers that disagree. This is the specification ceiling from adversarial-vs-reliability-reasoning in its most concrete form.

The protocol

○ The response is to stop treating authorization as a check and start treating it as a protocol with a stated contract:

  1. One decision shape: principal × action × resource × context → decision. Every enforcement point speaks it.
  2. Centralize the semantics and version them. One place defines what the rules mean. The version is part of every decision.
  3. Enforce at every effect boundary, not only at the UI or API edge. The edge is where requests arrive; effects happen deeper, and background jobs, queue consumers, admin tools, and migrations never pass the edge at all.
  4. Bind cached decisions to policy version and relevant state, so a revocation invalidates rather than waits out a TTL.
  5. Generate negative and metamorphic tests from the policy (below).
  6. Log decision inputs and committed effects out of band — see post-breach-investigability.
  7. Deny on missing tenant, stale identity, or indeterminate policy. Fail-closed is only real if “I don’t know” is on the deny side.

Note that centralizing semantics is not centralizing enforcement. The decision object should be minted centrally and then carried — which is exactly where this meets capabilities: central policy decides whether to mint; the capability constrains what the holder may then do. Either layer alone has a characteristic failure — a central engine alone creates an online dependency and a single semantic failure domain; local grants alone become ungoverned bearer authority with no way to change policy globally.

○ The honest cost: a policy service can become a latency bottleneck and a single point of semantic failure. The answer is not scattering ad-hoc checks back through the handlers; it is replicated enforcement with explicit policy-version and cache semantics, so you know what a stale replica is allowed to answer.

Testing a relation: metamorphic properties

○ This is the practical payoff, and it is underused. You usually cannot write down the whole policy as an oracle — but you can write down invariants the policy must satisfy under transformation, which is testable without knowing every rule:

  • Monotonicity of privilege — removing a privilege must never increase the set of reachable effects.
  • Tenant substitution — changing the tenant in a request must never preserve a resource handle’s validity.
  • Idempotency — replaying an idempotency key must not multiply a value transfer.
  • Revocation liveness — after revocation lands, no cached path may still commit the effect.
  • Self-approval — no principal may satisfy both sides of a separation-of-duties requirement.

These are the tests that stay green in a codebase with the bug and go red the moment someone refactors authorization badly. Unlike a fuzzer’s crash oracle, they have a real oracle for “valid but forbidden” — which is precisely the class security-assurance-tooling-ceilings shows fuzzing structurally cannot see.

Automation is possible — under one condition

○ “Authorization cannot be automated” is too strong, and the exceptions teach the rule.

◐ Two older research systems found genuine, previously-unreported authorization flaws at scale. RoleCast (Son, McKinley & Shmatikov, 2011) inferred role-consistency patterns across PHP/JSP applications — if most handlers in a role perform a check and one does not, that inconsistency is a signal. AuthScope (Zuo, Zhao & Lin, 2017) used differential traffic analysis across thousands of Android apps — substitute a protocol field, observe whether the response changes in a way that indicates the substitution was honored.

(Both papers report specific counts of flaws found; those figures were not re-verified in this pass and are deliberately omitted rather than quoted.)

The condition they share is the transferable part: each had a narrow inconsistency oracle or a substitutable protocol field. Neither infers arbitrary unwritten policy, and neither proves absence when no differential appears. So the automation question for your own system is not “can a tool check authorization” but “is there an internal consistency I can make a tool compare against?” Where handlers should be uniform, deviation is machine-findable. Where every rule is bespoke, it is not.

Where this meets generated code

The AI-specific instance is already in the vault and is the sharpest illustration of the whole note: vibe-coding-authorization-flaws documents generated applications whose front-end code is flawless while server-side enforcement was never configured — the flaw is an absence, in a system the diff never touches. This note is the design-side answer to it. If authorization is a protocol with an explicit decision shape and metamorphic tests, “nobody wrote the policy” becomes a failing test rather than an invisible omission.

The caveat that note raises applies here too and should not be waved away: an agent that omitted the enforcement will just as cheerfully omit the negative test, or write one that passes against its own assumptions. The metamorphic properties above are more resistant than hand-written negative tests precisely because they are stated over transformations rather than over specific expected answers — but they still have to come from someone who wants the system to be secure.

Counterargument

The strongest case against this note is that most teams should not build any of it. A single-tenant application with three roles gets more security from a well-reviewed middleware and a couple of integration tests than from a policy service, a version field, and a metamorphic suite — and the elaborate version introduces its own outage mode, where a policy-engine failure takes down a system that would otherwise have been merely wrong for one user.

It also assumes the business relation is knowable at design time. Frequently it is not: the rules are genuinely under negotiation, and encoding them prematurely produces a versioned, audited, precisely-enforced encoding of a policy nobody agreed to — the “verifier proves the wrong policy perfectly” failure with extra infrastructure.

The threshold worth naming: multi-tenancy and irreversible effects. Once one customer’s data can be reached through another customer’s request, or an action moves money or deletes state, the relation becomes load-bearing and ad-hoc checks stop being defensible. Below that line, steps 1, 3, and 7 alone (one decision shape, enforce at effects, deny on unknown) capture most of the value.
