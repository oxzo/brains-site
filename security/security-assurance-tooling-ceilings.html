Security Assurance Tooling — Ceilings and Economics

Assurance tools pay when three things are narrow: the question, the oracle, and the workflow. Without all three, “coverage” is mostly activity. This note is what each major tool family can be asked, what it structurally cannot see, and what the finding actually costs once a human has to act on it.

It is the measurement half of adversarial-vs-reliability-reasoning’s claim-layer table — each row there needs a different oracle, and this is which tool supplies which.

Confidence legend: ✅ = primary source fetched and quoted text matched verbatim during this pass · ○ = mechanism argued, not a measured result.

There is no portable “SAST precision”

○ Precision varies by rule, language, framework, repository, configuration, and — most of all — whether findings are filtered to changed code. Published tool comparisons often use seeded or labeled corpora that behave nothing like production. A vendor precision number is a claim about their corpus, not about yours.

What generalizes is the workflow, and Google’s Tricorder is the best-documented instance.

✅ From Sadowski et al., Tricorder: Building a Program Analysis Ecosystem (ICSE 2015), fetched and quoted verbatim:

  • Scale and staffing: Tricorder “generates some 93,000 analysis results each day. A small team of 2-3 people maintain TRICORDER and the ecosystem around it.”
  • The code-review threshold: “We still enforce a very low effective false positive rate here (< 10%). Additionally, we only display results for most analyses on changed lines by default.”
  • Latency: “we typically expect analyses to complete in less than 5 − 10 minutes (ideally much less), as developers may be waiting for results.”
  • The stricter bar for build-integrated analyses: “the effective false positive rate must be essentially zero. They also cannot significantly slow down compiles, so must have < 5% overhead.”
  • The contract: they reserve the right to disable an analyzer if nobody fixes bugs filed against it, if it hurts performance, or if “the analyzer results are annoying developers.”

✅ The most transferable idea is a redefinition, not a threshold. Tricorder measures the effective false positive: “any report from the tool where a user chooses not to take action to resolve the report.” That is a developer-perspective metric, deliberately not a correctness metric — as the paper puts it, “developers will decide whether an analysis has high impact, and what a false positive is.” A technically-correct finding nobody acts on counts against the tool.

✅ One more design choice worth stealing: they “got rid of all priority or severity ratings for analysis results,” on the reasoning that only high-value results should be shown at all, and that letting users filter hides broken analyzers instead of fixing them.

○ Meta’s published account of deploying Infer at scale reaches similar workflow conclusions from a different codebase. Neither establishes that any scanner works on your TypeScript service. What they establish is the mechanism for adoption: findings arrive on the responsible change, while the author still has context and ownership; checks are fast enough that no bypass market forms; noisy analyzers get demoted or repaired; and fixes are measured, not alerts.

DAST and SCA, briefly

○ DAST buys runtime realism and can expose deployment and configuration behavior that source analysis never sees. It loses source-level causality, and it only sees states its crawler, accounts, test data, and timing actually reach — which is a much smaller set than the reachable state space.

○ SCA is excellent at inventory and version matching. “Package present” is not “vulnerable path reachable,” and the gap between those two is where nearly all the wasted triage time goes. The economic win comes from reachability, exploit evidence, exposure, ownership, and upgrade feasibility — not from another undifferentiated alert. What fuzzing structurally misses

○ A fuzzer needs four things: a generator, reachability, an execution budget, and an oracle. Crashes and sanitizer violations are strong, generic oracles — which is why fuzzing is so effective on parsers and so weak elsewhere. “User A can read User B’s draft after role revocation during a retry” is not a crash.

The structural blind spots follow directly:

  • semantically valid but unauthorized outcomes;
  • long, stateful, multi-service sequences;
  • rare identity, clock, queue, and cache combinations;
  • environment-dependent behavior absent from the harness;
  • economic abuse with no crisp threshold;
  • anything whose wrongness requires external business knowledge to recognize.

○ The Magma ground-truth benchmark makes a related point that matters more than any leaderboard: it distinguishes reaching a bug from triggering it, and finds no universally best fuzzer across targets. The lesson is not that fuzzing is weak — it is that harness and oracle design dominate tool choice.

○ For a web system the split is clean: fuzz parsers and pure state machines hard; use property-based and metamorphic testing for authorization and workflows. The metamorphic relations for authorization are in authorization-as-a-protocol, and they exist precisely because that class has no generic oracle.

Where formal methods paid

○ AWS reported using TLA+ to find subtle design errors in distributed systems that survived ordinary design review, code review, and testing. seL4 established machine-checked refinement between a formal specification and a C implementation under explicit assumptions. These are strong existence proofs and weak universal ROI estimates — both are heavily selected cases.

○ The conditions under which a target becomes tractable are the useful part:

  • the state machine is smaller than the implementation;
  • semantics and interfaces are stable;
  • invariants can be written without encoding the whole business;
  • the verified component is reused enough to amortize the proof;
  • the consequence of a subtle error justifies specialized labor;
  • assumptions about compiler, hardware, crypto, configuration, and operations are made explicit.

○ And when they fail economically: when the specification changes as fast as the code, when intent is still contested, or — the common one — when a team verifies a clean core while unverified adapters hold the real authority. For a TypeScript organization the right target is almost never “verify the application.” It is the policy algebra, the money/state transition, the token-minting rule, or the replication protocol whose error would dominate loss.

Counterargument

This note’s framing — narrow question, narrow oracle, ruthless noise control — optimizes for developer acceptance, and developer acceptance is not the same as security. Tricorder’s effective-false-positive metric makes that explicit: a finding developers decline to fix is scored against the tool, which is exactly backwards when the finding is a real vulnerability that is merely tedious to fix. A regime tuned to sub-10% annoyance will systematically demote the noisy, high-recall, hard-to-triage detectors — and those are disproportionately the ones that find authorization and injection flaws rather than style issues.

The reconciliation is the non-gating research channel plus seeded-defect recall above; without both, “our static analysis has high acceptance” describes a tool that has been tuned into harmlessness. It is also worth being clear that Tricorder’s numbers come from an organization with a dedicated platform team, a monorepo, and a strong review culture — the thresholds transfer as a design pattern, not as targets a small team can hit.
