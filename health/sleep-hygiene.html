Sleep Hygiene and Sleep Architecture

What it is

Sleep hygiene is the set of behavioral and environmental conditions that determine whether the body actually completes its overnight restoration work — not whether you’re “asleep” but whether the structured sequence of NREM (slow-wave) and REM cycles runs to completion. Insufficient or fragmented sleep is one of the most-replicated risk factors for cognitive impairment, mood dysregulation, metabolic disease, and elevated all-cause mortality.

For an adult in normal training and cognitive load, the consensus recommendation from the U.S. National Sleep Foundation and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine is 7–9 hours per 24h (with substantial individual variation — a small minority genuinely thrive on less or need more).

Why it matters

Sleep is the single highest-leverage modifiable input on next-day cognitive performance, mood baseline, immune function, and training adaptation:

  • Memory consolidation runs primarily during sleep — slow-wave sleep (SWS) for declarative material, REM for procedural and emotional consolidation. Sleep loss directly steals the consolidation pass that active-recall, spaced-repetition, and memory-consolidation depend on
  • Glymphatic clearance of metabolic waste (including amyloid-β) is markedly elevated during sleep — chronic sleep restriction is implicated in elevated long-term neurodegenerative risk
  • HPA-axis regulation depends on sleep — fragmented sleep raises baseline cortisol, which feeds into the same prefrontal suppression mechanism described in sidi-stress-cognition
  • Hormonal recovery (growth hormone pulses during SWS, testosterone synthesis, leptin/ghrelin balance) is concentrated in deep sleep, making sleep the rate-limiter on training adaptation as much as nutrition is

For the user’s profile (cyclist, lead web developer, ~270W FTP target): sleep is the multiplier on everything else in this vault. A bad night’s sleep degrades both Zwift wattage and the cognitive headroom for code review and architectural decisions described in cognitive-burden-of-ai-use.

Sleep architecture, briefly

A normal night cycles roughly 4–6 times through:

  • NREM 1 (light, ~5%): transition from wake; very arousable
  • NREM 2 (~50%): bulk of sleep; sleep spindles and K-complexes; some memory consolidation
  • NREM 3 / SWS (~15–25%): “deep sleep”; concentrated in the first half of the night; growth hormone, glymphatic clearance, declarative memory consolidation
  • REM (~20–25%): dreams; concentrated in the second half of the night; emotional and procedural memory consolidation

Practical implication: going to bed late truncates REM, getting up early truncates SWS — the cost of cutting sleep is not symmetric. Cutting the last 90 minutes of an 8-hour night disproportionately removes REM; missing the first 90 minutes (going to bed late then waking on schedule) disproportionately removes SWS. Both halves matter; both halves do different work.

Sleep hygiene practices, ranked by leverage

High leverage

  • Consistent schedule — same bedtime and wake time within ~30 min, including weekends. Circadian regulation is more sensitive to consistency than to the specific clock time. The single biggest behavioral lever.
  • Light exposure timing — bright (ideally outdoor) light in the morning anchors the circadian phase. Dim, warm light in the evening allows endogenous melatonin to rise. Reverse this and the circadian system fights you all night
  • Block evening blue light — blue-rich light (screens, LED room lighting) suppresses melatonin onset. Effect size varies between individuals; the conservative protocol is screens off ~1h before bed, or use night-shift / blue-light filters and reduced screen brightness
  • Cool bedroom — core body temperature must drop to initiate sleep. Recommended bedroom temperature is roughly 15–19°C (60–67°F); warmer rooms fragment sleep even when subjectively unnoticed
  • Caffeine cutoff — caffeine half-life is ~5–6h with substantial individual variance (see caffeine-and-performance for CYP1A2 genetic effects). The conservative cutoff is 8–10h before bed; for slow metabolizers, earlier
  • Limit alcohol near bedtime — alcohol shortens sleep onset but suppresses REM and fragments the second half of the night. The “I sleep better with a drink” intuition is reliably wrong on objective measurements

Medium leverage

  • Dark room — even small light leaks (street light, LED indicators) measurably affect melatonin. Blackout curtains, eye mask, or covering indicator LEDs
  • Bed for sleep and sex only — strengthens the conditioned association between bed and sleep onset. Working from bed weakens this; chronic insomniacs especially benefit
  • Wind-down routine — a 30–60 min ramp of low-arousal activity (reading, light stretching, meditation or 4-7-8 breathing) signals the parasympathetic shift
  • Don’t lie awake — if not asleep within ~20 minutes, get up and do something boring in dim light until drowsy. Lying awake trying to sleep conditions the bed as a place of rumination
  • Time meals — large meals close to bedtime fragment sleep; for cyclists, fueling around evening rides should leave a buffer before sleep

Lower leverage but worth getting right

  • Exercise timing — exercise improves sleep on net, but very intense work in the 2–3h before bed elevates core temperature and sympathetic tone in some people. For most, morning or afternoon training is preferable
  • Bedroom environment — quiet (or consistent white noise), comfortable mattress/pillow that doesn’t cause back/neck pain, no work-related cues visible
  • Hydration balance — hydrated enough that thirst doesn’t wake you, not so much that bladder pressure does

Low leverage / overrated

  • Specific “sleep stack” supplements — most over-the-counter sleep supplements have weak evidence. See longevity-supplements-2026 for melatonin caution: most consumer doses are 5–10x what the body produces, can shift circadian phase in unwanted directions, and don’t help sleep quality much beyond a modest reduction in sleep onset latency for jet-lag-style situations. Magnesium glycinate has slightly better support but the effect is small
  • Sleep tracker obsession — wearables estimate sleep stages by heart rate and movement and are roughly correct in aggregate but unreliable per-night. Useful for trends, not for diagnosing a specific bad night. A poor “sleep score” is itself known to worsen subjective sleep quality the next night (“orthosomnia”)

When good hygiene isn’t enough: CBT-I

For chronic insomnia (sleep difficulties ≥3 nights/week for ≥3 months despite reasonable hygiene), the first-line evidence-based treatment per the American Academy of Sleep Medicine is Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I), not sleep medications.

CBT-I works on the conditioned arousal and maladaptive cognitions that maintain insomnia after the original trigger has passed. Core components:

  • Stimulus control — reassociate the bed with sleep (the “bed for sleep only” rule, formalized)
  • Sleep restriction — temporarily reduce time in bed to compress sleep, then expand once efficiency rises
  • Cognitive restructuring — challenge catastrophic thinking (“if I don’t sleep tonight tomorrow is ruined”)
  • Sleep hygiene education — the practices above, but framed as adjunctive rather than primary
  • Relaxation training — progressive muscle relaxation, paced breathing

Effect sizes for CBT-I are typically equal to or better than sleep medications, with persistent benefit after treatment ends — unlike medications. CBT-I is increasingly available via app-delivered programs (digital therapeutics) where in-person clinicians aren’t accessible.

Counterargument

Some of the strict “rules” in popular sleep-hygiene advice (no screens after sunset, exact 7–9h dose, identical wake time on weekends) are stronger than the evidence supports for any individual. Genuine short-sleepers exist; some people sleep fine with screen use; weekend variance up to ~1h doesn’t measurably wreck most circadian rhythms.

The more defensible framing: identify which levers are actually moving the needle for you. Track for 2–3 weeks (subjective alertness, ride wattage, mood baseline) while modifying one variable at a time. The high-leverage list above is where to start, not where to stop.

What is not up for debate: chronic insufficient sleep (well below your individual norm) compounds badly, and there are no widely-replicated tricks that fully substitute for sleep itself.
