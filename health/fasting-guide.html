Fasting Guide — Durations, Benefits, and Protocols

What it is

Fasting is the deliberate, time-bounded cessation of caloric intake. Unlike continuous caloric restriction, it manipulates the timing of nutrient availability, driving the body to switch from glucose to fat/ketone metabolism. That switch triggers a cascade of evolutionarily conserved repair pathways that modern ad libitum eating patterns suppress: autophagy, stem cell renewal, anti-inflammatory signaling, and endocrine recalibration.

Why it matters

Fasting is one of the few interventions that simultaneously improves insulin sensitivity, reduces systemic inflammation, triggers cellular repair, and requires no equipment, prescription, or cost. Benefits scale with duration but so do risks — different fast lengths unlock different biology. The practical question isn’t “should I fast?” but “which fast serves which goal?”

For a cyclist balancing training-load-tss-tracking with metabolic health, fasting can be a potent tool if timed around training load. It is not universally appropriate and interacts with caffeine-and-performance, hydration, and sleep.

The Fasting Timeline — What Happens Hour by Hour

The timeline below is approximate. Individual variation is large (3–7h shifts are common). Metabolic flexibility, prior diet, activity level, and body composition all affect the curve.

0–4 hours (Fed / postprandial)

Insulin is elevated, digesting the last meal. Glucose is the primary fuel. mTOR is active — anabolic, growth-oriented signaling dominates. No fasting benefits yet.

4–8 hours (Postabsorptive)

Blood glucose normalizes. Insulin drops toward baseline. Liver begins breaking down stored glycogen to maintain blood sugar. This is “between meals” for most people — no particular benefit, but the body is no longer in digestion mode.

8–12 hours (Early fasting / glycogen depletion)

Hepatic glycogen stores draw down. Glucagon rises; insulin falls further. Lipolysis begins — free fatty acids start trickling into circulation. Fasting insulin measurements taken here are already meaningfully lower than postprandial values. Most overnight fasters never go past this window.

12–16 hours (Metabolic switching begins)

Glycogen approaches depletion. The liver starts converting fatty acids to ketone bodies (primarily beta-hydroxybutyrate, BHB). BHB typically rises above 0.3–0.6 mmol/L. AMPK — the energy-sensor kinase — activates, beginning to inhibit mTOR and initiate early autophagy. Human studies show autophagy markers (ATG5, ULK1) start trending up in this window. This is the minimum effective dose for most metabolic benefits.

16–24 hours (Deepening ketosis, autophagy ramps)

BHB climbs to 0.5–1.0 mmol/L — nutritional ketosis. Fat oxidation is now the dominant fuel pathway. Growth hormone begins rising (it roughly doubles in this range). Noradrenaline rises to sustain alertness and energy output. Autophagy intensifies — damaged mitochondria, misfolded proteins, and senescent components start being cleared. Arachidonic acid rises, directly suppressing the NLRP3 inflammasome and dampening innate inflammation.

24–36 hours (Autophagy peaks, ketones dominate)

BHB reaches 1–2 mmol/L. Autophagic flux markers (LC3 up, p62 down) reach a meaningful peak in animal and limited human data. Gluconeogenesis plateaus — the liver makes just enough glucose for obligate glucose-users (brain, RBCs). IGF-1 starts dropping. Insulin is at its nadir. Hunger is typically lower here than at 20 hours — “hunger waves” come and go rather than building linearly.

36–48 hours (HGH spike, deep autophagy)

Human growth hormone secretion increases up to ~400% over baseline, with more frequent pulses across the day. HGH preserves lean mass even during caloric void, mobilizes adipose tissue, and supports muscle repair. Autophagy remains elevated. Ketones climb toward 2–3 mmol/L. Cortisol patterns shift — the cortisol awakening response is amplified, supporting metabolic flexibility without triggering muscle breakdown. This is where “you feel weirdly good” for many people — the ketone-adapted clarity phase.

48–72 hours (Stem cell priming, immune remodeling)

IGF-1 drops to its lowest sustainable levels. PKA (protein kinase A) signaling declines — a key regulator of stem cell dormancy. Hematopoietic stem cells begin shifting from dormant to regenerative states. Ketones reach 3–6 mmol/L. AgRP hunger neurons in the hypothalamus actively orchestrate a redistribution of circulating monocytes, reducing pro-inflammatory populations. The immune system is now in an active remodeling phase, not just a quiescent one.

72+ hours (Immune regeneration, deep therapeutic window)

Longo’s work (USC, 2014 and onward) showed 72h+ fasts trigger near-complete immune system rejuvenation via hematopoietic stem cell-driven renewal. BHB can reach 6–8 mmol/L. Arachidonic acid-mediated NLRP3 suppression is deeply established. Thyroid T3 drops 20–30% — a hypometabolic, longevity-mimetic state. This is the clinical-research zone: strong benefits for autoimmune remission, chemo-patient protection, and metabolomic age reversal. Also the zone where medical supervision starts to matter.

5–10 days (Clinical / supervised prolonged fasting)

Maximum depth of autophagy, stem cell activation, and immune reset. Used in Buchinger-Wilhelmi clinics and research settings for rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and metabolic syndrome. Muscle strength is preserved (lean tissue loss is ~60% water, ~14% glycogen+water, minimal contractile protein). VO2max drops by ~13% after 7 days. Electrolyte derangement (especially sodium/chloride) becomes the primary risk. Refeeding must be staged carefully to avoid refeeding syndrome.

Fasting Protocols

12:12 — Circadian baseline

12 hours eating, 12 hours fasting (e.g., 7pm–7am). This is essentially a disciplined “don’t eat at night.” Minimal difficulty. Good entry point for women, older adults, or anyone with a history of disordered eating. Locks meals to the light cycle and improves sleep quality more than it drives fat loss.

14:10 — Gentle TRE

14 hours fasting, 10 hour eating window. Easier on women’s hormonal axes than 16:8 and still delivers most cardiometabolic benefits. The “minimum effective dose” for metabolic health in many 2024–2025 TRE trials.

16:8 — The classic (Leangains)

16 hour fast, 8 hour eating window (typical: 12pm–8pm, or 11am–7pm). The most-studied TRE protocol. Highest probability-ranked intervention for reducing IL-6, TNF-α, and leptin in network meta-analyses. The Crohn’s & Colitis Foundation TRE trial achieved 40% reduction in Crohn’s disease activity using this protocol over 12 weeks.

Compliance is higher than calorie counting because it only asks “when,” not “how much.” Good default for most adults.

18:6 — Intermediate

18 hour fast, 6 hour window. Noticeably deeper ketosis than 16:8, with autophagy markers meaningfully higher. Still sustainable daily for metabolically flexible individuals. A reasonable endpoint if 16:8 stops producing results.

20:4 — Warrior Diet territory

20 hour fast, 4 hour window (often afternoon/evening). Harder to hit protein/caloric targets in 4 hours. Works for some; most people struggle to sustain without undereating or bingeing. Best used intermittently (2–3 days/week) rather than daily.

OMAD / 23:1 — One Meal A Day

Single meal, ~1 hour window. The longest daily TRE variant. Delivers weight loss and modest metabolic benefit but carries risks: LDL elevation in some individuals, delayed insulin response the next morning, and the highest risk of drifting into disordered eating patterns. Lean mass preservation is harder than with moderate TRE. Not recommended as a daily long-term protocol — more defensible 1–2x/week.

24-hour fast / Eat-Stop-Eat (Pilon)

Fast from dinner to dinner (or breakfast to breakfast) once or twice a week. Pushes into meaningful autophagy territory without the sustainability challenges of daily 20:4. Reasonable add-on to a baseline 14:10 or 16:8. Pair with an easy training day — fasted Zone 2 is tolerable; intervals are not.

36-hour “Monk Fast”

Skip a full day entirely — e.g., dinner Monday, no food Tuesday, breakfast Wednesday. Hits the HGH surge window. Run once per week or every other week for repeated benefit without cumulative stress. Popular in the Jason Fung protocol for insulin resistance reversal.

5:2 Diet (Mosley)

Five days of normal eating, two non-consecutive days at ~500 kcal (women) or ~600 kcal (men). Ranks highest among IF variants for sustained CRP reduction. Gentler than ADF; easier long-term adherence. Produces deep immunophenotyping shifts — reduces effector memory T cells, increases naive T cell populations.

Alternate-Day Fasting (ADF)

Fast day, feast day, repeat. Two variants: true ADF (zero calories) and “modified” ADF (~25% intake on fast days). Strong weight-loss tool but highest dropout rate among IF protocols. Better for short, defined interventions (8–12 weeks) than as a permanent pattern.

Fasting-Mimicking Diet (Longo / ProLon)

Five consecutive days per month of a calibrated low-calorie, low-protein, high-fat plant-based intake (~1100 kcal day 1, ~800 kcal days 2–5). Designed to keep the body metabolically in a fasting state — inhibited mTOR, suppressed IGF-1, active autophagy — while providing essential micronutrients.

The only consumer protocol clinically shown to trigger measurable autophagy in humans. Three monthly cycles reduce trunk fat, lower blood pressure, and reduce biomarker-estimated biological age by 1+ years. Used clinically in MS trials showing reduced metabolomic age and pathogenic T-cell depletion. Safer than a water fast for the same 5 days; costs ~$250/cycle if using the ProLon kit, or can be DIY’d with strict macro discipline.

Prolonged water-only fast (5–10 days)

The deepest therapeutic window. Used for rheumatoid arthritis (7–10 day protocol followed by Mediterranean/plant-based diet), psoriasis (7-day + plant-based transition per RiseFast protocol), and severe metabolic disease. Requires medical supervision — electrolyte monitoring, blood pressure tracking, and a structured refeeding plan. Not a self-serve intervention.

Breaking the Fast

The refeeding phase is non-trivial and often underestimated. The longer the fast, the higher the stakes.

Short fasts (≤24h): Any reasonable meal will do. Protein + non-starchy vegetables + moderate fat is lowest-risk. Avoid sugar bombs — they spike insulin on a sensitized system and cause energy crashes.

Medium fasts (24–72h): Start small. Bone broth or a few bites of eggs and avocado, wait 30–60 min, then a proper meal. Avoid high fiber and high fat on the first meal. Don’t celebrate-feast — many people eat more the day after a fast than they would normally, negating most of the caloric benefit.

Long fasts (72h+): This is the danger zone for refeeding syndrome — rapid shifts in phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium that can cause cardiac arrhythmias or death in extreme cases.

  • Day 1: bone broth, small amounts every 2–3 hours
  • Day 2: bone broth + steamed non-starchy vegetables
  • Day 3: soft proteins (eggs, fish), small portions
  • Continue electrolytes; plan to refeed for half as many days as you fasted
  • Do not attempt 5+ day fasts with self-directed refeeding if BMI is low or lean mass is already depleted

The regenerative vulnerability caveat: Recent stem cell research (2024–2025) shows that the refeeding phase is a hyper-proliferative state where intestinal stem cells switch to lipid metabolism and divide rapidly. Mutagenic or genotoxic exposures during this window (certain dietary carcinogens, alcohol, processed meats) may be amplified. Keep the first refeeding meals clean and plant-forward — not the time for cheat-day indulgence.

What Breaks a Fast

Strict rule: any measurable insulin response, any significant caloric intake, or any protein/amino acid signal to mTOR breaks the biochemical fast.

Does not break a fast:

  • Water (plain, sparkling, mineral)
  • Black coffee (no milk, no sweetener)
  • Plain tea (green, black, herbal)
  • Zero-calorie electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium without sugars or sweeteners — LMNT, Redmond Re-Lyte, DIY with salt)
  • Small amounts of pink/sea salt

Grey zone:

  • Artificial sweeteners (aspartame, sucralose) — technically zero-cal, but can trigger a modest insulin response in some people and affect gut microbiota
  • Apple cider vinegar — minimal caloric load, probably fine
  • MCT oil / bulletproof coffee — breaks autophagic fasting but maintains ketosis; acceptable for “metabolic” fasts, not “cellular cleanup” fasts

Breaks a fast:

  • Anything with calories above trace amounts
  • BCAA/EAA supplements — directly activate mTOR
  • Protein shakes, collagen
  • Gum with sugar alcohols (minor but real insulin signal)
  • Diet sodas with reformulated sweeteners

Anti-Inflammatory and Immune Mechanisms

Fasting reduces systemic inflammation through five interconnected pathways, not one:

  1. NLRP3 inflammasome suppression via arachidonic acid. Fasting elevates circulating arachidonic acid, which directly inhibits the NLRP3 complex — the primary sensor that matures IL-1β and IL-18. Mechanism parallels NSAIDs.
  2. Autophagy of damaged mitochondria (mitophagy). Clears ROS-leaking mitochondria that would otherwise activate the cGAS-STING and NLRP3 pathways. mTOR is inhibited, AMPK and FOXO are activated.
  3. NRF2-driven antioxidant response (mitohormesis). Transient mild oxidative stress upregulates SOD, GPx, CAT, and glutathione — the body builds more defenses than it consumes.
  4. HPA-axis and AgRP hunger-neuron modulation. Cortisol dampens peripheral inflammation; the hypothalamic hunger signal itself reduces circulating pro-inflammatory monocytes (demonstrated in mice even without actual caloric deficit).
  5. Gut microbiome reorganization. Favors SCFA (butyrate, propionate) producers, which inhibit NF-κB. Tight-junction integrity improves, reducing LPS endotoxemia that drives TLR-mediated systemic inflammation.

The biphasic immune response

Prolonged fasting (≥48h) can transiently increase hsCRP and some cytokines in the first 24–72 hours. This is not a deterioration — it’s an adaptive hormetic signal mobilizing cellular defenses. Long-term, inflammation normalizes well below baseline, especially in individuals starting from pathologically elevated levels. Don’t test CRP on day 2 of a 7-day fast and panic.

Exercise and Fasting

Key rule: fasted low-intensity work is beneficial; fasted high-intensity work is counterproductive.

Fasted Zone 2 / endurance (fine-to-beneficial):

  • 6 weeks of fasted endurance training yields ~21% increase in FATmax vs 6% with fed training
  • Increases β-HAD and citrate synthase activity — foundational for fat oxidation capacity
  • Good for base-building in early cycling-profile season; supports “train low, race high” adaptation

Fasted intervals / threshold / race efforts (avoid):

  • Carbohydrate availability is rate-limiting for high-power output
  • Performance drops meaningfully; session quality suffers
  • Not an appropriate context to “get tough” — the adaptation stimulus is lower, not higher

After a long fast (48h+):

  • VO2max drops measurably (~13% after 7 days of fasting)
  • Do not expect race-quality sessions for 2–3 days post-refeed
  • Plan fasts during rest weeks or after a heavy training block, not during a build phase

Practical cycling integration:

  • 14:10 or 16:8 daily: fine, pair morning caffeine with fasted Z2, break fast post-ride
  • 24–36h occasional fast: schedule on a rest day, not before a hard Zwift session
  • FMD or 5+ day: treat as a recovery/rest block; resume intensity gradually

Women and Fasting

The evidence base for fasting in women is thinner and more contentious than for men. Key nuances:

  • Premenopausal women are generally more hormonally sensitive to energy deficit. Aggressive TRE (20:4, OMAD) can suppress LH pulsatility, affect menstrual regularity, and interact with thyroid function.
  • 72h fasts in follicular phase did not disrupt menstrual cycling in one controlled study — but that’s a narrow, supervised condition.
  • PCOS shows clear benefit from 8h TRE — reduced total testosterone, higher SHBG, improved eumenorrhea.
  • Pragmatic recommendation: start with 12:12 or 14:10, not 16:8. Consider cycling the fast — more aggressive in the follicular phase, gentler in the luteal phase. Back off if menstrual patterns change.
  • Postmenopausal women tolerate standard TRE protocols similarly to men.

Safety and Contraindications

Absolute contraindications:

  • Type 1 diabetes — severe hypoglycemia / DKA risk
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding — fetal/infant nutrient deprivation
  • Active eating disorders or history of anorexia/bulimia
  • BMI under 18.5
  • Children and adolescents under 18

Relative contraindications — medical supervision required:

  • Type 2 diabetes on insulin or sulfonylureas (dose adjustments needed)
  • Chronic kidney disease
  • Gout (uric acid spikes during ketosis can trigger flares)
  • Hypotension or on BP-lowering medication
  • History of gallstones (long fasts can precipitate stones in susceptible individuals)

Muscle preservation — largely a non-issue. DEXA studies on 6–10 day fasts show ~60% of “lean tissue” lost is water, ~14% glycogen + bound water, with structural muscle protein spared. Isometric and isokinetic strength are preserved after 6–10 day water fasts. Plasma 3-methyl-histidine (a muscle breakdown marker) drops sharply by day 5, confirming protein-sparing.

Electrolyte risk is real. Sodium and chloride drop significantly during multi-day fasts. Supplement ~2–3g sodium/day on fasts >24h; add potassium (~1g) and magnesium (~300mg) for fasts >48h.

Controversies Worth Knowing

The AHA 2024 “16:8 = 91% higher CV death” poster. Widely reported; methodologically fatal. Based on two 24-hour dietary recalls from NHANES, projecting 8 years of mortality from a single snapshot of eating times. Reverse causality dominates — people with tiny eating windows in NHANES were often sick (cancer, psychiatric illness) rather than deliberately doing IF. Current consensus: no credible CV mortality signal from properly implemented TRE in healthy populations, but long-term RCTs are still needed.

Stem cell refeeding and cancer risk. The same regenerative signal that heals the intestinal epithelium during refeeding can accelerate oncogenesis if mutagenic exposures coincide. Relevant for anyone timing fasts around chemotherapy or high-genotoxic exposures. Keep refeeding meals clean; this is not hypothetical-only research.

Autophagy thresholds in humans are less certain than the marketing suggests. Most detailed data come from rodent models. Human studies (Bensalem 2025, Jordan 2025) show autophagy gene expression (ATG5, ULK1) rising during 16:8 and 17–19h daily fasts, but direct human autophagic flux measurements remain limited. The “autophagy kicks in at exactly 16 hours” claim is a simplification — it’s a rising curve, not a switch.

Practical Starting Protocol

For a metabolically healthy adult new to fasting:

  1. Week 1–2: 12:12. Establish a clean overnight fast with no bedtime snacks. Sleep improves.
  2. Week 3–4: 14:10. Push breakfast later or dinner earlier. Most people don’t notice hunger once habituated.
  3. Week 5+: 16:8 if desired. Hold here long-term for most metabolic benefit with minimal downside.
  4. Occasional: Once comfortable with 16:8, add a single 24h fast per week or a 36h fast every other week for deeper autophagy.
  5. Advanced: Consider a 5-day FMD (ProLon or DIY) quarterly for the stem cell / autophagy deep-benefit without the risk profile of water-only 5-day fasts.
  6. Don’t: Jump to OMAD, a 72h fast, or a 7-day water fast without building up. Adverse events cluster in unprepared fasters.

Track: morning bodyweight, HRV (morning), sleep quality, subjective energy, and — if available — a quarterly lipid panel + hsCRP + fasting insulin. The biomarkers tell you what the scale doesn’t.
