Geroprotective Foods — Functional Compounds in Whole Foods

What it is

A short list of bioactive compounds has credible mechanistic links to the aging pathways. These compounds are concentrated enough in specific whole foods that dietary intake can plausibly move biomarkers. None of these is a miracle on its own; the evidence is strongest when they’re consumed as part of a broader dietary pattern rather than chased in isolation.

This note covers the food-side of these compounds. Concentrated supplement forms are in longevity-supplements-2026.

Why it matters

Most of the “longevity foods” lists circulating online are either marketing or vague. There is a tighter, mechanism-grounded subset that is worth integrating into actual meal planning. Knowing which compound a food is for makes substitution and prioritization easy: if you don’t like one source of spermidine, swap to another, rather than chasing the food itself.

Ergothioneine (ERGO) — the “longevity vitamin” candidate

A sulfur-containing amino acid antioxidant produced by fungi and some bacteria, transferred up the food chain to plants and animals. Humans have a dedicated transporter (OCTN1/SLC22A4 / ETT) that concentrates ergothioneine in tissues with high oxidative stress: erythrocytes, bone marrow, liver, lens, skin, brain. The presence of a dedicated transporter is the main argument for treating it as conditionally essential — animals don’t usually invest in transporters for compounds they don’t need.

Mechanism: powerful, mitochondria-protective antioxidant; resistant to autoxidation at physiological pH; scavenges hydroxyl and peroxyl radicals; protects mitochondrial DNA.

Exact mg/kg figures vary heavily by species, soil, and cultivation method (cultivation on grape marc and similar substrates can dramatically raise content). (Specific concentration tables in the source material are unverified; the species ranking is broadly correct.)

Practical: 80–100g of mushrooms several times a week is a low-effort way to maintain meaningful ERGO intake. Cooking does not destroy ergothioneine.

Aging signal in humans: lower plasma ergothioneine has been associated with frailty, mild cognitive impairment, and all-cause mortality in older-adult cohorts. Whether raising it via diet or supplementation extends healthspan is the open clinical question.

Spermidine — autophagy inducer

A natural polyamine present in all cells, declining with age. The most-studied dietary autophagy inducer; works in part by inhibiting EP300 (a histone acetyltransferase that normally suppresses autophagy) and by interacting with AMPK and SIRT1 signaling.

Best dietary sources:

  • Wheat germ — by far the densest dietary source. A few tablespoons daily delivers a meaningful dose.
  • Soy products (especially natto, tempeh, and aged soy)
  • Mushrooms, particularly aged or dried
  • Aged cheeses (parmesan, aged cheddar, blue cheeses)
  • Mature legumes — lentils, chickpeas, beans
  • Whole grains and bran
  • Green peas, broccoli, cauliflower
  • Mangoes, grapefruit

Aging signal: in the Bruneck Study (Italian cohort), higher dietary spermidine intake was associated with lower all-cause mortality and lower cardiovascular events. The effect size was meaningful in observational terms but the study is not a randomized trial. (Direction of effect is replicated; magnitude warrants the standard observational caveats.)

Practical: a daily pattern that includes wheat germ on yogurt or oatmeal, plus regular legumes, soy, and aged cheese, hits the same intake range as the high-spermidine quartile in observational cohorts. Direct supplementation (1–2 mg/day) is also available; food intake gets you most of the way there.

Fisetin — the strawberry senolytic

A flavonoid that selectively induces apoptosis in senescent cells in preclinical models. Most of the pre-clinical and early human work is in supplement form (longevity-supplements-2026); the dietary signal is much weaker because intake from food alone is well below the doses that show senolytic effects in trials.

Dietary sources (per ~100g):

  • Strawberries — the densest readily available source
  • Apples (with skin)
  • Persimmons
  • Onions (some varieties)
  • Cucumbers, grapes — minor

Practical: eating strawberries regularly is fine and probably beneficial for the broader polyphenol load. Treating dietary fisetin as a senolytic intervention is overreach — supplement doses in trials are roughly 100–500 mg/day; you cannot eat enough strawberries to reach that.

Sulforaphane — Nrf2 activator from broccoli sprouts

A potent activator of the Nrf2-Keap1 pathway, which in turn upregulates a broad array of endogenous antioxidant and detoxification enzymes (HO-1, NQO1, glutathione synthesis enzymes). Acts more like a “training stimulus” for the cell’s defense systems than a direct antioxidant — the hormetic mechanism.

Source density:

  • Broccoli sprouts (3-day) — ~10–100x the sulforaphane precursor (glucoraphanin) of mature broccoli
  • Mature broccoli, cauliflower, kale, cabbage, Brussels sprouts — moderate
  • Mustard greens, watercress, arugula — lower but contribute

The active compound is generated by myrosinase enzyme activity when the plant tissue is damaged (chewing, chopping, blending). Cooking deactivates myrosinase. Tactics to preserve activity:

  • Eat raw or lightly steamed (not boiled)
  • Chop and let sit 30–40 minutes before cooking — myrosinase acts during the rest, releasing sulforaphane that survives cooking better than the precursor
  • Add a little raw mustard or daikon (myrosinase source) to a cooked cruciferous dish to “rescue” the conversion

Practical: a small amount of broccoli sprouts (30–50g, ~3 tbsp) several times a week is the most concentrated practical food-based source. Mature crucifers daily are a fine substitute.

Urolithin A — postbiotic, mostly via supplement

Urolithin A is not present in food. It’s produced by certain gut bacteria from ellagitannins, the precursor compound found in:

  • Pomegranates and pomegranate juice
  • Walnuts
  • Strawberries, raspberries, blackberries
  • Aged red wine, oak-aged spirits (small)

Whether you produce urolithin A from these depends on your gut microbiome composition. A meaningful fraction of the population does not produce significant urolithin A from ellagitannins — estimates around one-third (unverified, varies by cohort and methodology). For non-producers, dietary intake of pomegranate has weaker effects than direct supplementation (longevity-supplements-2026).

Practical: pomegranate, walnuts, and berries are worth eating regardless (good polyphenol load, fiber, healthy fats in walnuts), but if mitophagy induction via urolithin A is the goal, the dietary route is unreliable across individuals. Supplement is the controlled-dose option.

Other notable food-based actors

  • Extra-virgin olive oil — oleocanthal and other phenolics (anti-inflammatory, mild COX-2 inhibitor, ibuprofen-like signaling). Foundational to Mediterranean effects.
  • Green tea — EGCG; modest mTOR modulation, AMPK activation, polyphenol load. Synergizes with caffeine; see caffeine-and-performance.
  • Coffee — chlorogenic acids, polyphenols; consistent observational signal for lower all-cause and CV mortality at moderate intake. The “coffee is bad” priors from older literature have not held up.
  • Dark chocolate (cocoa flavanols) — endothelial function, blood pressure modulation. The signal is real but the typical dose is small per serving and easy to overshoot in calories.
  • Berries (blueberries, strawberries, raspberries) — anthocyanins; cognitive aging signal, polyphenol delivery.
  • Cruciferous vegetables (beyond broccoli) — cabbage, kale, Brussels sprouts; the Nrf2-active baseline.
  • Allium family (garlic, onions, leeks) — sulfur compounds; CV signal in observational cohorts.
  • Fatty fish — omega-3 (EPA, DHA); anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, cardiovascular.
  • Nuts and seeds — magnesium, polyphenols, unsaturated fats; among the most consistent mortality signals in cohort data (PREDIMED especially).
  • Fermented foods — yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut, miso; microbiome diversity → SCFA production → systemic anti-inflammatory effect.

How to combine without overthinking

A weekly rotation that hits most of the above without effort:

  • Mushrooms (oyster or shiitake) in 2–3 meals/week
  • Wheat germ on yogurt or oatmeal most days
  • Legumes daily (soup, salad, side, hummus)
  • Broccoli or cabbage 3–4 times/week, with at least one raw or lightly cooked
  • Berries most days
  • Pomegranate or walnuts a few times/week
  • Olive oil as the default cooking and dressing fat
  • Fish 2–3 times/week
  • Green tea or coffee daily; not late afternoon

This is also approximately the Mediterranean / Blue-Zone template. The functional-food framing isn’t an alternative — it’s a lens on why the pattern works.

What this is not

  • Not a substitute for the dietary pattern. The base diet matters more than any single food.
  • Not a replacement for TRE and other levers.
  • Not a license to add concentrated supplements on top without thinking about doses; many of the food-form compounds become more risky at supplement-level intakes (e.g., high-dose isolated quercetin, see longevity-supplements-2026).
