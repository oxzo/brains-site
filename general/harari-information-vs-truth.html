Harari: Information vs Truth

What it is

In Nexus (2024), Yuval Noah Harari argues that information and truth are distinct categories that the modern “naive view” routinely conflates. The naive view holds that information → truth → wisdom → power, with more data automatically improving each downstream step. Harari claims this reverses the actual function of information: most information that has ever existed — myths, scripture, brand stories, currency, propaganda, bureaucratic records — was never trying to represent reality. Its defining function is connection (tying minds, institutions, and behaviors into coordinated wholes), not representation (accurately depicting how things are). Truth-tracking is a special case of what information does, not its default.

The often-quoted formulation: “information has no essential link to truth.”

Why it matters

Carrying this distinction into AI use disarms a common implicit assumption — that an LLM, being an information-generation engine, is therefore a truth-generation engine. LLMs are optimized for coherence, fluency, and stylistic fit (the connection-function); world-representation accuracy is something that has to be enforced from outside, by verification. The naive view is precisely the assumption the vault’s bias-aware verification stack exists to counter.

The same frame clarifies which claims in any artifact actually need truth-tests. Many claims in organizational, social, and personal artifacts are non-empirical by design (identity, coordination, narrative); applying truth-tests to them is a category error in the other direction. This is the same cut claim-function-scan makes at the document level.

Details

The two functions of information

FunctionWhat it doesExamplesBound by truth?
RepresentationDepicts reality accuratelyScientific paper, weather forecast, sensor readingYes — falsifiable in principle
ConnectionCoordinates a network of minds and institutionsMoney, nation, scripture, brand, party platform, corporate missionNo — coherence + adoption suffice

Most information operates on the connection axis. The representation axis is a special case whose value happens to depend on empirical accuracy. The naive view conflates the two, assuming all information is representation-information, or that “more representation-information automatically wins.” As a result, it gets the failure modes of large information systems wrong.

Three reality registers

Harari distinguishes three registers (drawing on a long social-science lineage, not original to him; see Sources):

  • Objective — true regardless of belief; physics, biology, the contents of an empty room.
  • Subjective — exists if one mind believes it; pain, preference, mood.
  • Intersubjective — exists because a network of minds believes it; money, nations, corporations, gods, laws, scientific paradigms. Not “false” — real in their effects — but not objective.

Most powerful information operates in the intersubjective register. Treating intersubjective claims as objective (or vice versa) is the central pathology Harari is naming.

Why truth loses a free fight — the cost asymmetry

The mechanism beneath “no essential link.” Truth and fiction don’t compete on equal terms; the asymmetry runs three ways (Harari’s argument as given in interviews around the book’s release; presumably also in the book itself, not checked against the text):

  • Truth is costly — research, evidence, expertise take time and money. Fiction is nearly free to produce.
  • Truth is complicated — reality rarely fits a clean narrative. Fiction can be exactly as simple as its audience wants.
  • Truth is often painful — unflattering to persons and nations. Fiction can be shaped to flatter ingrained beliefs.

So in an unregulated information market the equilibrium favors fiction: “more information” doesn’t drift toward truth, it drifts toward cheap, simple, and flattering. The connection-function wins by default economics, not only by historical accident. Generative AI collapses the production cost of fiction-shaped content still further, widening an asymmetry that was already lopsided.

Self-correcting mechanisms — Harari’s positive thesis

Harari’s prescription follows from the cost asymmetry: since truth loses a free fight, truth-tracking has to be paid for via self-correcting mechanisms (SCMs) — institutionalized error-detection that is empowered to revise the network’s own canon. Peer review, appellate courts, a free press, constitutional amendment. His running contrast: the US Constitution’s Article V acknowledges the document’s human, fallible origin and builds in a revision procedure; scripture that claims infallibility structurally cannot self-correct.

The predictive claim: what distinguishes truth-tracking networks is not how much information they process but how strong and independent their SCMs are. His AI worry, restated in these terms: AI-scale networks may generate and propagate content faster than human-speed SCMs can correct. A further vault inference (ours): because SCMs are pure cost on the order axis, they are the first thing competitive pressure trims.

There is pushback on the AI half: Johnson (2025) argues that AI development already embeds feedback loops (iterative deployment, user feedback, evals) and that Harari’s “alien intelligence” framing underplays them. The vault counter-reading (ours, not Johnson’s): those loops mostly optimize engagement and helpfulness — connection-axis metrics — so whether they count as SCMs in Harari’s sense depends on whether anything in the loop rewards representation-accuracy specifically.

Truth vs order — the standing tension

Every durable information network has to do two things at once: discover truth and create order. These pull against each other:

  • Pure truth-seeking erodes shared fictions, weakening the network’s coordination capacity.
  • Pure order-keeping suppresses inconvenient truths, weakening the network’s grip on reality.

Historical institutions have mostly optimized for order and treated truth as a side-effect when it was cheap. The exceptions — scientific institutions, market-pricing systems, adversarial legal/journalistic systems — are explicit attempts to build truth-seeking infrastructure that doesn’t immediately collapse under order pressure.

Implication for AI (vault reading)

This section is the vault’s bridge, not a direct quote from Harari. LLMs and recommender systems sit squarely on the connection axis. Their training objectives reward engagement, coherence, and stylistic fit; they have no built-in mechanism that rewards representation-accuracy as such. A naive “more LLM throughput → more truth” expectation misreads what the system is optimizing.

This is Harari’s frame applied to the same mechanism that ai-output-bias-fingerprints describes at the artifact level: fluent + coherent + confident is the production-target, not a byproduct of accuracy. The bias-aware verification stack exists because the truth-axis has to be enforced externally — the model doesn’t enforce it.

The per-utterance version: LLM output as Frankfurtian “bullshit”

Academic philosophy reached the same conclusion through a different door. Hicks, Humphries & Slater (2024) argue LLM falsehoods are best understood as bullshit in Harry Frankfurt’s sense — speech produced with indifference to truth — rather than lies (which require intent to deceive) or “hallucinations” (which imply a perceptual malfunction in an otherwise truth-tracking system). They distinguish hard bullshit (active deception about the nature of the enterprise) from soft bullshit (mere unconcern for truth) and argue LLMs meet at least the soft definition. This is the per-utterance restatement of Harari’s network-level claim: the system’s production target is truth-apt-looking text, not truth.

The framing is contested: Gunkel & Coghlan (2025) reply that Frankfurt’s bullshitter has attitudes and motives LLMs lack, so “bullshit” anthropomorphizes in its own way, and that “hallucination”/“confabulation” may remain useful metaphors. Note what the dispute is about — which anthropomorphism misleads least. Neither side claims the system is truth-tracking by design, which is the load-bearing point for verification practice.

The policy operationalization: epistemic security

The truth-vs-order tension is becoming a security field. The epistemic security literature treats reliable information supply chains as infrastructure a democracy needs to deliberate and respond to crises (Elizabeth Seger and colleagues; Demos reports Epistemic Security 2029, 2025, and Epistemic Security for Crisis Resilience, 2026) — i.e., it frames defending self-correcting mechanisms as a national-security problem rather than an academic virtue. Adjacent: Coeckelbergh (2022) argues AI threatens citizens’ epistemic agency — the capacity to control how one forms and revises beliefs — via recommender bubbles and pressure to defer to AI-equipped experts, and that losing epistemic agency erodes political agency itself.

The note’s own frame predicts the failure mode here: securitizing the truth-axis is an order-axis move. Whoever defines what counts as a threat to “epistemic security” holds order-keeping power over the information network, so the field carries its own truth-vs-order tension rather than escaping it.

Is the slogan even right? The veridicality debate

“Information has no essential link to truth” is contested terrain in philosophy of information — a literature Nexus doesn’t appear to engage (unverified negative; based on reviews, not a bibliography check). Luciano Floridi’s veridicality thesis defines semantic information as well-formed, meaningful, and truthful data: on this view false “information” is not information at all but misinformation, and the factivity requirement is doing real work (it dissolves the Bar-Hillel–Carnap paradox, where contradictions would otherwise count as maximally informative). Dretske (1981) held a similarly factive view; Primiero (2009) and others reject the thesis.

Part of the disagreement is terminological — Harari uses “information” in the everyday/engineering sense (whatever flows through networks), Floridi in the factive sense (what you can learn from). But the translation is instructive in both directions: under Floridi’s usage Harari’s slogan is false by definition; under Harari’s usage Floridi’s definition concedes the empirical point — most of what circulates in information networks isn’t (Floridian) information at all. Either way the operational lesson survives intact: truth-boundness is a property to be checked, never assumed.

Limits and criticisms

Harari’s broad-historical-synthesis style draws standard objections that apply here too:

  • He compresses multi-century debates into legible binaries. The truth/order tension is real but more textured in any specific institution than the chapter-length summary admits.
  • The “intersubjective” register is not original to Nexus. Searle’s The Construction of Social Reality (1995) and Berger & Luckmann’s The Social Construction of Reality (1966) are direct upstream sources. Harari popularizes; he does not originate.
  • The book’s prescriptive sections (what AI governance should look like) are weaker than the descriptive frame and have drawn skeptical reviews. The framing-of-the-problem is the more durable contribution.
  • The “alien intelligence” framing of AI draws specific criticism: reviewers argue it commits the dehumanization-of-the-other move Harari himself warns against, and that “aggregate human intelligence” (a system reflecting human training data, steered by human feedback) describes current systems better.

For vault use, the value is the framing — information ≠ truth, function determines verification regime — not the specific historical narratives or policy proposals.
