How Much Information to Give at Once

The sender-side question — how much can I put in this message? — has no absolute answer, and the number people reach for (7±2) is the wrong instrument for it. The binding constraint is not how many items you sent. It is how many of them the receiver has to hold simultaneously to make sense of any one of them, and how many of those they already have a schema for.

This is the human-receiver counterpart to information-density-in-written-text (surface density of the text itself) and cognitive-load-theory-for-ai (load on an AI executor). Same load decomposition, different substrate.

The unit that binds: element interactivity

Cognitive Load Theory’s actual predictor of intrinsic difficulty is element interactivity — whether elements can be understood one at a time or only in relation to each other (Sweller, 2010).

  • Low interactivity: ten independent facts. Each lands or doesn’t, independently. Cheap, and the count barely matters.
  • High interactivity: four facts where each only makes sense given the other three. Expensive, and four may already be too many.

Vocabulary lists are low-interactivity; the grammar that combines them is high. A status update listing five unrelated items is low; a design rationale where the constraint, the tradeoff, and the chosen mechanism only cohere together is high. Count interacting elements, not sentences or bullets.

This is why “make it shorter” so often fails to help. Cutting a high-interactivity explanation in half doesn’t halve the load — it removes the connective material that let the pieces cohere, and the receiver now has to reconstruct it.

What the capacity numbers do and don’t say

  • Miller (1956) — “the magical number seven, plus or minus two” — measured immediate serial recall of unrelated items (digits, letters). It was never a comprehension budget, and Miller’s own paper is substantially about chunking as the escape hatch.
  • Cowan (2001) — revised the figure to ~4±1 once rehearsal and chunking are experimentally controlled.

Neither number tells you how many ideas to put in an email. What transfers is the shape of the constraint, plus the mechanism that relaxes it:

A chunk is expertise-relative. Chase & Simon (1973) found chess experts reconstructed real board positions far better than novices — and that the advantage largely collapsed for randomly arranged pieces. The expert wasn’t holding more; they were holding fewer, larger units, and only where their schemas applied. The same message is literally a different quantity of information to two people.

The often-repeated version of that result — that the expert advantage vanishes on random boards — is overstated. Gobet & Simon (1996) reviewed the random-position control conditions and found stronger players retain a small but reliable superiority even there, just far smaller than on game positions. The correction matters for the argument: schema-leverage explains most of the capacity difference between two receivers, not all of it. Some of the gap is general skill you can’t hand someone in a paragraph.

The practical consequence: the number that predicts overload is novel, interacting elements — not total elements. Anything the receiver already has a chunk for is close to free.

The receiver-dependence problem

Three findings say the right amount cannot be set once and reused:

  • Expertise reversal (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller, 2003): instructional support that measurably helps novices measurably hurts experts. The extra scaffolding becomes material the expert must process and reconcile against a schema they already hold. This isn’t “experts don’t need it” — it’s that the same content actively degrades their performance.
  • The redundancy effect (Chandler & Sweller, 1991): restating content the receiver already has — narrating a self-explanatory diagram, re-explaining a known concept — impairs rather than reinforces. Kalyuga et al. treat expertise reversal as a form of this effect rather than a separate mechanism: the support isn’t neutral-but-wasted, it’s redundant, and redundant material still has to be processed and reconciled.
  • The curse of knowledge (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, 1989): once you know something, you cannot accurately model what it’s like not to know it. Your estimate of what the receiver needs is systematically wrong, and the error does not feel like an error.

Together these mean you cannot introspect your way to the right amount. You can only get feedback. See also social-attribution-biases on the illusion of transparency — the same failure applied to whether your intent came across at all.

Two kinds of compaction — only one of them works

This is the actionable core. “Be more compact” names a goal, not a method, and the two available methods have opposite effects.

Cut scopeCut cohesion
What you removeIdeas, sections, tangents, the third exampleConnectives, context, transitions, explicit referents
Effect on word countDownDown
Effect on densityFlat — fewer ideas in fewer wordsUp — same ideas in fewer words
Effect on the receiverEasierHarder, while looking more professional
How it feels to writeLike giving something upLike tightening

The second is the compression trap (lever 6 in information-density-in-written-text): deleting “that,” “because,” “however,” and explicit antecedents raises the inference burden on every clause. The author saves characters; the receiver pays with re-reads. Because it feels like craft, it’s the failure mode that survives editing passes.

The test after a compaction pass: did the idea count drop proportionally to the word count? If you cut 30% of the words and 0% of the ideas, you made it denser, not lighter. Cutting scope is almost always the right move; cutting cohesion almost never is. Fix-mode operations for the second case are in prose-revision-operations.

Channel sets the ceiling

The same payload has very different capacity limits depending on the channel, because channels differ in whether the receiver controls the pace and whether they get a second pass.

ChannelReceiver-paced?Re-readable?Relative capacity
Written docYesYesHighest
Async message / emailYesYesHigh, but competes with a queue
Meeting / callNo — you set the paceNoLow
Interruption / hallwayNoNoLowest

Speech also runs at much lower lexical density than writing (Halliday, 1985) — spoken language spreads the same content across more words, which is a feature, not sloppiness. Reading a written-register paragraph aloud in a meeting is a common way to overload people who would have absorbed it fine as a document.

The move: match channel to interactivity. High-interactivity material (architecture rationale, tradeoffs, anything where the pieces only cohere together) goes in writing, and the meeting is for the decision. Low-interactivity material (status, ack, one fact) is fine spoken.

Working heuristics

These are heuristics for design decisions, not findings — the empirical literature above constrains them but does not produce them.

  • 3–4 genuinely new, interacting ideas per document, meeting, or message. “New” means the receiver has no existing schema for it. Familiar material doesn’t count against the budget.
  • One decision per ask. Bundling two decisions reliably returns one answer and one deferral — and the deferral usually takes the whole message with it. Related: decision-fatigue.
  • Put the ask first. Point-first structure means the receiver knows what to hold the rest against; the mechanics are in readability-mechanics.
  • Available, not absent. Everything past the budget goes into an appendix, a link, or “ask me about X” — not deleted. This is what makes cutting scope safe.
  • Front and back, not middle. Serial-position effects (Murdock, 1962) mean the middle of a long list is where items get dropped. Load-bearing content goes at the top or the end.
  • Ask for the read-back. “What are you taking away from this?” beats any estimate you make of their capacity, because the curse of knowledge makes your estimate unreliable in a direction you can’t feel.

The failure-mode asymmetry

Under-informing and over-informing fail differently, and that asymmetry should drive the default.

  • Too little → they ask. You find out immediately, and the correction is cheap.
  • Too much → they don’t tell you. They silently drop items, act on the part they retained, or defer the whole thing. You find out weeks later, when the dropped item surfaces as a surprise.

Over-informing produces no error signal at the moment it fails. That argues for erring low and keeping the remainder reachable — the combination, not either alone. (This asymmetry is a design argument from the mechanisms above, not a measured result.)

Contested or thin

  • The tappers-and-listeners study — the widely-repeated demonstration that tappers predicted ~50% of listeners would identify a tapped song while ~2.5% actually did — traces to Elizabeth Newton’s 1990 Stanford dissertation, popularized by Heath & Heath’s Made to Stick. It is an unpublished dissertation with no independent replication established here. Treat the figures as illustrative of the curse-of-knowledge mechanism (which has stronger support), not as an established effect size.
  • The 3–4 idea heuristic has no direct empirical warrant. It’s an analogy from Cowan’s chunk figure to a domain (multi-paragraph comprehension) the chunk literature does not cover. It’s a reasonable starting default that should be recalibrated against your own receivers, not a finding.
  • Optimal quantity as a pragmatic norm. Grice’s maxim of quantity (1975) — be as informative as required for the current purposes of the exchange, and no more — states the target correctly but defines it circularly. It’s a good frame for asking the question (“required for what?”), not a procedure for answering it.

What this doesn’t cover

  • Group vs. individual receivers. A room has heterogeneous prior knowledge, so no single density is right for everyone in it. The expertise-reversal literature implies you’re degrading someone either way; how to handle that tradeoff is a separate question.
  • Motivation and trust. Capacity is not the only reason people drop information — disagreement, low stakes, and low trust in the sender all look identical to overload from the outside. Compacting a message that’s being ignored for political reasons won’t help.
  • Persuasion. This note is about transmission and comprehension. How much information to give when the goal is to change someone’s mind is a different literature with partly opposing recommendations.
  • Multi-session accumulation. Everything here is per-exchange. How much someone absorbs over a week of exchanges involves consolidation and spacing — see evidence-based-learning.

Counterargument

The strongest objection is that this note dresses ordinary communication advice in citations. “Lead with the ask, cut tangents, don’t over-explain to experts” is what any decent editor says; element interactivity and Cowan’s chunk figure don’t generate that advice, they decorate it.

The honest position, same as cognitive-load-theory-for-ai takes about itself: the value is diagnostic, not prescriptive. The mechanisms tell you which fix a given failure needs — that a message landing badly is an organization problem, not a length problem; that adding an explanation for a senior reviewer will make things worse rather than neutral; that the tightening pass just made comprehension harder. Someone who already knows which lever to pull gains nothing here.

A second objection cuts against the note’s whole direction: under-informing is a real failure too, and often the more expensive one. Withheld context reads as evasive, forces round-trips that cost far more than the paragraph you saved, and in low-trust settings compaction is indistinguishable from concealment. The instinct to compact is not self-evidently correct — it should be checked against whether the receiver is actually dropping things, or whether you’re optimizing for how the message looks to you.
