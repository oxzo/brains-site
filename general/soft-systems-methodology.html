Soft Systems Methodology

Scope of this note

A third systems-thinking tradition that sits between the engineering lens ([[systems-thinking-engineering]]) and the human / inner-conditions lens ([[systems-thinking]]). Where the engineering lens diagrams the structure of an external system and the human lens cultivates the operator, Soft Systems Methodology (SSM) sits in the middle: it treats the situation as perceived as the primary object of inquiry — including the disagreement between stakeholders about what the system even is.

Developed by Peter Checkland and colleagues at Lancaster University from the late 1960s onward; canonical reference is Checkland’s Systems Thinking, Systems Practice (1981), with practical refinements in Soft Systems Methodology in Action (Checkland & Scholes, 1990) and a compact restatement in Learning for Action (Checkland & Poulter, 2006).

What it is

A structured method for navigating “messes” — complex, ill-defined human situations where stakeholders hold conflicting views about what is wrong, what would count as improvement, and even what the relevant system is. Where hard systems engineering presupposes a known goal and optimises toward it, SSM treats the goal itself as part of what’s being inquired into.

The methodology is interpretive rather than prescriptive. A practitioner using SSM is not solving the situation; they are facilitating a structured comparison between the situation as it is and purposeful activity systems that different stakeholders can imagine, with the explicit goal of surfacing where those imaginings differ and producing changes that are systemically desirable and culturally feasible — both, not either.

The vocabulary “messes” comes from Russell Ackoff, who argued that managers rarely face isolated problems; they face dynamic, interacting sets of problems where attempting to solve each component independently does not optimise the whole. SSM is one practical response to that diagnosis.

Why it matters

The vault’s existing decision tools — [[wrap-framework]], [[ooda-loop]], [[recognition-primed-decision]] — assume the decision-maker has a roughly stable understanding of the problem. The engineering systems lens assumes the analyst can sketch a structurally honest map of the system. Both fail in the situations SSM was designed for: where ten people in the room would draw ten different maps and disagree about which one is “the” system.

SSM is the right tool when:

  • Stakeholders disagree about what the problem is, not just how to solve it.
  • “The system” is partly a social construction that different actors hold different versions of.
  • A purely structural or quantitative model would either lose what matters or pretend an agreement that does not exist.

It is the wrong tool when the system is genuinely Complicated rather than Complex (in [[cynefin-framework]] terms), when stakeholders agree on goals and differ only on means, or when speed dominates — SSM is deliberate by design and slow under time pressure.

Details

CATWOE — the mnemonic for a root definition

The most portable artefact of SSM. Used to construct a “root definition” of a purposeful activity system — a one-sentence specification of what some stakeholder holds the system to be doing, from their worldview. CATWOE forces the practitioner to name each component explicitly, which is where unstated disagreement surfaces.

LetterElementQuestion it forces
CCustomersWho are the beneficiaries or victims of this transformation?
AActorsWho carries it out?
TTransformationWhat input is converted into what output?
WWeltanschauung (worldview)What worldview makes this transformation meaningful?
OOwnerWho has the power to stop or fundamentally redirect it?
EEnvironmentWhat constraints does the system take as given?

The W is the most consequential and most often skipped. Two stakeholders can agree on C, A, T, O, and E but hold incompatible Ws — and the disagreement that drives the mess will usually live in the W.

Rich pictures and root definitions

Two SSM-specific artefacts worth knowing:

  • Rich pictures — informal, often hand-drawn diagrams capturing the situation including its political, emotional, and cultural texture (who has power, who is frustrated, what the implicit conflicts are). Deliberately not a systems diagram. The point is to capture what a structured model would lose.
  • Root definitions — precise statements of what a chosen system is to do, conventionally expressed in a “do P, by Q, in order to contribute to achieving R” form. Multiple root definitions are usually constructed for the same situation, each from a different worldview, and explicitly compared.

The seven-stage model (and why Checkland later loosened it)

The classical SSM presentation is a seven-stage cycle: (1) enter the problem situation, (2) express it (rich pictures), (3) formulate root definitions, (4) build conceptual models from them, (5) compare models with reality, (6) identify culturally feasible and systemically desirable changes, (7) act.

Checkland later moved toward a more fluid “two-stream” formulation (a logic-driven stream of analysis and a cultural stream of accommodation) in part because practitioners were turning the seven stages into a checklist, which defeated the purpose. The seven-stage version is still useful as a teaching scaffold; the two-stream version is closer to how mature practitioners actually deploy it.

Systemically desirable and culturally feasible

SSM’s distinctive criterion for a “good” change is both systemically desirable (it would actually improve the situation) and culturally feasible (the people in the situation can accept and operate it). Most failed organisational change is one of these without the other: technocratic reorganisations that solve the structural problem but the culture rejects, or culturally palatable reshuffles that change nothing structural. Holding both criteria simultaneously is the methodology’s central discipline.

The three “systems” notes carve the territory cleanly: engineering for the structure, soft systems for the perceived situation and its multiple framings, human-systems for the operator. A thorough analysis of a real organisational mess usually needs all three.

Failure modes

  • Method-as-checklist — running the seven stages mechanically while skipping the disorienting work (especially the W). Produces SSM-shaped output with none of its insight. Checkland’s pivot to the two-stream model was a direct response.
  • CATWOE as a single-author exercise — the value of CATWOE is comparing CATWOEs from different stakeholders. A practitioner filling one in alone is doing structured introspection, not SSM.
  • Rich picture as deliverable — rich pictures are working artefacts, not output documents. Polishing one for a slide deck strips the texture that justified drawing it.
  • Drift to consensus — SSM’s emphasis on “accommodation between worldviews” can collapse into pressure for false agreement. The point is to make disagreement legible and actionable, not to dissolve it.
  • Wrong-domain application — using SSM where the situation is Complicated (knowable structure, expert diagnosis available) wastes time and frustrates the experts. Diagnose with [[cynefin-framework]] first.

Prompt patterns

Situation: [the messy situation, especially one with stakeholder disagreement].

For each of three different stakeholders involved, write a one-sentence
root definition in the form "a system to do P by Q in order to achieve
R", and a CATWOE breakdown (Customers, Actors, Transformation,
Weltanschauung, Owner, Environment). Where do the Ws differ? What does
the disagreement on W make visible that a single shared problem
statement would have hidden?

Rich-picture pass: list the elements of [the situation] that an
organisational chart, a process diagram, or a stocks-and-flows model
would all leave out — power, frustration, history, unspoken conflicts,
who avoids whom, what topics never make it onto the agenda. These are
what a rich picture is for.

Desirable-and-feasible test: for each candidate change to [the
situation], answer two questions separately. (1) Would this actually
improve the situation if implemented? (2) Will the people involved
accept and operate it? A change that scores yes/no or no/yes is the
typical failure mode; only yes/yes is worth pursuing.
