Representing Information: Tables vs Graphs vs Text

What it is

A decision framework for choosing how to present quantitative information: plain text, a table, or one of many chart types. It is grounded in the empirical psychology of how humans decode visual encodings, and its central finding is that the choice is perceptual and task-dependent, not aesthetic. Different representations make different judgments accurate, and the right one depends on what the reader needs to do with the numbers, not on what looks impressive.

Why it matters

A well-chosen representation lets a reader extract the intended message preattentively — before conscious effort. A poorly chosen one forces low-accuracy perceptual judgments (comparing angles, areas, volumes), or worse, actively misleads (truncated axes, dual axes, 3D). The same data can support a correct read or a false one depending only on encoding. Choosing well costs little and changes what the reader takes away.

The decision: text → table → graph

The first question is not “which chart” — it is whether to use a chart at all. Work down this ladder:

  1. One or two numbers, or a single narrative point → plain text or a big number. A sentence (“Revenue grew 12% to €4.2M”) or a large bold figure (a “BAN”, big aggregate number) beats a chart for a single value. A chart for one number wastes the reader’s decoding effort. (Practitioner consensus from dashboard design, not a controlled study — see Contested claims.) A BAN needs context to be useful: a comparison (vs. target, vs. prior period) and a trend cue (a sparkline or directional arrow), so it answers “good or bad, better or worse?” without thought.

  2. Need precise/lookup values, mixed units, or a small set (~≤20 numbers) → table. Tufte: “Tables usually outperform graphics in reporting on small data sets” (the “~20 numbers” threshold is his rule of thumb, cited approximately). Stephen Few’s table criteria — use a table when the reader must look up or compare individual values, when precise values matter, when the data spans more than one unit of measure, or when you must show both summary and detail.

  3. The message is in the shape of the data → graph. Few’s governing question: “Is the message contained in the shape of the values?” Trends, patterns, outliers, relationships across whole sets of values — these are what graphs reveal and tables hide. When you need both lookup and shape, show both (or a table with embedded sparklines).

This is Vessey’s cognitive-fit theory (1991) restated: performance is best when the representation matches the task. Graphs encode spatial information and aid spatial tasks (trends, relationships); tables encode symbolic information and aid symbolic tasks (extracting discrete values). Neither is universally better — which is why decades of “graphs vs tables” experiments gave mixed results until cognitive fit explained them.

Caution on a famous title: Andrew Gelman’s paper “Why Tables Are Really Much Better Than Graphs” is satirical — his actual position is that graphs (esp. small multiples of plots) usually beat tables. Don’t cite the title at face value.

Choosing the chart: by analytical task

Once you’ve decided a graph is right, pick by the relationship in the data, not by chart catalog. Two practitioner frameworks dominate; they agree on the core mappings and differ mainly in granularity.

Andrew Abela’s Chart Chooser sorts into four families:

FamilyYou want to show…Typical charts
Comparisonvalues across items or over timebar/column (across items), line (over time)
Distributionhow values spread / how often they occurhistogram (1 var), scatter (2 var)
Compositionparts of a whole, static or changingstacked bar/area, waterfall, (pie, caveated)
Relationshiphow two/three variables co-varyscatter (2 var), bubble (3 var)

Cross-framework consensus mappings (high confidence):

  • Trend over time → line chart
  • Comparison across categories → bar / column chart
  • Distribution → histogram / box plot / violin
  • Correlation → scatterplot (2 vars) / bubble (3 vars)
  • Part-to-whole → stacked bar / treemap (pie only with caveats below)
  • Ranking → ordered bar / dot plot / slope chart
  • Flow → Sankey / chord diagram
  • Geospatial → choropleth / symbol map

For a more theoretical route, Tamara Munzner’s what–why–how framework decomposes any visualization into what (data type: tables, networks, fields, geometry; attributes nominal/ordinal/quantitative), why (task: analyze / search / query; targets like trends, outliers, correlation), and how (idioms: encode, manipulate, facet, reduce). It doesn’t hand you a chart — it gives you the vocabulary to reason about why a chart works, via marks and channels (below).

Why the rankings exist: graphical perception

The decision rules above are downstream of how accurately humans decode visual encodings. This is the load-bearing empirical foundation.

Cleveland & McGill (1984), “Graphical Perception” ranked elementary perceptual tasks by decoding accuracy (most → least accurate):

  1. Position along a common scale (e.g. dots/bars on a shared axis) — most accurate
  2. Position along non-aligned scales
  3. Length, direction, angle (roughly equal)
  4. Area
  5. Volume, curvature
  6. Shading, color saturation — least accurate

This single ranking explains most chart advice. Bar charts win because they use position + length (top of the list). Pie charts are weak because they force angle and area judgments (lower down). Heer & Bostock (2010) replicated the ranking via crowdsourcing (Mechanical Turk) and confirmed position > length > angle held. Munzner’s “channels” ranking carries the same ordering and adds the split: magnitude channels (for ordered/quantitative data, e.g. position > length > angle > area > luminance) vs identity channels (for categorical data, e.g. spatial region > color hue > motion > shape).

Why area and volume mislead — Stevens’ power law. Perceived magnitude scales as stimulus^n. For length, n ≈ 1.0 (near-accurate); for area, n ≈ 0.7 (compressed; large areas are systematically underestimated). This is the mechanism behind distrust of bubble charts, area encodings, and 3D: the eye reads them nonlinearly. (An often-quoted specific Heer-Bostock area exponent was not corroborated in research and is omitted here; the classic Stevens area ≈ 0.7 is the well-established figure.)

Preattentive processing. Certain visual features are detected in parallel, automatically, in < ~200–250 ms before focused attention — color/hue, size, orientation, shape, motion, and more (catalogued by Healey; grounded in Treisman’s Feature Integration Theory). Good design exploits this: one preattentive cue (a single colored bar among grey ones) directs attention to the message instantly; overusing them (everything colored) destroys the effect.

Bertin’s visual variables (Semiology of Graphics, 1967) is the older taxonomy underneath all of this: position, size, shape, value (lightness), color hue, orientation, texture — each classified by whether it is selective (instantly isolates a group), associative, ordered, or quantitative. Position is strongest on all four; hue is selective and associative but not ordered or quantitative (so don’t encode a quantity as hue alone).

Common pitfalls

  • Pie charts. Rely on angle/area (low-accuracy channels). Few calls the pie the least effective common chart type; readers can’t reliably tell a 13% slice from 14%. Bar-length judgments were reported as ~1.96× more accurate than pie-angle judgments in Cleveland & McGill (figure from secondary summaries; verify against the primary paper). Defensible only for rough part-to-whole with very few categories (~2–3). The common “max 5–6 slices” rule is folk guidance, not a sourced threshold. Tufte: “a table is nearly always better than a dumb pie chart.”
  • Truncated / non-zero baseline. Bar and column charts must start at zero. They encode value by the length of a filled shape, so a cut baseline distorts the length ratio. Line and dot charts may use a non-zero baseline, because they encode by position and slope, not filled length. Pandey et al. (2015) found deceptive techniques (truncated axis, area-as-quantity, etc.) increased misinterpretation by a reported 58.5%–129.5% (figure from secondary summaries; verify against the paper).
  • Dual-axis charts. Two independently-scaled y-axes let an author make any two series appear as correlated as desired, implying false relationships. Prefer two panels or index both series to a common base. (Tyler Vigen’s Spurious Correlations is the canonical cautionary illustration.)
  • 3D charts. Perspective distortion (nearer = bigger), occlusion (front hides back), and inflated area/volume perception. Avoid for data display; major BI tools largely omit 3D charting by design. (One 2024 eye-tracking study found no significant comprehension difference for 3D bars — so harm may be task-dependent, not universal.)
  • Rainbow / jet colormaps. Not perceptually uniform (false bands where data is smooth) and not colorblind-safe. Use perceptually-uniform maps — the viridis family, or cividis (optimized for color-vision deficiency). Note Google’s Turbo fixes some jet artifacts but is still not colorblind-friendly.
  • Lie Factor (Tufte) = (effect size shown) ÷ (effect size in data); should sit near 1.0.
  • Overplotting in dense scatterplots — fix with transparency/alpha, jitter, 2D binning (hexbin), or density contours.

Chartjunk: a genuine debate, not settled dogma

Tufte’s data-ink ratio (maximize ink devoted to data; erase decoration) is the classic minimalist position. But the evidence is not one-sided:

  • Bateman et al. (2010), “Useful Junk?” — heavily embellished charts (Holmes-style) were described no less accurately than plain ones, and showed significantly better long-term recall (2–3 weeks later).
  • Borkin et al. (2013), “What Makes a Visualization Memorable?” — pictograms, multiple colors, and low density boosted memorability; memorable charts were not necessarily less effective.
  • Li & Moacdieh (2014) — embellishment effects are conditional, not uniformly good.

Reasonable synthesis: minimalism for analytical/decision charts where reading accuracy is the goal; judicious embellishment is defensible where the goal is memory or engagement (presentation, journalism). Don’t treat “remove all chartjunk” as an absolute.

Communication context: exploratory vs explanatory

The representation also depends on why you’re showing it. Exploratory analysis (the analyst’s own search) opens many views to find the few worth telling — Knaflic’s “pearls in oysters.” Explanatory communication narrows to a single message for an audience. The shift demands:

  • State the message first. Knaflic’s “Big Idea”: one sentence capturing the core point, written before the chart. Titles should carry the takeaway (“West Coast Sales Drove Q4 Growth”), not a generic label (“Sales by Region”).
  • Declutter. Remove anything that takes space without increasing understanding (Gestalt grouping, white space, restraint with gridlines/decimals).
  • Direct attention with one or two preattentive cues, consistently used. Larger = more important; top-left is read first (Western order).
  • Direct labeling > legends where feasible (less eye travel).

Dashboard design (Stephen Few, Information Dashboard Design): fit a monitoring dashboard on a single screen (no scrolling), avoid gauges/dials/decoration, give every metric context (vs. target, vs. prior period). Few invented the bullet graph as a compact, colorblind-safe replacement for gauges (a measure bar + target marker + qualitative bands) and gave sparklines (Tufte’s word-sized charts) a central role. (Caveat: Few’s single-screen rule is for at-a-glance desktop monitoring; responsive/mobile dashboards stack vertically and scroll — a “match the representation to the medium” exception, not a contradiction.)

Accessibility is not optional. ~8% of men (and ~0.5% of women) have color vision deficiency — red-green most common. Never rely on color alone. Pair it with labels, shape, or pattern. Use a colorblind-safe categorical palette (e.g. Bang Wong’s 8-color Nature Methods palette). Provide alt text and sufficient contrast.

Quick reference

  • 1–2 numbers → text / BAN-with-context
  • Precise values, lookup, mixed units, ≤~20 numbers → table
  • Shape, trend, pattern, relationship → graph
  • Over time → line · across categories → bar · distribution → histogram/box · correlation → scatter · part-to-whole → stacked bar/treemap (rarely pie) · ranking → ordered bar/dot · flow → Sankey · geography → choropleth
  • Always start bars at zero, avoid dual axes and 3D, use colorblind-safe perceptually-uniform color, lead with the message in the title.

Contested / lower-confidence claims

  • “BAN beats a chart for one number” — strong practitioner/dashboard consensus and sound preattentive rationale, but no controlled study located. Lower evidentiary tier than Few/Tufte/Vessey.
  • The 1.96× (pie) and 58.5–129.5% (Pandey) figures — from reputable secondary summaries; primary PDFs were not re-extracted. Treat as approximate pending source check.
  • Tufte’s “~20 numbers” table threshold — a frequently-quoted rule of thumb, not verified against the original page.
  • Pie-chart real-world harm — a 2024 study questions whether the lab-measured pie disadvantage translates to high-level decisions; Few himself critiqued the Cleveland-McGill design. The perceptual ranking is robust; its practical decision impact is debated.
  • Max pie-slice count — folk guidance, not a sourced empirical threshold.
