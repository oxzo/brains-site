Representing Information: Choosing the Form

Hub note. How to choose the external form in which information is presented — prose, list, table, chart, diagram, map, pictogram, equation, picture — and why the choice is a cognitive decision, not a stylistic one. The deep dives live in the spokes:

  • representing-information-tables-vs-graphs — the quantitative-data branch (text vs table vs which chart, graphical perception)
  • diagrams-for-structure-and-process — the relational/procedural branch (network, tree, concept map, flowchart, state machine, timeline…)

The one idea everything rests on

A representation is not good or bad in itself — only relative to a task and a reader. The same information, re-expressed in a different external form, can be trivial or near-impossible to reason with. Part of this is a framing — “good for what?” is built into the claim. But it makes a falsifiable prediction: hold the information and the task fixed, swap the external form, and reader performance should differ systematically and predictably. It does (the representational-effect experiments below). What would falsify it: a form that’s reliably best across unrelated tasks regardless of reader expertise — no such universal form has been found. Note the supporting work is two interlocking lineages, not five independent confirmations — an external-cognition strand (Larkin & Simon → Zhang & Norman → Scaife & Rogers, who cite each other) and a multimedia-learning strand (Paivio → Mayer):

  • Larkin & Simon (1987), “Why a Diagram is (Sometimes) Worth Ten Thousand Words.” Two representations are informationally equivalent if each can be derived from the other; computationally equivalent if, additionally, any inference that’s easy/fast in one is equally easy/fast in the other. Diagrams and the text they encode are usually informationally but not computationally equivalent — and that gap is the entire point. A diagram wins by (a) locality — grouping jointly-used information by position in a plane, so attending to one location surfaces all of it and search collapses; (b) minimal labeling — position substitutes for symbolic labels; (c) perceptual inference — the visual system makes many inferences automatically and cheaply that text would force you to deduce.
  • The “sometimes” caveat is load-bearing. A diagram helps only if the reader already has the perceptual recognition procedures to exploit it, the task is genuinely spatial/relational, and the text being compared is a poorly-organized list. The first condition is expertise-dependent — the chess master sees an “open file” the novice can’t. A well-organized sentential form narrows the gap. The title’s “ten thousand words” is rhetorical, not a measured ratio — the diagram advantage is real but problem-specific. (A separate later study, Cheng et al. 2004, measured specific geometry/physics diagrams as ~6× easier than equivalent sentences — also problem-specific, and not a Larkin & Simon figure, as it’s sometimes misquoted.)
  • Zhang & Norman (1994), the representational effect. Isomorphic representations of the same formal structure produce markedly different difficulty (Arabic vs. Roman numerals for multiplication; Tower-of-Hanoi isomorphs). External representations aren’t memory aids bolted onto thinking — they are part of the task’s structure and change what cognition the task requires.
  • Scaife & Rogers (1996), external cognition. Graphics work via computational offloading (less mental effort), re-representation (different external forms of the same structure differ in difficulty), and graphical constraining (the form constrains which inferences are even possible).
  • Dual coding (Paivio) / Mayer’s multimedia learning — but conditionally. Words + relevant pictures beat words alone only when integrated. Mayer’s own boundary effects cut the other way: the coherence effect (decoration hurts), the redundancy effect (on-screen text duplicating narration impairs learning), and split-attention (separated words and pictures hurt). Be skeptical of the pop-culture “always add a picture / mind-map everything” advice — the verified literature is all boundary conditions.

A useful semiotic backdrop (Peirce, interpretive gloss): a realistic picture signifies by resemblance (iconic, low abstraction); a diagram resembles the structure not the appearance (iconic-but-schematic); notation/text signifies by convention (symbolic). Moving from picture → diagram → notation trades concreteness for precision and generality.

The decision procedure

  1. What is the structure of the information? Values? A ranking? A relationship between two variables? A network of entities? A process with branches? A position in space or time? A category breakdown? A single fact?
  2. What must the reader DO with it? Look up an exact value, compare, spot a trend, trace a path, follow a procedure, grasp a proportion, verify, remember?
  3. Pick the family that makes that inference perceptual rather than effortful (table below).
  4. Pick the specific form within the family (the spokes handle this).
  5. Match form to medium and audience — precision vs engagement, expert vs lay, print vs interactive vs mobile.

The families of representation

These are organized by the dominant structure each one fits — not a clean partition. They overlap and nest: maps are charts with a geographic coordinate system; pictograms and icon arrays are a chart subtype; infographics are composites of several families plus prose (a layout strategy, not a peer category). Treat the table as a routing heuristic, not a taxonomy — when a case sits on a boundary, that usually means two forms both work and the choice is about emphasis.

Text: prose vs lists

The first fork is often within text, not chart-vs-text. Prose carries reasoning whose meaning is in the relationships — causal, qualified, nuanced argument; plain-language guidance recommends paragraphs for “complex or highly technical” interconnected information. Lists suit discrete parallel items (bullets) or sequences (numbered); keep items short and parallel.

Tufte’s The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint argues bullet hierarchies fragment narrative and obscure causal/statistical logic (the Columbia accident slides are his centerpiece). Treat this as a strong design heuristic, not a proven law — it’s argument and illustration, not controlled experiment, and short grouped parallel bullets can genuinely aid scanning. The defensible rule: don’t use bullets to carry an argument whose logic lives in the links between points.

Maps, pictograms, equations, pictures

  • Maps — use only when geography is load-bearing, not just because the data has place names. Choropleth (shaded regions) requires normalized data (rates, densities) — raw counts mislead because large/populous units dominate; use proportional-symbol maps for raw counts.
  • Pictograms / ISOTYPE (Neurath & Arntz) — the rule is more icons of equal size for more quantity, never a bigger icon (size variation defeats accurate comparison). Icon arrays can improve comprehension of probability and proportion, especially for low-numeracy audiences (they make part-to-whole visible and reduce denominator neglect; pair with frequency framing like “35 in 100”). But they are not uniquely better than other good graphics — a controlled trial found no significant comprehension difference versus a bar chart (and readers preferred the bar), and systematic reviews find neither clearly wins.
  • Equations / pseudocode — beat prose for precise formal relationships and algorithms, where English is ambiguous or un-analyzable. Pseudocode trades English’s readability for code’s precision; notation is compact and language-independent.
  • Pictures / infographics — photos are maximally iconic (recognize a real referent). Infographics are composite narratives that can raise engagement and short-term recall, but the literature is mixed and design-dependent; the engagement-vs-precision tradeoff is real and benefits collapse when statistics are decontextualized. When combining text and image, apply Mayer’s signaling (cue the structure) and spatial contiguity (put words near the picture they describe).

Evaluating any notation: Cognitive Dimensions

The Cognitive Dimensions of Notations framework (Green 1989; with Petre 1996) gives a vocabulary for critiquing any representation, not a metric. Useful dimensions: viscosity (resistance to small changes), hidden dependencies (relationships you can’t see — change one thing, something unseen breaks), hard mental operations, secondary notation (meaning carried by layout/color outside the formal syntax), premature commitment, abstraction gradient, closeness of mapping (does the notation match the problem domain?), visibility & juxtaposability. When a representation feels bad, naming the dimension usually locates why.

Contested / lower-confidence claims

  • Dual coding is widely overstated in study-tips culture; the real evidence is conditional (Mayer’s coherence/redundancy effects show pictures and duplicated text often hurt). Don’t assert “pictures beat words.”
  • Bullets-damage-reasoning (Tufte) is a heuristic, not experimentally established.
  • Infographics improve comprehension only conditionally on good design; much supporting work is low-rigor.
  • Icon arrays are effective but not uniquely better than other good graphics.
  • The Larkin & Simon failure-conditions and the Scaife & Rogers three-property triad are corroborated via expert summaries; treat exact phrasings as paraphrase.
