Logical Fallacies

What it is

Logical fallacies are structural errors in the construction of an argument — failures in how premises connect to conclusions, independent of whether the premises themselves are perceived correctly. They are mistakes in deduction or induction; the form of the argument is broken.

This is distinct from cognitive-biases, which are perceptual and interpretive distortions — failures in how the mind takes in and filters information. The distinction is operationally important:

Logical fallacyCognitive bias
LocusThe argumentThe perceiver
VisibilityVisible in the argument’s structureHidden inside the perceiver’s processing
CorrectionRestructure the argument; demand valid premisesRestructure the environment; install reflective protocols
Example”P → Q, ¬P, therefore ¬Q” (denying the antecedent)“I remembered three plane crashes recently, so flying must be dangerous” (availability heuristic)

A statement can contain both — a fallacious argument built on biased perceptions — but they need to be diagnosed and addressed separately.

Why it matters

For verification work generally — reviewing model output, colleagues’ PR descriptions, project pitches, research papers — logical fallacies are the structural axis of bullshit detection. Each artifact contains an argument whose validity can be assessed structurally before evaluating whether the premises are even true. If the structure is broken, the conclusion is unsupported regardless of premise quality.

In adversarial reading (which much of verification is), fallacy detection is faster and lower-context than premise-checking. You can spot a non sequitur in unfamiliar territory; checking premises requires domain knowledge.

Catalogue (the common ones)

Failures of relevance

Ad hominem — attacking the source of an argument rather than the substance. “X is wrong because they previously believed Y.” Substance and source are independent; the argument needs to be addressed on its own terms. Note: pointing to a track record of unreliability is not automatically ad hominem if the track record is the actual point (e.g., “this source has been wrong about 80% of similar claims”); the line is whether the attack engages the argument or evades it.

Appeal to authority (improper) — “X is true because [authority] said so.” Authority is evidence, sometimes strong evidence, but it is not deductive proof. Improper specifically means the cited authority is outside their domain, or treating expert opinion as ending the inquiry rather than informing it.

Appeal to popularity (bandwagon) — “many people believe X, therefore X.” Truth doesn’t track popularity. The bandwagon fallacy in argument is structurally distinct from the bandwagon effect bias in personal belief formation, though they share a name.

Red herring — introducing irrelevant material to distract from the argument under discussion.

Failures of induction

Hasty generalization — drawing a sweeping conclusion from a sample too small or unrepresentative to support it. “This new framework crashed once; it’s unreliable.” Frequently overlaps with the availability heuristic but is structurally a fallacy independent of which examples come to mind.

Anecdotal evidence — citing a single vivid case in place of statistical evidence. The specific case may be real; it just doesn’t establish the general claim.

Cherry-picking — selectively presenting evidence that supports the conclusion while omitting evidence against. The structural counterpart to confirmation bias.

Causation failures

False cause (post hoc ergo propter hoc) — “X happened, then Y happened, therefore X caused Y.” Temporal sequence is not causation. Especially common in retrospective postmortems and in narrative explanations of market or system behavior.

Single cause — assuming one factor explains an outcome that has multiple contributing causes. Real systems are usually multicausal; arguments that allocate 100% of the explanation to one variable are typically wrong about the structure of the world.

Correlation implies causation — see false cause; the version that uses statistical language. A correlation is consistent with causation, reverse causation, common-cause confounding, or coincidence.

Structural failures

Circular reasoning (begging the question) — the conclusion is embedded in the premises. “X is true because X.” The argument adds no information.

Non sequitur — the conclusion does not follow from the premises. The catch-all for “the inferential leap is broken.” Often visible as an unstated middle step.

Affirming the consequent — “If P then Q. Q. Therefore P.” This is invalid; Q can have other causes. Frequently disguised in real arguments by complex P and Q expressions.

Denying the antecedent — “If P then Q. Not P. Therefore not Q.” Also invalid; Q can hold even when P doesn’t.

Fallacy of composition — assuming what is true of the parts is true of the whole. “Each member of the team is excellent; therefore the team is excellent.” (Coordination, communication, and complementarity are properties of the whole, not the parts.)

Fallacy of division — the inverse: assuming what is true of the whole is true of each part. “This system is reliable; therefore each component is reliable.” (A system can be reliable through redundancy of unreliable components.)

Probabilistic failures

Gambler’s fallacy — believing independent random events are influenced by previous outcomes. Independent events are independent; “we’re due for a win” is not a real claim. See probability-judgment-biases for the broader probabilistic-reasoning failure cluster.

Base rate neglect — ignoring the prior probability and reasoning only from specific evidence. The structural fallacy version of the base rate fallacy.

Framing failures

False dilemma (black-and-white) — presenting only two options when more exist. The structural counterpart to narrow framing in wrap-framework. “Either we ship now or the project fails” — usually false; intermediate options exist.

Strawman — misrepresenting an opponent’s argument in a weakened form, then attacking the weakened form. Often unintentional in good-faith disagreement; deliberate in adversarial rhetoric.

Equivocation — using a term in two different senses within the same argument, where the inference depends on conflating them. “All laws have exceptions; gravity is a law; therefore gravity has exceptions” — “law” means different things on each line.

Loaded question — a question with an embedded claim that the answer assumes. “When did you stop violating the spec?” assumes prior violation.

Use in verification work

For AI verification specifically:

  • Model outputs that present an argument can be assessed for logical structure independently of whether the premises are true. A model that affirms the consequent in its reasoning is doing flawed inference even on correct facts.
  • The “explanation” produced by a model after a tool call is an argument; check whether the conclusion (e.g., “the test passes”) follows from the actual evidence (the tool output). Frequently the inference is non sequitur and the model has produced a plausible-sounding bridge.
  • The ai-output-bias-fingerprints checklist targets cognitive-bias fingerprints in model output; logical-fallacy detection is the structural counterpart that catches a different failure mode.
  • Run-time safety: a model generating multi-step plans can produce a locally valid step that does not connect to the goal. Logical-structure auditing catches plans that look reasonable per-step but are incoherent overall.

For human-authored content (PR descriptions, project pitches, postmortems), the same applies: the structure of the argument is reviewable in advance of premise verification, and is a faster filter.

Sources

The fallacy catalogue is largely traditional, dating to Aristotle’s Sophistical Refutations and refined through 20th-century informal-logic literature.

  • Walton, D. (2008) — Informal Logic: A Pragmatic Approach (modern textbook treatment)
  • Tindale, C. W. (2007) — Fallacies and Argument Appraisal
  • Hamblin, C. L. (1970) — Fallacies (the modern critical re-examination)

Note: the boundary between “fallacy” and “weak argument” is not crisp. Modern informal-logic literature increasingly treats fallacies as patterns of argument that fail in specific contexts rather than as universally invalid forms. An appeal to authority, for example, is reasonable evidence in many domains and fallacious only in specific misuses. Treat the catalogue as named patterns to check for, not as a list of forbidden moves.
