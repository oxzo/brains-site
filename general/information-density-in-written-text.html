Information Density in Written Text

What it is

Surprisal is the negative log probability of a token given its preceding context: St​=−log2​P(wt​∣w1​…wt−1​). One bit of surprisal = halving the predicted probability. Information density is the surprisal per unit of text — per character, syllable, word, or sub-word token, depending on the operationalization.

Surprisal is information-theoretic, not semantic. A highly predictable but factually wrong word is low surprisal. A perfectly true but unexpected word is high surprisal. The empirical link the field has established is between surprisal and cognitive processing cost (reading times, fixation durations, N400 amplitudes), not between surprisal and meaning.

The granularity matters: per-character density captures orthographic redundancy, per-syllable captures phonological packaging, per-word captures lexical/morphological richness, per-token captures whatever the tokenizer was optimized for (usually English-heavy). Cross-language comparisons of “density” using LLM tokenizers are mostly tokenizer artifacts, not linguistic facts.

Why it matters

Most writing instruction is heuristic — “shorter is better,” “vary your sentence length,” “show, don’t tell” — and the heuristics conflict. Surprisal-based psycholinguistics gives a tunable account of what the reader’s parser is actually doing, which lets you reason about specific writing decisions instead of vibing.

Three popular claims the empirical literature overturns or qualifies:

  1. “Shorter is better” — only when density drops proportionally. Cutting connectives without cutting ideas spikes local surprisal and damages comprehension.
  2. “Hemingway is easy to read” — Hemingway scores easy on Flesch but exhibits very high propositional density (CPIDR scores near 0.99 for The Old Man and the Sea). Easy word recognition; very demanding model building.
  3. “High density = bad” — high propositional density impairs verbatim memory but enhances deep textbase comprehension (the desirable-difficulty effect). Density is a tunable, not a defect.

For prompt design specifically: terseness is a common cause of surprisal spikes. The instinct to delete “fluff” routinely deletes the very predictive cues the receiver — model or human — needs to anticipate the next clause cheaply.

The six actionable levers

Each lever names a pattern that can be detected (in a draft) and modulated (in a rewrite). These are the operational targets for any density-analysis skill.

1. Surprisal-spike detection

Pattern: a single word forces a much higher cognitive jump than its neighbors. Common causes: deleted complementizer (“that”), deleted causal/contrastive connective (“because”, “however”, “therefore”), deleted determiner before a long modifier, asyndetic clause boundaries.

Evidence: Smith & Levy (2013) showed reading time scales linearly with surprisal across six orders of probability magnitude. Removing a function word that signals an upcoming clause structure forces the reader to re-parse on the embedded subject — the cost lands on the reader, not the author who saved a word.

Detection in prose: scan for places where two content-heavy clauses sit adjacent without a function-word bridge. A model can flag candidates by asking “could a reader misparse this clause boundary on first pass?“

2. Sentence-length variation as pacing

Pattern: monotonous sentence lengths in either direction. All-short produces disjointed surface that forces inference generation between sentences. All-long sustains working-memory load until structural forgetting kicks in.

Evidence: Dependency Locality Theory (Gibson) and Lossy-Context Surprisal (Futrell, Gibson, Levy 2021) both predict that sustained long-distance dependencies degrade processing. Short sentences after long ones act as a working-memory flush. Variation is the cognitive pacing mechanism the “vary your sentence length” heuristic gestures at.

Detection: compute sentence-length variance across a paragraph; flag passages where consecutive sentences cluster within ±20% of each other for more than 4–5 sentences.

3. Propositional density vs. word count

Pattern: a passage is short by word count but packs many idea-units (predicates plus their arguments) per word. Reads as terse but is high-load.

Evidence: Kintsch & Keenan (1973) established propositional density. Subsequent work (and recent CPIDR-style measurements) shows divergent effects: high density impairs verbatim memory and slows reading, but enhances memory for the textbase (the logical proposition graph). It’s neither a virtue nor a defect — it’s a tunable.

Detection: count predicate-argument units per 100 words. The stylometric move that distinguishes Hemingway from “easy reading” with the same word count is high propositional density beneath simple surface syntax.

Tuning rule: match density to purpose. Reference material that needs verbatim recall → lower density. Material that wants the reader to think hard and remember the structure → higher density is fine, as long as cohesion (lever 1) is preserved.

4. Long-distance dependency cost

Pattern: subject far from verb; nested relative clauses; center-embeddings; a referent that has to survive intervening material before integration.

Evidence: DLT predicts a discrete cost for each unit of linear distance a dependency is held open in working memory. Lossy-Context Surprisal generalizes this: the parser works from a degraded memory trace, so dependencies across long spans are integrated against unreliable representations. Object-relative clauses (“the director that the dancer admired”) are the canonical case.

Detection: flag sentences where the subject and main verb are separated by more than ~7 words of intervening material, or where multiple clauses nest without a top-level break.

5. Lexical density

Pattern: ratio of content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) to total words — or to function words. High lexical density characterizes written-mode prose; spoken language runs much lower.

Evidence: Halliday’s foundational distinction: written language is “crystalline” (structurally simple, lexically dense, heavy nominalization); spoken is “choreographic” (structurally intricate, lexically sparse). Modern corpus work replicates this across English, Bantu, Chinese.

Detection: easy to compute mechanically (POS tag, count). The diagnostic question is whether the density matches the channel. Heavy nominalization in a piece meant to read conversationally is a fix target. Sparse content words in a reference document is wasted space.

6. The compression check

Pattern: short text that feels tight but has actually been gutted. Symptoms: unexplained referent jumps, deleted causal links, pronouns whose antecedents drift, single sentences that compress what should be three.

Evidence: this is the synthesis of levers 1, 3, and 4. The “shorter is better” edit pass routinely violates UID (creating surprisal spikes), inflates propositional density without lowering word count proportionally, and compresses long dependencies into ambiguous reference. All three damage the reader; the author saved characters.

Detection question: did density rise proportionally to the cut? If a pass dropped 20% of words but the propositional content was unchanged, surprisal went up. If a pass dropped 20% of words and 20% of ideas, the cut was clean. The check separates good editing from false economy.

Contested or context-bound

These are real findings worth knowing but not robust enough to lint against.

  • Uniform Information Density as audience design — UID predicts that speakers/writers smooth surprisal across the signal to aid comprehension. Strongly replicated in production (syllable lengthening, optional “that” retention, lexical choice). Much weaker in comprehension — there’s a distinct dearth of evidence that readers prefer uniform text in actual comprehension tasks. Mandarin classifier production (Zhan & Levy 2018, 2019) and English dative alternation studies are explicit counterexamples. UID-based prescriptions for the writer are reasonable; UID-based predictions about the reader are not.
  • Coupé et al. (2019) 39 bits/s convergence — single study across 17 languages found that speech rate × information density converges near a constant transmission rate. Heavily cited, but no independent experimental replication established at the time of writing, and the syllable-inventory and language-sample limitations are non-trivial. Treat as suggestive.
  • Linear vs. super-linear surprisal-RT — Smith & Levy’s linear finding replicates in many languages and corpora; super-linear (positive quadratic) effects appear consistently in Russian and German and when monolingual GPT-2-style models are used cross-lingually. The shape of the linking function is sensitive to the language model used to estimate surprisal. The qualitative claim — more surprising = more expensive — is robust. The exponent is not.
  • LLM perplexity as readability proxy — moderate-to-strong correlations (0.4–0.6) with expert readability ratings, beats surface-feature formulas in zero-shot settings. But at very large scale (GPT-4, Llama 3+), perplexity diverges from human reading times — the model is no longer a resource-constrained parser. Smaller models sometimes fit human data better.

What this doesn’t cover

  • Paragraph-level structure. The empirical literature operates at character, word, and sentence levels. Projecting surprisal-based prescriptions to topic sentences, paragraph length, or section ordering is speculation, not empirical extension.
  • Local-to-global comprehension mapping. There is no validated mathematical model linking word-level surprisal to the construction of a multi-paragraph situation model. Density tuning at the sentence level does not automatically yield comprehension at the document level.
  • WEIRD bias. Most of the foundational eye-tracking and reading-time corpora are English-language and Western-participant. Cross-linguistic generalization is partial.

Prompt patterns

Three templates operationalize the levers above. The first is for analysis (input: a draft), the second for improvement (input: a draft flagged as too compressed), the third for prompt-specific compression checking.

Density scan (analysis)

Scan the following text for information-density failure modes. For each
finding, give: (a) the location (sentence or span), (b) which lever it
hits, (c) one-line reason, (d) suggested rewrite if obvious.

Levers:
1. Surprisal spike — missing connective ("that", "because", "however"),
   asyndetic clause boundary, deleted determiner before long modifier.
2. Sentence-length monotony — 4+ consecutive sentences within ±20% of
   each other, in either direction.
3. Propositional density mismatch — terse word count packing many
   predicate-argument units; flag if the channel doesn't justify the
   density.
4. Long-distance dependency — subject-to-verb >7 words intervening, or
   nested clauses without a top-level break.
5. Lexical density mismatch — heavy nominalization in conversational
   register, or sparse content in reference register.
6. Compression trap — text reads short but ideas weren't reduced; cuts
   removed cohesion rather than content.

Do not rewrite. Do not recommend wholesale style changes. Flag only.
Cap at 10 findings, ranked by severity. Note explicitly what you
checked and didn't find.

[paste]

Smoothing pass (improvement)

This text has been compressed too aggressively — it reads tight but
forces the reader to infer cohesion that should be explicit. Restore
function words and connective markers where their absence creates a
surprisal spike. Do not lengthen by adding new content; only restore
predictive cues that the reader needs to anticipate the next clause
cheaply.

Specifically restore:
- "that" complementizers before unexpected embedded clauses
- causal/contrastive connectives ("because", "however", "therefore",
  "while") between clauses with implicit logical relation
- determiners before long noun phrases
- explicit antecedents for ambiguous pronouns

Show before/after for each change with a one-line reason.

Compression check (for prompts and short specs)

This is a [prompt / spec / instruction]. It needs to be tight but not
gutted. Audit it for the compression trap:

For each clause, ask:
- Could the receiver misparse this on first pass?
- Is there an implicit causal/contrastive link the receiver has to
  infer?
- Does any content word stand in for an idea that needed two or three?
- Are there pronouns or referents whose antecedent is not unambiguous?

Output: a list of compression-trap candidates, each with the question
it fails and a minimum-cost restoration. If the text is clean, say so.
