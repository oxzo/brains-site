Probability Judgment Biases

What it is

The human mind is fundamentally poor at processing statistical probability. We don’t natively represent distributions, base rates, or independence — we substitute similarity, vividness, and narrative. This note collects the biases that specifically distort judgments of likelihood: how likely an event is, how informative a sample is, and how confident we should be in a prediction.

Why it matters

Every estimate you make at work — project risk, incident probability, model accuracy, hiring success, investment outcomes — is a probability judgment. If the underlying estimator is broken, no amount of downstream rigor will save the decision. Recognizing which bias is active lets you reach for the right correction (base-rate anchoring, reference classes, calibration training) instead of just “thinking harder.”

Details

Gambler’s fallacy vs. hot hand fallacy

Two opposing errors, same root cause: the mind’s refusal to accept that sequences of independent events are allowed to look streaky.

FeatureGambler’s fallacyHot hand fallacy
Core beliefA streak must end to restore balanceA streak will continue due to momentum
Typical contextGames of pure chance (roulette, coins, lottery)Skill-based or performance contexts (sports, trading, hiring)
Underlying errorMisunderstanding statistical independenceLaw of small numbers — mistaking variance for a trend
CorrectionRemind yourself the coin has no memoryAsk whether the sample is big enough to justify the inference

Both are alive and well outside casinos. Studies of asylum judges and loan officers show they start to “autocorrect” streaks of approvals by rejecting the next qualified applicant — a gambler’s fallacy dressed up as fairness. On the other side, traders and hiring managers routinely extrapolate momentum from very small samples of success.

Representativeness heuristic and the Tom W. problem

Kahneman and Tversky’s classic demonstration: participants read a personality sketch of a fictional graduate student that matched the “computer scientist” stereotype. ~95% predicted computer science as his major, ignoring the base rate that humanities and education programs had far higher enrollment. The mind substitutes how much the description resembles a category for how common the category is.

This is the engine behind a lot of interview-based hiring, clinical diagnosis, and product-market judgments: the prototype does the work, the base rate is forgotten.

Base rate fallacy

The direct consequence: systematically under-weighting background frequencies. The canonical medical example reliably defeats both patients and clinicians without explicit Bayesian reasoning: a positive result on a test for a rare disease is often more likely a false positive than a true positive, because the base rate of the disease is very low (see cognitive-biases → medicine).

Useful reflex: when estimating P(event), ask “how often does this happen in the reference class?” before adjusting for the specific case.

Illusion of validity

Unwarranted confidence in the accuracy of one’s judgments, persisting even after those judgments have been statistically shown to be little better than chance. Kahneman’s army-selection work is the origin story: evaluators’ predictions of leadership potential correlated near zero with outcomes, but each new evaluation still produced a strong subjective sense of “I can see who will succeed.” The internal coherence of the narrative is mistaken for accuracy.

Where it shows up: technical interviews, VC pitch meetings, clinical gestalt, and any setting where experts have a richly-textured mental model but thin outcome feedback.

Hard-easy effect

Confidence calibration is not uniform. On very hard questions (low-probability judgments), people are overconfident; on very easy ones, they are underconfident. Classic Lichtenstein & Fischhoff demonstrations using general-knowledge trivia show this repeatedly. The pragmatic consequence: the more novel or complex the decision, the more overconfidence you should expect from yourself and others.

Dunning-Kruger as a probability-judgment failure

Dunning-Kruger (covered in cognitive-biases) is fundamentally a probability judgment: how likely am I to be right? The metacognitive failure is that the skills needed to be competent are the same skills needed to assess one’s competence. Novices cannot estimate their own error rate because they cannot see their errors; experts overestimate others’ ability because the task feels easy.

Practical corrections

  • Reference class forecasting — find the base rate of similar projects/events and anchor your estimate there before adjusting
  • Calibration training — record confidence levels on predictions and score them; over dozens of predictions, miscalibration becomes visible
  • Explicit independence check — for any streak argument, ask “would the next outcome change if I hadn’t seen the prior ones?”
  • Pre-registration of prediction — write the estimate before seeing the outcome, not after (kills hindsight bias too)
