Agentic SDLC — The Best Guess, the Alternatives, and What Decides

Drafted by Claude on 2026-11-04 under the program’s delegation (D-008); not yet reviewed by oxzo. Every Decision line below is Claude’s; the end-of-program review packet ranks them.

Deliverable of Plan 11 in the Agentic SDLC program. It answers the program’s question — for enterprise-level software built with coding agents as of November 2026, what lifecycle is the best-supported guess, what alternatives remain live, and what observable conditions decide between them — from the desk-research notes alone. Plan 10 (the pilot) was cancelled before it ran (D-089): no GitHub organisation, no second human, no empirical observation exists anywhere in this program beyond what the drafter read. Every claim below that would have rested on a pilot’s grade-B observation is written and labelled untested instead, per the master plan’s evidence rule.

As of 2026-11-04. Evidence ids (E-NNN) point into projects/agentic-sdlc/evidence-ledger; decision ids (D-NNN) into projects/agentic-sdlc/direction-log.

Terms used below

Short codes recur throughout; this table is the one stop, so the note stands alone without the other eleven.

CodeMeans
G1–G6The program’s six pass/fail gates (agentic-sdlc-evaluation-criteria). G1 — no one certifies their own change. G2 — a change’s authority, intent, context, action and artifact (plus a fall-back record where a regime requires one) stay recoverable from retained records without the session that produced it. G3 — a named human could have blocked every change. G4 — every control the lifecycle relies on is enforced outside the agent’s own reach, not just by instruction. How the three candidates were assembled

Plan 11 step 1 ran /contradiction-scan across all seventeen program documents and found no contradiction and one already-reconciled tension (D-090). Steps 2–4 then assembled candidates from the nine phase notes’ own best-guess and alternative sections, scored them against agentic-sdlc-evaluation-criteria, and built the table below (D-091). Three candidates survive to scoring — not the checkpoint’s leading/runner-up pair (D-083), because synthesis asks a different question than the pilot did: which candidates survive to a contingency table, not which one gets piloted first.

  • A — the assembled lifecycle. The checkpoint’s leading candidate: the strawman as amended by Plans 02–09, in the revised five-phase partition (D-073).
  • B — the platform-native baseline. Spotify’s “Honk” background-agent pipeline, GitHub’s platform rules for its coding agent, and Stripe’s “Minions,” read as literally sourced and then completed with A’s grafted mechanisms where they are silent (below).
  • C — the classic gated lifecycle. NIST SP 800-53 / FFIEC / PCI DSS / DO-178C / SLSA Source L4 / ITIL change classes, read as the pre-agent baseline and completed the same way B was.

C is a genuinely distinct admission policy — blanket human review of every change, no tiering at all — not a model-specific variant of A or B, so the master plan’s ≤3-candidate merge trigger does not fire (D-091).

The central finding: three custom mechanisms, none independently evidenced, all shared

Before describing the three candidates separately, here is the finding that makes most of that description moot. G1 (under its settled strict reading, D-034), G2 and G5 are each met only by a custom mechanism the program invented by assembly. These are three distinct artifacts — a check-file lock, a joined write-once record, and an irreversibility-classification-plus-dual-authorization rule — not one mechanism wearing three names. What they share: none has independent evidence, none is native to B or C, and all three are imported identically into every candidate.

  • G1. Under the program’s settled strict reading (D-034, settling D-014), none of B’s or C’s native sources wall off a check-file edit behind a separate owner approval distinct from the whole-PR approval. On the survey’s own scorecard, Spotify and GitHub pass G1 only on the lenient reading the program has since abandoned; Stripe’s G1 is silent even under the lenient reading — its posts “state no mechanism that keeps a minion from merging, and do not say whether it can change tests or lint configuration inside its diff” (agentic-sdlc-proposals-survey). So B passes G1 at all only by importing A’s check-file lock (the add-versus-edit detector, the owner-approval requirement, the pure-test-addition exemption); C, built from texts that predate agent producers entirely, needs the same import.
  • G2. No surveyed system joins the five record columns (agentic-sdlc-proposals-survey). All three candidates pass G2 only via the joined record Plan 07 designed (D-051): the change record, evidence bundle and release record, joined on PR id, exported at write time to a write-once store neither the agent nor the directing engineer can write.
  • G5. No source, classic or agentic, treats irreversible changes as a class with a stricter gate — the survey’s own trigger-1 verdict puts it plainly: “G5 has no evidence base anywhere, classic or agentic” (agentic-sdlc-proposals-survey). ITIL’s change classes narrow but do not close this for C: they are urgency- and risk-based, not reversibility-based (survey addendum). All three pass only via A’s grafted classification-plus-dual-authorization mechanism.

So of six gates, three are met only by mechanisms no deployed lifecycle runs (strawman gate-coverage table). The checkpoint’s K2 scope note named each of those mechanisms separately as untested — D-078 lists the joined record, the release-policy check, the check-file detector, revert-and-rerun, dual authorization and the exclusion rule as six distinct items, not one. What distinguishes the candidates on paper, then, is not what carries the evidence weight between them. But “not evidenced” is not “identical,” and that claim has a real gap: nobody has attempted an independently constructed alternative completion of B’s or C’s gaps to see whether it would converge on A’s mechanism or diverge from it. Until that attempt is made, this finding describes what the program happened to build, not a proof that no other design could pass these gates.

G4 is more mixed for C than G1/G2/G5. Classic regimes are not entirely silent here: NIST CM-5 and PCI 6.5.3 (secondary) state restrictions on who may change production, enforced by mechanism (agentic-sdlc-proposals-survey’s classic-baselines table) — a real, independently sourced G4-relevant principle, just not an agent-specific one. Nothing in them addresses sandboxing, network egress or credential scoping for an agent producer. So C’s G4 is part-native (role-based production-access restriction) and part-grafted (the agent-specific technical boundary, imported from A or B). B’s G4 is the one gate where B stands on its own native evidence rather than a graft — Spotify’s stop-hook sandboxed container (E-125), GitHub’s firewall (E-101; E-100 backs its separate G1 merge-restriction, not G4), and Stripe’s devbox isolation (E-126) are each independently sourced at grade B from a named organisation, not assembled.

G3 and G6 pass natively for all three: a named human approves somewhere in every candidate (G3), and all three use plain-text, model-free artifacts (G6).

V2/E-473 is the one cell in the whole scoring pass backed by real evidence rather than being untested for all three. The UK FCA’s review of 23 firms and over a million production changes found change advisory boards “approved over 90% of the major changes they reviewed” (E-473, grade A for the descriptive figures, pre-agent). This is direct negative evidence specific to C’s always-reviewed, no-tiering shape, not a generic risk shared evenly across the three.

The procedural default: Candidate A, the assembled lifecycle

Read this heading as “what the master plan’s own rule produces,” not as “the evidence-backed winner.” Program trigger 1 did not fire at the survey stage (D-013), so the master plan’s rule makes the candidate the composite of the phase plans’ best guesses. A is that composite in the revised partition — true by the rule’s construction, not because A beat B or C in a comparison. The central finding above shows A, B and C share the same unevidenced core on three of six gates, so A does not win there, and the contingency table below shows no candidate wins every column. Turning that into a single ranked answer would need real numbers and the weighting Plan 11’s brief required, and that weighting has never been tested against real numbers (see “What this note does not resolve”).

With that said plainly up front: A is the program’s designated default for three directional, untested reasons — each graded for what it is worth in the contingency table below, not asserted as proven superiority:

  • It is the only candidate with an explicit admission-policy relief (tier 0) that can lower cost on non-code content in some regime classes.
  • It has a named, built-out team-topology and governance mapping (ten roles plus two duties, four regime classes) that B and C would have to improvise.
  • It has an explicit, four-event-triggered phase for pattern-setting architectural decisions, where B has none (a named failure mode, below) and C’s architecture board is a native but per-milestone, not per-change, instrument.

Phases. Event-entered stretches, each run at its own grain; a change enters only the phases its events call for (checkpoint’s revised partition, D-073).

Two out-of-band change routes with their own entry, approval and record: class A security-parameter changes (D-049), and ops-agent writes under per-action credentials (D-040). Five standing functions hold accountabilities and feedback loops across the phases: operations, verification (oracles, held-out suites, check files, tier policy), platform (envelope, credentials, evidence store), support (the owning team for the support period), and governance (regime map, compliance gate, model register).

Roles. The ten roles plus two duties of agentic-sdlc-team-topology (D-059): directing engineer, requirement owner, area technical owner (rule and contract owner), approver (a duty, not a job), release owner, on-call engineer, platform owner, verification owner, design-system owner and design engineer, compliance owner, security function, plus the approver and agent-identity-sponsor duties. Collapse rules by regime class are in team-topology’s step 4 table; a two-owning-team or one-team-plus-external-approver-pool floor is the smallest configuration that can run any tier-2 path (every enterprise has one: CI files, security paths and check files are tier 2 by rule).

Artifacts of record. The requirement baseline and intent records; the rule set, decision records, contract files and irreversibility classification; the diff and the write-time change record; the evidence bundle; the release record. All joined on PR id plus commit ids in the write-once store, exported at write time (D-051).

Gates. As in the strawman’s gate-coverage table. The regime class decides tier 0 and the release approval (D-049): no tier 0 in classes A (DORA full framework) or B (internal human-approval standards, SLSA L4); in class C only for expressly preapproved, non-executable types under the escape-rate condition; in class D the same, by the deterministic path rule (D-072). Everywhere else, tiers 1 and 2 apply: one independent approver by default, two (one from outside the team) for security, CI, check files and irreversible classes.

Feedback loops. Incident to intent, opened by the incident review (D-081); rule red to architecture decision; human correction to rule; model change to golden-set check (D-068). Metrics M1–M11 have frozen definitions (agentic-sdlc-economics-and-metrics, D-067).

The eight questions, condensed

  1. Survives? All five phases survive at a different grain than the pre-agent version; requirements and architecture run per-change/per-decision rather than once per project (D-018, D-023); build absorbs module design (D-073); verify stays separate from build with the forge re-run as the gate of record (D-035); release absorbs system test and stays separate from operations, which becomes a standing function (D-039).
  2. Who does the work? Humans hold every named approval, ownership and acceptance role; agents draft, propose and produce; the directing engineer cuts units and answers for the PR but cannot approve it.
  3. Artifact of record. As above — the joined record is the program’s single largest untested invention.
  4. The gate. As above — three of six gates (G1 strict, G2, G5) resolve to one untested mechanism; G4 is evidenced at the mechanism level (grade B, multiple organisations) but unverified in use; G3 and G6 pass cleanly.
  5. Failure modes. From the phase notes: agents guess instead of asking (E-149); prose rules decay within a session (E-204, E-208); the envelope leaks where a vendor default is open (E-287–E-290); CABs rubber-stamp under volume (E-473); a model swap silently breaks an approval (E-345, E-347, E-350, E-351).
  6. Team shape. Headcount relocates toward directing, checks and review rather than shrinking, conditional on demand absorbing agent output — untested (D-057, D-060).
  7. What would change this. See the watch list below.
  8. Best guess and alternatives. This section and the next.

The alternatives, and when each wins

B — the platform-native baseline

As literally sourced, B is incomplete, not a cheaper A. Spotify, GitHub and Stripe each name no zero-human tier and no feedback loop from production back to requirements or architecture (D-091) — a fifth-part gap the checkpoint’s “completed with G2 and G5” framing never covered. Structurally, every change in B gets reviewed; there is no tiering at all. That makes B closer to C’s always-reviewed posture than to A’s tiered one, even though its mechanisms look native and modern.

A survivorship caveat on B’s whole evidence base. All three of B’s source accounts are self-published success stories from companies with every incentive to showcase a working pipeline, in one narrow professional register — large, well-resourced platform-engineering organisations. Organisations whose agentic pipelines failed, stalled, or were never written up are invisible to this survey by construction (the survey note names this directly: “every practitioner row comes from a first-party success post”). B’s “when B wins” condition below is a direct generalisation from exactly these three cases, not from a sample that could have shown the opposite.

The five parts, completed:

  • Requirements. No owned baseline, no per-area owner, no ambiguity or conflict pass. The ticket or the version-controlled prompt is the requirement (agentic-sdlc-requirements-phase’s “ticket or prompt as requirement” alternative; Spotify’s migration prompt, E-125). This fails G2’s “a requirement holder approved it” reading whenever the prompt’s author is not the holder — a named, uncorrected gap.
  • Architecture. Decided once, enforced mechanically (OpenAI’s fixed layer order, Spotify’s golden state, Stripe’s directory-scoped rule files, E-226, E-228, E-229). No one is named to decide a pattern-setting change when one arrives — agentic-sdlc-design-phase’s “no design phase” alternative calls this exactly: it “fails the moment a change needs a new element type… because nobody is named to decide it, which is G3’s failure.”
  • Build. Identical in shape to A’s build phase — one agent per unit inside a platform-owned envelope (Stripe’s blueprints and devbox isolation, Spotify’s stop-hook sandboxed container and no-push git tool, GitHub’s ephemeral runner and branch scope). This is not a coincidence: A’s build-phase best guess draws from these same three organisations (agentic-sdlc-build-phase).
  • Verify. Always reviewed, natively: Spotify’s verifiers plus an unevaluated LLM-judge veto that blocks about a quarter of sessions (E-125); GitHub’s server-enforced approval rules; Stripe’s selective CI and “another Stripe engineer.” No risk tiering, no held-out-suite/check-file-lock machinery and no requirement-owner acceptance step unless A’s graft is imported.
  • Release. Silent in all three source accounts. Grafted wholesale from A: the release-policy check, dual authorization for the irreversible class, OIDC-only credentials, progressive rollout.

When B wins. Migration factories and mechanical change classes, where the architecture was decided once and nothing pattern-setting arrives, and where the “requirement” genuinely is a short, owner-authored prompt (Spotify’s shape). It is the best guess with the entry events and the owned baseline removed, and it is cheaper in roles: no requirement owner, no area technical owner, no tier-0 escape-rate apparatus to build. It loses the moment a change needs a new architectural decision or a conflicting stakeholder requirement, both of which are G3 failures under B as sourced.

C — the classic gated lifecycle

The five parts, completed:

  • Requirements. Spec-first for everything: agreed before building, always (agentic-sdlc-requirements-phase’s first alternative). This is the classic answer the regulated and contractual profiles already assume (E-141).
  • Architecture. A classic architecture review board with per-change design documents reviewed by humans (agentic-sdlc-design-phase’s first alternative). Its instruments were built for milestones, not for per-change review at agent volume (E-269).
  • Build. No classic regulatory text addresses agent-specific technical boundaries, so C must borrow A’s or B’s H/F envelope to pass G4 at all; nothing in NIST, FFIEC, PCI or DO-178C predates agents enough to specify one natively.
  • Verify. A full human review on every change, with no AI pass, or the classic separate QA team gating releases (agentic-sdlc-verification-phase’s alternatives). No tiering, no zero-human class, ever.
  • Release. A change advisory board retained for every change, or the literal reading that a release owner approves every deployment (agentic-sdlc-release-operate-phase’s alternatives). ITIL’s standard/normal/emergency classes give a stricter path by urgency and risk (survey addendum, E-189) but not by reversibility, so C still needs A’s G5 graft.

When C wins. Where a regime text is read to require independent human review of every change and no deterministic-check substitute (agentic-sdlc-design-phase’s and agentic-sdlc-verification-phase’s classic alternatives both name this condition, neither confirmed by Plan 07’s search), or where change volume is low enough that a board can read every change rather than sample it. It is undercut, specifically and by real evidence, by E-473: the one dataset in the whole program shows blanket board review degenerating to near-total approval under volume. C is the most expensive candidate per change — potentially two review layers (architecture board plus change board) where A and B have one or zero — and the least evidenced to actually deliver the assurance it is chosen for.

No candidate wins every column. That is itself the finding the criteria note’s “a null is not a kill” clause anticipates: the gates matter, and — on this evidence — the lifecycle shape wrapped around them matters less than which conditions a given enterprise is actually in.

What would falsify the two weakest cells. The “legacy-heavy codebase → tie” row has no stated falsifier anywhere in this note: it would be tested by building a legacy-behaviour oracle for the same codebase under each of the three candidates’ rules and comparing its cost and coverage, not asserted as identical by default. The central finding above (three mechanisms, none independently evidenced) would be tested by someone actually attempting a different completion of B’s or C’s G2/G5 gaps — a genuinely distinct joined-record or irreversibility-classification design, not a relabelling of A’s — and checking whether it converges on A’s mechanism or diverges from it. Neither test has been run. Both are named explicitly rather than left as unlabelled gaps, because an untested claim with no stated test is weaker than one with a named, unmet one.

What the central finding’s own evidence depends on. The claim that “no surveyed system” meets G2 or G5 rests on a bundle, not an isolated fact:

  • the completeness of Plan 01’s and Plan 07’s source search;
  • the definition of “joined” fixed by G2’s five-column schema (a design choice made at D-034/D-051, not a given);
  • the strict-versus-lenient reading of G1 settled at D-034 (a judgement call); and
  • the fetch fidelity of the underlying ledger rows (several of which are flagged in the ledger itself as time-sensitive or extract-only).

A falsification of “no surveyed system does this” would really be a verdict on that whole bundle, not on the mechanism-sharing claim in isolation.

What one person could not test, and what Plan 11 found instead

Plan 10 was cancelled before step 1b (D-089): no GitHub organisation, no second human role, no empirical observation of any kind exists in this program beyond the drafter’s reading. Per D-089, Plan 10’s “what one person cannot test” framing — team topology, real separation of duties, a real auditor, a real change board, real legacy work — now applies to the whole candidate set, not only to the roles a solo pilot could never fill. Nothing below should be read as resolved by a pilot that did not happen.

The pilot’s preregistration (projects/agentic-sdlc/pilot-preregistration) filed 53 mechanisms and held 24 scored predictions, frozen as a design artifact. They are resolved as unrun in the calibration log as part of this synthesis’s retrospective, not silently left pending past a date nobody will act on.

Untested parts, listed plainly

Everything below rests on grade C alone, on reasoning without a ledger row, or on a pilot that never ran. Nothing in this list is resolved by this synthesis; it is the honest inventory the master plan’s evidence rule requires.

  • The shared gate core (G1 strict, G2, G5). The check-file add-versus-edit detector, the joined write-once record, and the irreversible-class classification plus dual authorization are one untested hypothesis shared by all three candidates (above). No deployed lifecycle anywhere in the survey does any of this.
  • The tier-0 escape-rate metric. D-081 requires a class-level escape-rate metric before any executable tier-0 class can be scored; this metric has not been defined anywhere in the program, including here. It is named as a direct follow-on below, not filled.
  • A’s distinguishing custom additions. The revert-and-rerun step, the release-policy check over bundles, the exclusion rule computing approver distinctness, and the outside-signal canary — all named by the checkpoint (D-078) as untested and never run, and none has a named falsifying test anywhere in this program: each would need a trial run against real changes (a null run for the exclusion rule, a false-negative count for the canary) that nothing in the program’s remaining scope currently produces.
  • Team-topology’s sizing function. The accounting identity in agentic-sdlc-team-topology step 5 is true by construction; which term dominates (review versus directing hours) is unmeasured, and the theory-holding floor rests on a single C-grade source (Naur, E-491).
  • The economics metric set’s link to outcomes. M1–M11 have frozen definitions but no data behind any of them (D-067); the human side of cost per change (M4) is explicitly “the pilot’s first number to collect” and was never collected.
  • Every model-change containment practice. Pin-and-watch, the golden set, re-checking gate coverage — all grade B or reasoning only; no source shows any of them reduces regressions that reach production (agentic-sdlc-economics-and-metrics step 4). No falsifying test is named for this either: it would need a measured per-item flip rate across a real model swap, compared with and without the containment practices, which nothing in the program produces.
  • B’s missing feedback loop and zero-human tier. Never specified by any of its three source organisations; inventing one to complete B per the criteria note’s five-part definition is itself an untested addition this synthesis has not performed.
  • C’s borrowed envelope. No classic regulatory text specifies agent-specific technical boundaries; C’s G4 pass depends entirely on importing a mechanism no classic source actually requires.
  • Whether any control catches more than it costs. Named directly in agentic-sdlc-governance-layer’s best guess: “whether the controls catch more than they cost is untested,” and nothing in this synthesis changes that.
  • The weighting itself. The tradeoff-axis weights in agentic-sdlc-evaluation-criteria are Claude’s value judgement (D-008), not a finding; Plan 11’s brief requires showing whether the best guess survives other weightings (equal weights, each family moved ±0.10). This sensitivity check has not yet been run as a numeric exercise — the contingency table above is qualitative, not a scored ranking under alternative weights, because no candidate has numeric scores to re-weight: every family cell above is itself graded “untested” rather than measured. The honest statement is that the weighting question is moot until at least one axis has real numbers behind it.

Second-order risks of adopting the best guess

A /second-order forward simulation of an enterprise adopting Candidate A surfaced how the program’s own named gaps would actually propagate, rather than new unknowns. Three findings are concrete enough to record here rather than only in the simulation’s own output:

  • The classification-gaming equilibrium. G1 exists to stop a producer certifying its own work. The regime-class map (governance note) that decides whether a system gets tier-0 relief at all is itself scored by a compliance owner under incentive pressure to classify systems into the cheaper class — the same self-certification pathology G1 was built to prevent, recurring one layer up, at the classification step rather than the code-change step. The governance note already names the regime map as “a single point of failure” (failure modes, step 5) but does not name this specific incentive structure.
  • The platform team’s maintenance burden is non-amortizing by the criteria note’s own logic. The three custom mechanisms (above) have no vendor roadmap or open-source community behind them; every model or forge change the economics note’s step 4 describes is a maintenance event for all three at once, with nothing to amortize the cost against. This is the criteria note’s own tie-break clause turned into a cost line: “every extra part is one more thing a team can let decay unnoticed.”
  • Dual authorization can sit between an incident and its fix. If an incident’s forward-fix or rollback itself falls in the irreversible-change class, G5’s dual-authorization requirement sits between the on-call engineer and remediation mid-incident — a direct mechanical tension with T9 (recovery time) exactly when speed matters most. No phase note names this interaction explicitly; the release-operate note’s ops-agent rules (D-040) cover routine writes, not an irreversible-class fix made under incident pressure.

What would change this — the watch list

Collected from every phase note’s pivot triggers, dated 2026-11-04 unless the note itself gave an earlier date.

Gate evidence

  • A controlled study showing agent pipelines that check themselves matching independently checked ones on defect escape reopens G1 at the checkpoint (criteria note).
  • A forge shipping a default that tells an added check file from an edited one, compares tests by identity, or locks check files by default moves the shared gate core from custom-and-untested to configuration (verify, release notes). Resolvable check: as of 2027-05-04, has any of GitHub, GitLab or Azure DevOps shipped such a default? (harvested as a dated prediction, below)
  • A supervisor, ESA Q&A or examiner ruling on DORA Art. 17(1)(b) admits tier 0 in class A under the functional reading, reopening the cost advantage A claims in the contingency table (governance note, step 4).
  • A measured injection rate against shipped coding agents, or a measured approval-prompt catch rate, re-grades T1 and T7 (governance note).

Cost and economics

  • Reviewer effort-minutes per agent-authored change, measured at an enterprise with a human-authored comparison, fills the program’s largest blank and could fire Plan 09’s deferred trigger 2 (verification cost per change as the dominant ranking variable) — the condition the checkpoint re-deferred with a 50% threshold that the cancelled pilot was supposed to test (D-077).
  • A measured hours-per-model-migration figure fills M10 and criteria axis T8.
  • An RCT or natural experiment measuring net delivery value from agents on enterprise codebases could reverse Plan 09’s trigger 1 (no grade-A net-speedup source), which currently excludes T3 from the deciding columns.

Team and skills

  • An enterprise study of team, reviewer or approver headcount against agent adoption replaces team-topology’s outside-view estimate (step 3).
  • A study comparing AI review with tiered human review on the same changes, by escaped defects, moves the approver term outside classes A and B if AI catches as much (verify note’s trigger 1, team-topology’s step 7).
  • Outcomes for a deliberate junior-rung design (retention, time to approver, escaped defects) turn team-topology’s step 6 from a design into a finding or drop it.

Governance and legal

  • A court decision allocating liability for defective agent-written code, or a text making an approving engineer personally answerable, rewrites governance step 6.
  • The AICPA’s 2027 criteria, read at source, could move SOC 2 toward class C if they require restriction rather than “restricting or monitoring” of single-user development and deployment.

Model-specific

  • A vendor shipping a sandbox on, fail-closed and pinned by default, with the configuration surface protected, moves build’s H-layer rows to a default install for that vendor (build note).
  • A vendor recording a dated model identifier, provider and region at the forge, signed, moves the change record’s context column from H to F (build note).

What this note does not resolve

  • No numeric contingency table. Every cell above is graded qualitatively (wins directionally / tie / untested) because no candidate’s tradeoff axes have real numbers behind them — the pilot that would have produced the first ones was cancelled (D-089). The weight-sensitivity check Plan 11’s brief requires is therefore moot until at least one axis carries a measurement.
  • No class-level escape-rate metric. D-081 made this a precondition for scoring any executable tier-0 class, and it has not been defined here. This is the single highest-leverage piece of unfinished program work; see the direction log’s closing entry for the follow-on. Resolvable check: is it still undefined as of this note’s review_by date, 2027-02-04? (harvested as a dated prediction, below)
  • No resolution of B’s or C’s completion as “correct.” Completing B and C with A’s grafted mechanisms is this synthesis’s own construction, following the checkpoint’s precedent for the runner-up (D-083) and D-091’s extension of it to C. It has not been attacked by anyone who prefers a different completion.
