Multi-Agent Coordination Topologies

Structural patterns for how agents communicate and coordinate. The choice of topology is the most consequential architectural decision in a multi-agent system — it determines failure modes, scalability ceiling, and observability characteristics.

For framework-level pattern selection, see agent-pipeline-tooling-landscape. This note goes deeper on the structural tradeoffs.

Refreshed 2026-11-02. The new section directly below adds the August–November 2026 evidence. Its headline: no fixed topology wins everywhere; task structure and an independent verifier matter more than the topology label. The 2026-08-03 material underneath stays, with one figure corrected (the “Do More Agents Help?” paper’s v2 numbers) and the self-evolving claim qualified.

Refreshed 2026-08-03. The structural material below (three topologies, their tradeoffs, the selection guide) held up and was not rewritten. Two things did change materially since April: the interop-protocol layer consolidated, and new empirical work moved the single-vs-multi-agent question. What changed since August 2026

Drafted 2026-11-02 from a research run oxzo asked for; not yet reviewed by oxzo.

Last verified 2026-11-02. Every arXiv ID below was checked against the arXiv API, and the quoted numbers against the raw abstract text. Most items are preprints, and a fetch of an abstract is not a read of the paper.

Which topology performs best — the current answer

(est.) None, unconditionally. Five results from the window point to the same ranking of what matters: task structure first, then whether there is an independent verifier, then the topology label, with agent count last.

  • ✅ Kim et al. is now peer-reviewed, and the published version is more cautious. It appeared in Nature Machine Intelligence (July 2026) as “Capable language models can outgrow the benefits of collaboration”; the arXiv version is 2512.08296. It covers five architectures × three LLM families × six benchmarks, with a matched per-system token ceiling. Results run from +80.8% on Finance Agent to −70.0% on PlanCraft, and every multi-agent architecture degrades on PlanCraft (centralized −50.3%). Error amplification by trace runs from independent 17.2× to centralized 4.4×: “validation bottlenecks intercept errors”. The ~45% capability threshold is now presented “as a validated selection rule rather than a coefficient-level scaling law”, because the baseline × team-size interaction does not survive cluster-robust correction. The rule still predicted the sign of the multi-agent effect in 94% of the SWE-bench Verified / Terminal-Bench validation configurations. Under fixed budgets, per-agent reasoning “becomes prohibitively thin beyond three to four agents”. No independent replication or rebuttal was found.
  • ✅ Difficulty decides when hierarchy pays. Across 614 code-generation problems, hierarchical collaboration’s pass@1 advantage over a single agent “grows from 2.4 points … on the easiest third of problems to 21.1 points on the hardest third”, at roughly 10× tokens. A difficulty-aware router running at 40% of always-hierarchical cost reaches 77.7% against 73.6%. The benchmarks are HumanEval-class rather than repo-level work (arXiv 2609.13890).
  • ✅ At matched cost, fixed teams don’t beat a single agent. In a 7B Planner–Executor–Critic team with total model calls held equal, the team scored “0.769 versus 0.754, p = 0.80, despite using 1.8 times more evaluation calls” (2609.04217; small scale, two benchmarks). A September preprint reaches the same split qualitatively: multi-agent helps on long-horizon tasks with sparse dependencies and hurts on tightly coupled sequential ones (2609.19759, abstract only, no numbers).
  • ✅ Generated multi-agent systems lose to self-consistency. “The Illusion of Multi-Agent Advantage” (2606.13003) finds automatically generated multi-agent systems underperform CoT self-consistency at up to 10× cost. Expert-designed ones win only on a diagnostic set that explicitly requires decomposition. (est.) A benchmark that doesn’t require decomposition is not evidence for any topology.
  • ✅ Debate is the weakest aggregation topology at matched compute. Across 23 small models, debate “ties or even loses to self-consistency sampling at 1.6× the wall-clock and 3.4× the token cost” (2609.35875). In a matched-ceiling study, 66% of the cases where debate helped (31/47) came when voting was unanimous but wrong, and cheap routers could not identify those cases in advance (2605.09618). The one positive matched-compute result is at 70B on multiple choice: debate and mixture-of-agents beat self-consistency “by 1.3% and 2.7% points” (2605.01566). (est.) The aggregation gains that survive token-matching sit where a cheap, executable check selects the answer (tests, a program verifier), not where an LLM opines.

What top production coding systems actually run

(est.) One writer, many readers, and a separate verifier. This is the dominant shipped shape. It is Cognition’s rule from April, which the evidence below now supports.

  • ✅ Read fan-out, serial writes. Codex’s docs reserve parallel agents for “read-heavy tasks such as exploration, tests, triage, and summarization” (read via a fetch summary). Claude Code’s agent-teams docs say “for sequential tasks, same-file edits, or work with many dependencies, a single session or subagents are more effective.”
  • ✅ A separate evaluator beats self-review, at a cost. Anthropic’s harness-design post reports that agents “reliably skew positive when grading their own work”. Its planner→generator→evaluator harness cost 6 hr / 200against20min/9 solo on the same app (vendor result, a handful of demo apps).
  • ✅ Parallel writers worked only with hard ownership. Cursor’s long-running browser build used recursive planners and workers, each on its own repo copy, with no worker-to-worker talk and no integrator. Its earlier self-coordinating design with locks failed: “Twenty agents would slow down to the effective throughput of two or three”. The target was greenfield, and the run accepted a steady error rate. Nothing shows this transferring to maintenance work in a shared repo.
  • ✅ The top benchmark harnesses are lean, not swarms. Terminal-Bench 2.0’s #1 (NexAU-AHE, 84.7% ±2.1, read from tbench.ai 2026-11-02) started from a single shell tool with no subagents and evolved its middleware and tools. Ranks 1–5 sit within each other’s error bars. One model swings up to 17 points across harnesses (Opus 4.6: 75.3% in Capy, 58.0% in Claude Code).
  • ○ Self-reported shared-context designs claim to beat orchestrator-worker. DeLM (2606.10662) uses shared context plus a task queue with no main agent, and reports beating Codex, Claude Code and their native subagents “in every setting”, by up to 17.5 points. ScaleMAS (2610.16120) claims to scale to 128 agents with bounded per-agent reads. Both are the proposing authors’ own numbers, with no replication; if they hold, the “3–4 agent ceiling” applies only to designs where read cost grows with team size.

What is actually deployed

  • ✅ Production agents are short and workflow-shaped. MAP (Measuring Agents in Production, arXiv 2512.04123, ICML 2026) ran 20 case-study interviews plus a survey of 86 deployed systems: “68% execute at most 10 steps before human intervention”. 16 of the 20 case studies used structured workflows, and 17 of 20 were built in-house rather than on a framework. The study gives no breakdown by topology.
  • ○ The “~70% of production deployments are supervisor/orchestrator-worker” figure (2026-08-03 refresh) has no primary source. A November search found it only in practitioner blogs, one of which cites an analysis that couldn’t be located. Treat it as folklore; MAP’s structured-workflow share is the nearest real number, and it measures a different thing.
  • ✅ Frameworks converged on the same menu but disagree on the default. Graph or explicit workflows lead in Google ADK 2.x, Microsoft Agent Framework, CrewAI (“start with a Flow”) and LangGraph. “Agents as tools” leads in AWS Strands and the OpenAI Agents SDK. Mastra deprecated its LLM-routed “agent networks” in favour of supervisor agents. AutoGen’s README now says it is archived, with no bug or security fixes (README change 2026-10-16).

— and governance moved

The most consequential change is that agent interop stopped being a standards fight. A four-layer stack has settled, and the two load-bearing protocols are now both under neutral foundations:

  • ✅ MCP (agent↔tool) moved to the Agentic AI Foundation, a directed fund under the Linux Foundation, on 2025-12-09 — “Anthropic is donating MCP to the Agentic AI Foundation.” Technical governance was deliberately unchanged: “For MCP little changes. The governance model we introduced earlier this year continues as is.” Reported scale at the time: “Over 97 million monthly SDK downloads” and “10,000 active servers”.
  • ✅ A2A (agent↔agent) is hosted by the Linux Foundation and reached v1.0, its first stable production-ready spec, in March 2026, adding “Signed Agent Cards for cryptographic identity verification”. More than 150 organisations supported the standard at its one-year mark, with GA support in Microsoft Copilot Studio, Azure AI Foundry and Amazon Bedrock AgentCore Runtime, plus a companion Agent Payments Protocol.
  • ✅ The competing agent-to-agent protocols folded. IBM’s Agent Communication Protocol was archived 2025-08-27 — “ACP is now part of A2A under the Linux Foundation!” — and AGNTCY’s similarly-named Agent Connect Protocol was archived 2026-04-11. AGNTCY (itself under the Linux Foundation since 2025-07-29) now sits underneath as discovery/identity/observability infrastructure rather than competing at the wire level.
  • ○ The resulting layering: MCP for tools, A2A for agent-to-agent, AG-UI for agent-to-user, AGNTCY for discovery and identity underneath.

✅ MCP went stateless in the 2026-07-28 spec — “We’ve officially retired the initialize/initialized exchange along with the Mcp-Session-Id header”, so “any request can now land on any server instance behind a plain round-robin load balancer without needing shared storage.” Roots, Sampling and Logging are deprecated with a twelve-month compatibility window. (est.) For topology design this matters more than it sounds: statelessness removes the sticky-session constraint that pushed tool-serving toward hierarchical fan-in.

○ One caveat worth carrying: a 2026 paper argues MCP, A2A and ACP all lack protocol-native mechanisms for dispute resolution, authority and accountability between the parties using them — interop is solved at the wire level, not the governance level.

The empirical picture moved, and it does not favour fixed multi-agent systems

Four 2026 results, and they cohere into a different recommendation than this note originally implied:

  • ✅ DPBench adapts Dining Philosophers to LLM agents and finds deadlock is a property of the protocol, not the model: “Whether the same model coordinates or deadlocks is determined by the protocol, not by the model’s capability.” One model deadlocked ~90% of the time under a minimal prompt and 0% with three rounds of pre-commitment communication. Counter-intuitively, doubling group size from N=5 to N=10 cut deadlock rather than worsening it.
  • ✅ “Do More Agents Help?” evaluates six multi-agent workflows against a matched single-agent baseline (GPT-4.1, ten benchmarks). Corrected 2026-11-02 to v2 (2026-10-09, retitled “Evaluating Accuracy and Cost in LLM Multi-Agent Workflows”); the v1 figures quoted here earlier are superseded. In v2, the evolving workflow EvoAgent “reaches 79.70% mean pooled accuracy versus 76.65% for Core and exceeds Core in all three runs; the other five MAS trail by 1.71-8.76 percentage points.” LLM-Debate gains 9.67 points on HumanEval but loses 1.96 pooled while using 1.39× the tokens.
  • ✅ OrchBench finds that in orchestration plans, information-transfer coverage between agents predicts quality far better than agent count (0.614 vs −0.021 correlation at n=100 subtasks), and that transfer coverage degrades badly at scale (0.981 → 0.441 at n=1,000).
  • ✅ Aggregation method flips the heterogeneity result. A 2026 paper resolves the standing contradiction between “diverse teams win” and “homogeneous teams win”: with judge-based selection, diverse teams hit an 81% win rate against a single-model baseline; with synthesis-based (MoA) aggregation, the same diverse teams won only 17.9% of the time.

(est.) Read together: topology choice and information flow dominate agent count and model choice. The April version of this note treated topology as the consequential decision, which these results support — but they also say that a fixed topology is usually worse than a dynamic one, and that the cheapest large win is improving what agents pass to each other rather than adding agents. This qualifies the selection guide below: use it to pick a starting shape, not a permanent one.

✅ Cognition revised its “don’t build multi-agents” position on 2026-04-22. The durable principle is narrower than either pole: multiple agents contributing intelligence “while writes stay single-threaded” works (generator-verifier loops, model routing, manager-coordinator), while “arbitrary networks of agents negotiating with each other, is mostly a distraction.” (est.) That is a sharper rule than any topology label — fan out on reading and judging, stay serial on writing.

Framework churn

○ The framework-alignment section below is stale in its names. Since April: Microsoft Agent Framework hit 1.0 GA (2026-04-03) and is positioned as “the direct successor” to both AutoGen and Semantic Kernel, which moved to maintenance mode; AG2 v1.0 replaced the classic GroupChat/swarm/nested-chat patterns with a protocol-driven “Network” model (Hub plus typed channels), moving the old code to a -classic repo; Google ADK 2.0 (Python GA 2026-05-19) replaced its hierarchical agent executor with a graph-based Workflow Runtime; and OpenAI’s Agents SDK gained a model-native harness with multi-provider sandbox execution. (est.) The pattern across all four is the same: everyone converged on graph-or-network execution over fixed role hierarchies.

Three fundamental topologies

Hierarchical orchestration

A centralized supervisor manages task allocation, sequencing, and state transitions. Workers communicate exclusively through the supervisor — no peer-to-peer messaging.

Strengths:

  • Strict auditability — every decision flows through a single authority
  • Prevents unsynchronized state updates
  • Natural layer separation: Strategy → Planning → Execution
  • Reflective retry: supervisor detects and corrects errors before propagation

Weaknesses:

  • Supervisor becomes a bottleneck (every transaction routes through it)
  • Limited horizontal scalability
  • Single point of failure

Best for: compliance-heavy environments, financial systems, any pipeline requiring reproducible audit trails.

Peer-to-peer coordination

Agents communicate directly through message passing. No central authority — agents negotiate tasks, share state, and re-route dynamically.

Strengths:

  • Maximum parallelization
  • Dynamic re-routing on agent failure
  • No single bottleneck

Weaknesses:

  • Highly susceptible to coordination deadlocks (see multi-agent-failure-modes)
  • Near-impossible to enforce global stop conditions
  • Significant communication overhead at each handoff
  • Non-deterministic behavior makes debugging difficult

Best for: exploratory research, brainstorming, scenarios where flexibility matters more than predictability. Rarely appropriate for production enterprise workflows.

Blackboard / shared memory

A centralized knowledge base that all agents monitor independently. Agents observe state and contribute when they can solve a problem — no direct message passing.

Strengths:

  • Eliminates token duplication — facts shared, not copied into each agent’s context
  • Provenance tracking: each fact tagged with contributing agent, resources, timestamps
  • Supports asymmetric access controls (bipartite graphs linking users, agents, resources)
  • Agent selection emerges from blackboard state — no explicit routing logic needed

Weaknesses:

  • Requires careful schema design for shared state
  • Concurrent write conflicts need resolution strategy
  • More complex to implement than hierarchical

Best for: multi-domain analysis, long-running research, systems where multiple specialists contribute to a shared knowledge base. The topology that scales best for token efficiency (see multi-agent-token-efficiency).

Advanced: Shared Recurrent Memory Transformer (SRMT) — pools and globally broadcasts individual working memories, enabling implicit communication without natural language generation overhead.

Empirical comparison (EntQA benchmark)

Enterprise benchmark: simultaneous querying of SQL databases and unstructured document collections.

MetricHierarchicalFlat networkSingle-agent RAG
Tier 3 accuracy (multi-hop)84.5%62.8%45.2%
Hallucination rate7.1%18.2%28.5%
Average analytical steps3.21.81.0
Query latency (p95)2.1s1.4s0.8s

Key takeaway: hierarchical orchestration nearly doubles accuracy over single-agent and suppresses hallucination by 4× — at the cost of ~2.6× latency. The accuracy gain comes from task decomposition into specialized sub-tasks, plus reflective retry that catches errors before propagation.

Hybrid topologies (production reality)

Real systems combine patterns:

  • Hierarchical + Router: each hierarchy level routes to the right child agent based on task type
  • Hierarchical + Blackboard: supervisor manages workflow, shared memory stores intermediate results
  • Sequential + Evaluator: pipeline with quality gates at each stage

Topology selection guide

2026-11-02: run this guide only after the single-vs-multi decision in multi-agent-diminishing-returns. Its node structure is April-vintage. The newer evidence puts “is the task sequential and tightly coupled?” and “is there an independent verifier?” ahead of auditability.

Default to hierarchical. It’s not the most flexible, but it’s the most debuggable, auditable, and resistant to coordination failures. Only move to P2P or blackboard when you can articulate why hierarchical doesn’t work for your specific case.

Self-evolving topologies (frontier research)

Meta-agent systems treat topology design as an optimization problem rather than a human design decision. Dynamically generated, problem-specific topologies outperform static human-designed architectures by 5-25% across math, coding, and QA benchmarks — against the baselines those papers chose. Qualified 2026-11-02: none of the 2026-06+ topology-search papers checked compares against a token-matched single agent or self-consistency. Search methods (ADAS, AFlow) fell below a plain single agent with 4–8B executors in one study. Generated systems lost to CoT self-consistency in another (2606.13003). Still experimental — not production-ready.

See self-evolving-agent-topologies for deep coverage: ADAS, AFlow, EvoAgent, ScoreFlow, FlowReasoner, G-Designer, and 6+ other systems, with benchmark results, algorithm mechanics, safety risks (misevolution), and practical architectural implications.
