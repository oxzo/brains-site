Bottleneck States as Split-Point Heuristics

The plan-split-scan answers whether a plan should be split. This note answers the next question: where should the split land? The structural answer from hierarchical reinforcement learning and human-planning research is consistent: at bottleneck states — points in the state space where many paths converge and through which all forward progress must pass.

Splitting at a bottleneck state truncates the planning tree at a natural seam. Splitting at a non-bottleneck pays the cost of splitting (re-establishing context) without buying its benefit (truncated dependencies). A non-bottleneck here: an arbitrary count of steps, a topical boundary that doesn’t match the state structure.

The graph-theoretic concept

In any state graph, the betweenness centrality of a node is the fraction of shortest paths between all node pairs that pass through it. High-betweenness nodes are bottlenecks — gateway states that most successful paths funnel through.

For planning, the load-bearing property of a bottleneck is dependency truncation: once the executor reaches a bottleneck state, the path from there to the goal can be planned without holding the path from start to bottleneck in working memory. Bottlenecks are where the executor can “forget the past and just go forward.”

Computing exact betweenness centrality requires solving all-pairs shortest paths — intractable for any non-trivial state space and certainly intractable for the human brain or an LLM planning ad hoc. Both biological and artificial planners use heuristics that approximate centrality without computing it directly.

Formal foundations — hierarchical RL

The structural result that bottleneck states are optimal split points was established in computational neuroscience and reinforcement learning:

  • Sutton, Precup & Singh (1999), “Between MDPs and semi-MDPs” introduced the options framework — temporally extended actions (“macro-actions”) for hierarchical RL. The framework needed a principled way to choose the temporal abstraction; bottleneck states became the canonical answer.
  • Solway, Diuk, Córdova, Yee, Barto, Niv & Botvinick (2014), “Optimal Behavioral Hierarchy” (PLOS Computational Biology) formalized this: an optimal behavioral hierarchy is one that minimizes the description length of the agent’s policy across task distributions, and the optimal subgoals turn out to be high-betweenness states. The result is structural — it holds independent of what task is being executed, given the state-space structure.
  • Correa et al. (2020, 2023) cited in the parent note extended this to human planning — humans behaviorally use bottleneck-state proxies when decomposing tasks in gridworld environments, with response-time signatures consistent with the centrality-truncation mechanism.

The human implementation is a memory-based proxy: track which states are frequently visited along successful past paths; treat those as candidate bottlenecks. This avoids the intractable all-pairs computation while capturing the empirical structure of centrality in environments the planner has experience with.

What this looks like in a plan

A plan-state isn’t a literal graph node, but the structural property maps cleanly: a bottleneck in a plan is the state, artifact, or decision that every downstream step depends on. After the bottleneck, the upstream context can be discarded; before it, all downstream work is conditional on its outcome.

Linguistic and structural fingerprints of plan-level bottlenecks:

  • “After we have X, we can…” — X is the bottleneck; everything downstream branches from it
  • “Before we can do Y, we need Z” — Z is the bottleneck for Y
  • An artifact or schema referenced by multiple downstream steps — building it is the bottleneck
  • A decision that determines the shape of the rest of the plan — making it is the bottleneck (e.g., “choose between SQL and document store” before the data-access layer can be designed)
  • A verification gate — confirming a result before proceeding (running a migration; receiving a code review; getting a destructive-action approval). Verification seams — the plan-split-scan’s name for these gates — are bottleneck states in graph-theoretic terms; see Signal 4 of the plan-split-scan

Anti-patterns — where splits don’t help

If the plan-split-scan says “split before issue” but the proposed split point is not at a bottleneck, the split pays cost without earning benefit. Common bad split locations:

  • Arbitrary length boundary — “Plan A: steps 1–5. Plan B: steps 6–10.” If step 6 is mid-flow through a single concern and depends on volatile state from step 4, the split forces re-establishing that state at the boundary. The cost is paid, no truncation is purchased.
  • Topical boundary that crosses state-carry — “Plan A: backend. Plan B: frontend.” If the frontend plan’s first step depends on a backend schema decision made mid-Plan-A and not yet finalized, the topical boundary cuts state-carry that hasn’t reached a stable point. Wait until the bottleneck (schema finalized) before splitting.
  • Splitting just before a destructive operation but downstream of the speculative state that produced it — the destructive step depends on the speculative output. If the split is at the destructive boundary but the speculative state is still mid-formation, the bottleneck is upstream of the split point, not at it. Move the split upstream to the point where the speculative state is confirmed.

The corrective: find the bottleneck first, then split there. If no bottleneck is visible within the proposed splitting region, the plan may need work before splitting — surfacing the implicit state structure so the bottleneck can be identified.

Detection heuristic (without running graph algorithms)

For a draft plan being audited:

  1. List the artifacts and decisions the plan produces at each step (schemas, files, decisions, intermediate outputs, confirmations).
  2. For each downstream step, ask which upstream artifact(s) it depends on. Steps that depend on the same upstream artifact are downstream of the same bottleneck.
  3. The artifact with the most downstream dependents is the highest-betweenness state in the plan. That’s the natural split point.
  4. Verification gates are bottlenecks by construction — if the plan has explicit verification steps, those are split candidates regardless of centrality calculation.

This heuristic mirrors the memory-based proxy humans use empirically: tracking which states are referenced by (i.e., “visited along the path to”) many subsequent states. You’re approximating betweenness via citation count rather than computing it.

Mapping to the plan-split-scan signals

The five signals of the plan-split-scan connect to the bottleneck mechanism:

  • Signal 1 (concern count) — concerns intersect at bottlenecks (the state that connects auth and database is a bottleneck across concerns). Split at the intersection.
  • Signal 2 (state-carry depth) — long state-carry means there’s a bottleneck the planner didn’t surface. The fix is to make the bottleneck explicit, then split there (or restate the bottleneck content at the point of use).
  • Signal 3 (decision branches) — the branching decision is a bottleneck. Either resolve it before the plan, split there, or use an implementation intention to bind the branch evaluation to a deterministic trigger.
  • Signal 4 (verification seams) — verification seams ARE bottleneck states. This signal is the most direct bottleneck-detection move.
  • Signal 5 (reversibility boundary) — destructive operations sit on the downstream side of a bottleneck (the speculative state that produced the inputs to the destructive operation). Split between the bottleneck (state confirmed) and the destructive step.

Tradeoffs and context-dependence

  • Bottleneck identification is itself search. For a plan you wrote, the bottlenecks may be obvious — you know what depends on what. For a plan you’re auditing without context, identifying the bottlenecks requires understanding the dependencies, which is non-trivial. The heuristic above is approximation, not algorithm.
  • Plans aren’t formally state graphs. The betweenness-centrality framing is structural analogy, not literal computation. The analogy is load-bearing where the plan has a state structure — most engineering plans, migration plans, multi-step research plans — and weakens where it doesn’t (creative work, brainstorming, exploratory analysis).
  • Memory-based proxy fails on first-of-kind plans. Humans use the proxy because they have history with environments they’re planning in. A genuinely novel plan (new system, new domain, no prior runs) has no memory to draw on; bottleneck identification falls back on first-principles dependency analysis. The first-principles analysis is more expensive but more reliable when memory is absent.
  • Some plans have no dominant bottleneck. A plan with five independent, non-interacting concerns has no high-betweenness state — every step is its own forward-flow. In this case the bottleneck-heuristic answer is “split by concern, not by state” — and Signal 1 (concern count) is the relevant move, not Signal 4.

What this doesn’t cover

  • Formal computation of betweenness on plan graphs. This note treats bottleneck identification as a heuristic move on a roughly-state-structured artifact. Tools that compute centrality on workflow DAGs (project-management software, task-graph analyzers) exist and could in principle be applied; not addressed here.
  • Multi-bottleneck plans. A long plan may have several bottlenecks at different scales. Which to split at is a design choice trading off the cost of splitting (more total plans) against the cognitive load of the un-split alternative.
  • Dynamic bottlenecks. In plans where the structure depends on intermediate results (the bottleneck emerges from a step’s output), bottleneck identification must happen during execution, not before. This is the case where implementation intentions or split-into-separate-plans is the right move — you can’t pre-identify a bottleneck whose existence depends on a not-yet-run step.

Counterargument

The strongest objection: plan structure is rarely tree-shaped or graph-shaped in the formal sense computational frameworks assume. Most engineering plans are loosely sequential with implicit dependencies, not formal DAGs. Importing betweenness-centrality vocabulary into informal plan auditing risks pseudo-quantitative framing — making the audit sound rigorous while doing the same heuristic work that pre-existing terms (“checkpoint,” “milestone,” “phase boundary”) covered.

The honest position: the bottleneck-state lens adds value at the placement layer of plan splitting, where the alternative is unprincipled boundary choice. It does not add value if the planner already knows where the checkpoints belong — in that case “split at checkpoint X” is the same answer the bottleneck heuristic would produce. The lens is useful when planners are unsure which of several candidate split points is the right one, and the dependency analysis disambiguates.

The framework is also potentially overfitted to state-graph-shaped problems. Plans whose structure is genuinely decision-tree-shaped (lots of branching, less linearity) may be better served by the implementation-intentions framing than by bottleneck identification.
