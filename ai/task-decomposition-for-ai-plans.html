Task Decomposition for AI Plans

The cognitive-science research on task decomposition — why humans intuitively break large tasks into smaller subtasks — has direct analogs in LLM and agent execution. This note bridges the research basis to the design problem: when should a plan or prompt be split into multiple, what structural signals indicate a split is overdue, and when does decomposing actually hurt.

This is the plan-structure sibling to information-density-for-llm-prompts (which addresses density inside a single prompt) and the executor-side sibling to cognitive-burden-of-ai-use (which addresses load on the human reviewer). As of 2026-05.

Why this matters

Whether the executor is a human, an LLM agent, or a Claude Code session, a plan that is too large for it fails in characteristic ways: attention drift on later steps, confabulated state-carry (earlier decisions mis-recalled by later steps), decision branches that flatten into one arbitrary path, and verification debt that accumulates until the final output is unreviewable. A plan that is too small fails differently: switching overhead, lost context across handoffs, and dilution of the larger “why.”

Choosing plan size is a design decision, not a stylistic preference. The choice is load-bearing for whether the executor can hold the plan and whether the reviewer can verify the output.

Three research pillars

The cognitive-science literature on task decomposition rests on three findings, each pointing at a different mechanism. The citations supplied as starting material are listed in the Sources section below with verification status; the mechanism each names is the load-bearing content here, not the specific citation.

1. Resource-rational decomposition — minimize planning runtime

Humans decompose tasks to reduce the computational cost of planning, not because larger plans are intrinsically harder, but because the cost of figuring out what to do next dominates as plan size grows. Decomposition trades one expensive global planning step for several cheaper local ones (Correa et al., 2020 / 2023).

LLM analog. Long context dilutes attention (position bias / lost-in-the-middle); state-carry across many concerns hits effective working-memory limits even when the nominal context window is large. A 200K-token model can still execute a 4K plan poorly if the plan demands holding three independent mental models simultaneously. The binding constraint is concurrent concerns, not token count.

2. Small victories — early wins build verification anchors

In behavioral-economics framings of motivation, ordering subtasks from easiest to hardest produces early “small victories” that build momentum and reduce the probability of abandonment (Brown & Lahey, 2014 — the specific effect is over task size, smallest-to-largest, not over difficulty per se). For human executors this is largely a motivation effect.

LLM analog. No motivation curve, but a structurally equivalent design move: each verification checkpoint converts a “maybe-correct” intermediate state into a confirmed-correct anchor. A 5-step plan executed sequentially with verification at each step produces 5 anchors, any of which can be a recovery point if later steps fail. The same work compressed into one monolithic prompt produces a single output whose correctness is all-or-nothing. The mechanism differs (anchor stability vs. motivation), but the design move (verification at every subgoal) is identical.

The easiest-to-hardest ordering itself matters less for LLMs (no fatigue curve) and more for information dependency — earliest subtasks should establish the state later subtasks depend on, so each step can be specified concretely rather than in terms of “whatever the previous step decides.”

3. Concrete subgoals — falsifiable done-states build self-efficacy

In goal-setting theory, abstract long-term goals feel distant and intimidating; concrete subgoals provide proximal markers of accomplishment, building self-efficacy and reducing procrastination (Locke & Latham, 1990, on goal-setting; Bandura, 1977, on self-efficacy). Each subgoal needs a clear done-state to function as a marker.

LLM analog. Each subtask in a plan must have a falsifiable done-state — what observation indicates this step succeeded? Vague subtasks (“refactor for clarity,” “improve error handling”) fail this test; concrete subtasks (“rename X to Y across files A, B, C; tests still pass”) pass it. This is the direct bridge to falsifiability-claim-fingerprints: a well-decomposed plan is a sequence of operationalized done-states.

The self-efficacy mechanism has no LLM analog (no belief-in-own-ability variable), but the operationalization requirement is sharper for LLMs: an agent given a vague subtask will execute against its training-data prior for that phrase rather than against your actual intent.

Structural signals — when a plan needs splitting

The right plan size is not a length measure. A 300-line plan touching one concern often executes better than a 60-line plan spanning three. The load-bearing signals are structural.

Signal 1 — Concern count

Number of distinct mental models the executor must hold simultaneously. Auth + DB schema + frontend routing = three concerns. Pure CSS work on one component = one concern.

Threshold heuristic. More than two simultaneous concerns is a split candidate. Each additional concern multiplies the state the executor must keep coherent across the plan.

Signal 2 — State-carry depth

How far back later steps reference earlier decisions. A plan where step 10 must recall a naming choice made in step 1 has deep state-carry; a plan where each step is locally complete has shallow state-carry.

Threshold heuristic. If any step requires verbatim recall of a decision made more than ~3 steps earlier, the state-carry is too long. Either re-state the decision at the point of use, or split before the recall.

Signal 3 — Decision branches

If/then forks that can’t be resolved up front. “If the existing migration works, modify it; otherwise rewrite” is a branch the executor cannot collapse without doing the check first.

Threshold heuristic. Each unresolved branch is its own plan. A plan with N independent branch-points is structurally 2^N plans masquerading as one. Three valid resolutions, in order of preference: (a) resolve the branch up front before issuing the plan; (b) split into separate plans at the branch point; (c) express as an implementation intention — a deterministic if-then with an observable trigger and a named response. Reach for (c) only when the trigger genuinely cannot be evaluated up front and the two downstream paths are similar enough in scope that splitting would duplicate setup; otherwise (a) and (b) are the defaults. “We’ll figure it out when we get there” is none of the three.

Signal 4 — Verification seams

Places where a human reviewer would want to pause and confirm before proceeding — typically after a schema change, before a destructive operation, or where downstream steps depend on a non-obvious intermediate state.

Threshold heuristic. Every verification seam is a natural split candidate. If you’d want to manually check before continuing, the executor should also stop and report there.

Signal 5 — Reversibility boundary

Destructive or irreversible operations are natural stopping points. Putting speculative work and destructive work in the same plan is the failure mode where exploratory edits accidentally land in production state.

Threshold heuristic. Never put a destructive step after speculative work in the same plan. Split immediately before the destructive step, with explicit confirmation as the first action of the next plan.

The split-or-consolidate heuristic

Combining the signals:

  • Concern count > 2 and state-carry shallow across concerns → split by concern boundary
  • Decision branches present and branching decision not resolvable up front → split per branch
  • Verification seam between unrelated subtasks → split at the seam
  • Destructive step preceded by speculative work → split before the destructive step

Conversely, do not split when:

  • State-carry is high across the candidate boundary — the split forces re-establishing context, paying the setup cost twice
  • Tight coupling — step B is only meaningful given step A’s specific output, and that output cannot be summarized without losing the structure step B operates on
  • One verification covers all steps — splitting adds overhead without buying anchor stability
  • Plan is short enough that switching cost dominates coherence cost — for plans under ~5 steps with one concern, monolithic execution is usually correct

Over-decomposition — the symmetric failure mode

The research on small victories and concrete subgoals creates a one-directional bias in popular advice (“always break it down further”). The symmetric failure mode is real: tiny subtasks accumulate switching overhead, dilute the larger goal, and produce a sequence of locally-correct outputs that don’t compose into the intended whole.

The motivational-psychology research itself contains the counter: Höchli, Brügger & Messner (2018) argue that superordinate goals — abstract, identity-based, long-horizon — are crucial motivators alongside concrete subgoals. Over-focus on subgoal granularity at the expense of the larger purpose loses motivational coherence, complicating the goal-setting-theory framing in pillar 3.

Signs of over-decomposition:

  • Each subtask requires re-stating the same shared context (high duplication across plans)
  • Plans become outlines of outlines — the unit of execution is smaller than the unit of decision-making
  • The reviewer can’t tell from the subtask whether it’s on-strategy without reconstructing the larger plan
  • Total token cost (including re-setup) exceeds the original monolithic plan

The corrective: subtasks should be sized to the unit at which a decision changes the plan. If two adjacent subtasks always succeed or fail together, they’re one subtask.

Goal-architecture safeguards — equifinality and cross-context compensation

The five signals above address plan structure. Two additional failure modes emerge from how the goal architecture is layered — and a well-split plan can still fail if these safeguards are missing. The vocabulary comes from Höchli, Brügger & Messner (2018), whose paper complicates the pure-subgoals story from pillar 3 in two specific ways.

Equifinality — multiple paths to the same end

A superordinate goal (“the migration is safe in production”) admits many subordinate paths (canary rollout, blue/green, dual-write, …). When the current subordinate path fails or hits an unexpected blocker, equifinality is the property that lets the planner pivot to an alternative path without abandoning the goal. The superordinate goal acts as an anchor; the subordinate paths are interchangeable means.

A plan structured only as a sequence of subordinate steps loses this property silently: when step 5 blocks, the plan looks dead because no alternative is visible. The fix is stating the superordinate purpose at each split point so the executor (or the planner on resume) can navigate to an alternative path without rebuilding the goal-architecture from scratch.

The applied move: at each split boundary, the next plan’s preamble names the superordinate purpose it serves, not just the local subordinate task. “Plan B: deploy the migration” is fragile; “Plan B: deploy the migration as one path toward making the migration safe in production; if blocked, the alternative paths are dual-write rollout or canary” preserves equifinality at the boundary.

Cross-context compensation — local victories licensing later slack

In human self-regulation, pure-subgoal architectures produce a known failure mode: completing a hard subordinate task licenses reduced effort on subsequent tasks (“I had a productive morning, so I can disengage this afternoon”). The local victory is the entire goal; cumulative progress decouples from the superordinate purpose.

LLMs have no motivation curve, but the structural analog is real and applies to mixed human + AI execution: a plan that surfaces only subordinate steps can be executed locally-correctly while drifting from the original purpose. Each step’s done-state is met; the cumulative output doesn’t add up to the superordinate intent. The human reviewer accepts each subtask’s output and the drift accumulates unobserved.

The corrective is the same as for equifinality: maintain visible superordinate purpose alongside subordinate steps. At verification seams, the question is not just “did this step succeed?” but “is this step contributing to the superordinate goal, or is it now optimizing for something else?” Without the second question, cross-context drift is invisible.

The composite design move

Both safeguards point to the same architectural principle: the goal architecture should be dual-layered, not single-layered. Subordinate steps without superordinate anchoring are vulnerable to both failure modes; superordinate goals without subordinate operationalization are vulnerable to procrastination and abstract paralysis. The pair is the design unit.

For plans about to be issued to an agent: name the superordinate purpose in the preamble and refer back to it at verification seams and split boundaries. For plans that span sessions or hand off across agents, the superordinate statement is what survives the handoff; without it, each new session reconstructs the local plan and loses the larger purpose.

Audit template — plan-split scan

This template operationalizes the structural signals as a checklist. Use as a prompt for the model to audit a draft plan, or as a manual checklist before issuing a plan to an agent.

Audit the following plan for split-or-consolidate signals.

For each finding, give: (a) which signal, (b) location (step number or
quoted span), (c) one-line reason, (d) recommended action: split at
boundary X (naming the bottleneck), consolidate steps Y-Z, resolve
branch up front, convert to implementation intention, lift setup, or
leave alone.

Structural signals:
1. Concern count — more than two distinct mental models held
   simultaneously.
2. State-carry depth — later steps requiring verbatim recall of a
   decision made more than ~3 steps earlier.
3. Decision branches — if/then forks that cannot be resolved before
   the executor starts.
4. Verification seams — points where a human would pause to check
   before continuing.
5. Reversibility boundary — destructive step preceded by speculative
   or exploratory work in the same plan.

Also check for the symmetric failure (over-decomposition):
- Are adjacent subtasks always succeed-or-fail together? → consolidate.
- Does each subtask re-state the same shared context? → consolidate.
- Is the subtask smaller than the unit at which a decision changes
  the plan? → consolidate.

Cap at 8 findings. Rank by severity. Note explicitly what you checked
and didn't find. Output a single split-or-consolidate verdict plus
recommended split points.

Tradeoffs and context-dependence

  • Plan size is executor-dependent. A senior developer with deep project context can execute a larger plan than a fresh agent. A vault-aware Claude Code session can execute a larger plan than a stateless API call. The right size shifts with the executor; a plan-split-scan should ideally take executor capacity as input rather than applying a fixed threshold.
  • The signals are heuristic, not measured. “Concern count > 2” and “state-carry > 3 steps” are operational rules of thumb extracted from prompt-engineering practice, not numbers backed by an experiment on AI plan execution. Treat them as starting calibration; adjust against your own failure data.
  • The verification-seam signal overlaps with falsifiability-claim-fingerprints — both name the same move (each subtask has a quotable done-state) from different angles. Verification seam is the plan-structure framing; falsifiability is the claim-shape framing.

What this doesn’t cover

  • Cross-session plan continuity — how to hand off a long plan across multiple sessions or agents. The commitments-log infrastructure is the persistence side; the plan-decomposition side of session handoff is unaddressed here.
  • Plan generation by AI itself. This note assumes the plan exists. An LLM-drafted plan has its own failure modes — over-decomposition is more common in AI-generated plans than human-generated ones, in informal observation. A separate analysis of AI plan-drafting would be its own note.
  • Quantitative measurement of plan-execution success. The signals here predict difficulty; they don’t predict success rate. A study correlating structural-signal scores with execution success would convert this from heuristic to empirical.

Counterargument

The decomposition framing risks an availability-driven over-application. Many short, simple plans do not need a split scan; running the audit on them is itself a form of over-decomposition. The honest position:

  • For plans under ~5 steps with one concern, default to monolithic execution.
  • For plans with any destructive operation, irreversible step, or unresolved branch, always scan regardless of length.
  • For long, single-concern plans, the bottleneck is usually density (position bias, compression trap), not decomposition.

The plan-split scan is most useful at the medium-complexity layer where neither default applies — multi-concern plans of 5-20 steps with mixed reversibility. This is plausibly where agent failures concentrate in informal observation, but the distribution has not been measured, so the leverage is conjectural — the tool is not universal.
