Agentic SDLC — Team Topology, Roles, Skills, Size

Drafted autonomously by Claude on 2026-11-01 under D-008; not yet reviewed by oxzo.

This is Plan 08’s deliverable in the agentic SDLC program. It is scored against agentic-sdlc-evaluation-criteria and reconciles the team-shape sections of the phase notes from Plan 02 to Plan 07. Evidence ids (E-NNN) point into projects/agentic-sdlc/evidence-ledger. Steps 1 to 3 were done in a first session and are kept, with every source citation, in projects/agentic-sdlc/extracts/08-s1-classic-evidence-refclass.md; this note summarises them and adds steps 4 to 7. Decisions are D-056 to D-063 in projects/agentic-sdlc/direction-log.

Facts are as of 2026-11-01. Firm headcounts and hiring policies carry their own dates.

Terms.

  • A role is a set of duties with an artifact and a gate. It is not a person (criteria note: “A role is not a person”). One person can hold several roles unless a rule below says they must be different people.
  • The directing engineer runs an agent session and answers for the change it produces. The producer is the agent, its session and any agent sharing its context (G1). Under that definition the directing engineer is not a producer, but is barred from approving the change (Plan 04).
  • Tier 0, 1 and 2 are Plan 05’s approval tiers: no human approval per change (a policy-admitted class of non-executable content, D-072), one approver, and two approvers with one from outside the producing team. Regime classes A to D are Plan 07’s: A is DORA’s full framework, B is an internal standard requiring a human approval per change (and SLSA L4), C is approval by authorised parties without stated independence (PCI DSS, NIST, DORA’s simplified framework, ISO provisionally), D is SOC 2-only once its system description has been read, or none.
  • An owning team holds one or more services or areas for their whole support period. In Team Topologies’ vocabulary it is a stream-aligned team.

The answer in seven lines

  1. No evidence says enterprise teams get smaller with agents, so this note makes no “smaller teams” claim; the outside view points to relocation, on a condition nobody has measured (Plan 08 pivot trigger 1 fires at function level only, D-057). No study measures enterprise team size, or reviewer and approver headcount, against agent adoption. The one A-grade size result is for startups, and there the AI tools named in job postings do not predict the smaller size (E-559). In the earlier shifts with data, total headcount in the function held or rose during the shift, while the routine slice and the entry rung shrank over a decade or more (E-584 to E-586). Every “held or rose” case ran through demand absorbing the gain. Where it did not, a role shrank and stayed down: the one team-level case (tellers per branch) was offset by more branches, until teller jobs fell 46% (E-585), and telephone operators largely vanished.
  2. Ten roles and two duties, not thirty-three roles (D-059). The phase notes named 33 roles. They consolidate into ten roles plus two duties, approving and sponsoring an agent identity, which other role-holders carry and which are not jobs. “Platform owner”, loaded by five plans, becomes one platform team. Plan 02’s “compliance or contract owner” splits into Plan 03’s contract owner and Plan 07’s compliance owner.
  3. Size the human work agents do not remove, not the number of agent runs (D-060). Per change, a team’s human hours are directing, the checks for new behaviour that the producer may not write, and review times the number of human approvals; per deployment, the release approvals; plus standing duties. The evidence says agents raise volume per engineer (E-560, E-573). No source measures which of those per-change terms they cut. The checks and the approvals are human work the rules require on every agent change, and the regime class sets how many approvals. So the candidate dominant variable is human verification work per change (checks plus review times approvals), and it dominates only if directing hours per change fall below it, which is unmeasured. Where demand stays flat, a team shrinks to a floor set by its rules, its on-call rota and the theory it must hold. This is the drafter’s model; no parameter in it is measured.
  4. The rules set a floor of distinct people, not a size (D-058). A tier-1 change needs two people. A tier-2 change or an irreversible release needs three, one from outside the producing team. Under MAS or APRA, the person who merges is never the directing engineer. Classes A and B add a release-owner approval per deployment, and classes A to C a compliance owner outside the delivery teams. So an organisation with a tier-2 path needs a second team or an external approver pool. An on-call rota with a second human for non-reversible writes sets a higher practical floor that no source sizes. The phase notes’ small-team collapses (several roles held by one person) are allowed only where no rule separates them, and the table in step 4 says where that is.
  5. The skill the lifecycle depends on, judging agent output, is the one agents erode if the agent does the learner’s thinking (D-061). Randomised studies split on who does the cognitive work: unguarded AI help lowered later unaided performance, and tutor-style use kept the gains (E-219, E-563). AI-aided output cannot show who is still building skill (E-565). In the earlier shifts with data on it, the entry rung went first (CAD and offshoring, E-586, E-579), and AI-era data show the same pattern (E-552). So the lifecycle keeps a junior intake as a decision with a mentor-time budget, a learner mode and AI-free assessment. The design is untested.
  6. The lifecycle runs with people who use agents little, because its gates are at the forge and platform, not in the method (Plan 04). What is likely to break is review: a low-fluency reviewer facing agent-rate intake either stalls the queue or waves changes through. That is the drafter’s inference; the nearest evidence is that small PRs were approved without comment most often (E-028, pre-agent). The evidence on moving low users is one B source: team-specific demos and protected learning time help, and productivity pressure without learning time stalls learning (E-575).
  7. Plan 08’s own gate is the separation floor, computed by the forge from identities. Plan 08 adds no new gate. Every role in the table answers for an artifact that an existing gate protects.

Step 1 — The classic answers, restated for agents

The classic rules held for three reasons: communication overhead between producers, cognitive load per team, and ownership boundaries (working file, step 1). Each still binds, at a different place:

  • Communication overhead is now producer-to-reviewer and producer-to-owner, not producer-to-producer. Per-member output falls as a human team grows, through editing others’ code (E-544, A, pre-agent). With agents producing, the bottleneck firms report is human attention, mostly review: Google slowed AI migrations to the speed of the reviewing teams (E-465), Anthropic’s agent sessions sit about 80% idle waiting for a human, and its meeting time rose 20% (E-573, B). Brooks’s sequential terms, debugging and verification, are the ones agents leave to people (E-543).
  • Cognitive load per team has two terms: what the team owns and how fast it changes. Team Topologies caps scope by cognitive load and sizes teams at five to nine on social-group grounds (E-546, C). Agents probably lower the routine load, though no source measures which per-change terms they cut (D-064), and they raise the rate of change the team must understand. The size range is about human trust, so agents are not counted in it.
  • Ownership is the scarcest thing to assign. Google’s one-reviewer norm rested on the author usually holding ownership or readability (E-549, A). An agent author holds neither, so on the drafter’s reading every agent change needs an owner who does. Every gate in Plans 02 to 07 names one.
  • Social loafing returns when the agent wrote a change and someone approved it, unless one human answers for each change (E-550, A). That is G3.

No classic gives a team size backed by software evidence, and every test predates agents.

Step 3 — The outside view, and pivot trigger 1

A fresh-context /reference-class run took the five forward size claims in the record and the earlier shifts in extract 08-r4. Its verdict was outside-view treatment needed (working file, step 3). The working file’s table has nine shifts: the eight the drill used, plus telephone operators, added at review as a shrinking case.

  • Total headcount in the function, during the shift: four rose and three held; none of the seven with in-window data fell (compilers, offshoring’s onshore IT, DevOps-era computer occupations, low-code-era developers rose; ATMs, CAD, national QA held). Spreadsheets have only C-grade in-window data. All are confounded with demand growth.
  • Routine sub-occupations, a decade or more later: five fell 32–47% over 14–22 years (programmers, sysadmins, clerks, tellers, drafters by one survey), and the sources do not settle the cause: BLS blamed offshoring for the programmer decline in 2009 and AI in its 2025–35 handbook, and CAD-era firms blamed business conditions (E-579, E-584, E-586; clerks from extract 08-r4 §4.16).
  • Per-team headcount: one pre-agent case, and it is a reduction. Tellers per urban branch fell from 20 to 13 while branches rose 43% (E-585, B). The offset was a phase: teller employment then fell 46% from 2011 to 2025, cause not established (E-584, E-585).
  • A role that shrank and did not come back: telephone operators under dial automation, with the work moving to other occupations (working file, step 3 table; E-585’s note).
  • Claimed task gains far exceeded realised labour savings wherever both were seen. CAD raised output per draughtsman 200–500% on average, with labour savings “nowhere near so high” because drawing was a minority of the job (E-586).
  • The bottom rung went first (E-586, E-579), and entry requirements rose (E-586).

Pivot trigger 1 fires at function level and not at team level (D-057). Its condition is that the drill “shows prior shifts did not reduce team size but relocated it”. For total headcount in the function, it does. For team size, the one case is a reduction offset for a time by more units. The trigger’s consequence, dropping any “smaller teams” claim, is applied, and it would be applied anyway: no A-grade or two independent B-grade sources support smaller enterprise teams, which is the master plan’s bar. What the trigger also licenses, a stated relocation, is written below as conditional on demand, because the outside view cannot carry it further.

The relocation, stated with its condition. If demand for enterprise software absorbs agent output, as it absorbed the gain in every “held or rose” case: headcount leaves the routine slice of production (translating requirements into tickets, boilerplate code, manual test execution, change-board meetings) and, unless the organisation protects it, the entry rung. The directing engineers absorb that slice’s work and hold or grow with demand. Small platform, verification and compliance roles appear around the agents. Human verification hours grow with the number of changes wherever the regime class puts a human on each change. If demand does not absorb it, teams shrink toward the floor in step 5, as tellers did once branch growth stopped. Neither branch is tested for software, and nobody has measured the demand response.

What this replaces. The release note’s “Plan 08 should size reviewers and owners, not producers” holds as a sizing rule only if “producers” means agent runs: size on the human work, not on how many agent sessions run (step 5). As a forecast about people it fails. The drill found the human role that took up the tool grew in every software case (E-584), and in this lifecycle the directing engineers are most of the reviewers and owners. The phase plans’ inside view, as the drill paraphrased it, that headcount moves away from producers is withdrawn.

What would reverse it. An enterprise study of team or reviewer headcount against agent adoption, or a measure of whether demand for enterprise software absorbs agent-scale output. Agents also span more of the job than drawing or cash handling did, so CAD’s brake, that drawing was a minority of the job (E-586), may be weaker. That is a hypothesis, not evidence.

Step 5 — Size as a function

The brief asks for size as a function of codebase size, regulatory profile, legacy share and verification cost per item. No parameter below is measured, so the function is the drafter’s model, labelled untested. It is an accounting identity: it is true for any team, and its content is which terms agents change and which the rules fix (D-060).

Per owning team, per week:

human hours needed = changes × (directing + checks + review per approval × human approvals) + deployments × release approvals + standing duties

people = human hours needed ÷ usable hours per person, and never below the floor

  • Changes is demand: what the team is asked to ship. Plan 05’s intake cap limits it to what the team’s people can verify, so the function runs two ways. Given demand, it says how many people are needed. Given the people, it says what intake to cap at. Which way an organisation runs it is a policy choice, not an output.
  • Directing is cutting the unit, writing the brief and steering the agent. This is the term agents are expected to cut, but the evidence is volume per engineer, not hours per change: merged PRs up 24% at Microsoft (E-560), commits fivefold at Anthropic (E-573). Anthropic also reports meta-work, which includes writing specs, rising from 10% to 22% of sessions (E-573), so part of directing grew.
  • Checks is the human check work for new behaviour, which the producer may not supply: the requirement owner’s held-out acceptance check, ratifying tests added in the change, and the check owner’s approval of any check-file edit (verification note). Agents do not remove this term.
  • Review per approval is verification cost per item. Nothing read says agents cut it. When the author cannot explain the reasoning, review gets harder (review-capacity note). Review effort concentrates in a predictable tail of PRs (extract 08-r2 §2.15), which supports Plan 05’s triage over staffing. One vendor dataset points the other way: review minutes grew 3.1× against 4.2× output, so per unit of output review time fell (review-capacity note, vendor, C). That can mean cheaper review or thinner review. The review-capacity note’s wider reading is that where review can be routed around, it is, and assurance inputs fall silently. In this lifecycle approvals cannot be bypassed, so whether per-change review time falls is unmeasured.
  • Human approvals per change come from the tier mix: zero at tier 0 (non-executable content only, D-072: class D, and class C’s expressly preapproved types, not under PCI or DORA’s simplified framework without an assessor, D-049), one at tier 1, two at tier 2, and a second person to merge under MAS or APRA. Classes A and B allow no tier 0.
  • Release approvals per deployment are the release owner’s approval of each deployment in classes A and B and the two irreversible-class approvals. A deployment can carry many changes, so this term scales with deployments, not changes.
  • Standing duties are owning the requirement baseline, the rule set and contracts, the release policy, the on-call rota, and keeping the theory of the service.

Across the organisation: number of owning teams = what must be owned ÷ what one team can hold. What one team can hold falls with the rate of change and with legacy share. Legacy code without an oracle needs Plan 06’s legacy-behaviour oracle written before agents change it. The argument that a service’s theory is held only by people rests on Naur (E-491, C); Lehman’s laws (E-444, B) support continual adaptation and growing complexity, and his familiarity law was not tested. So the theory-holding floor rests on a C source alone and is labelled untested. Add one platform team, a small verification function, and a compliance owner per regime scope in classes A to C.

Which variable dominates, and on what condition.

  • Where demand rises with output: the human verification work per change, checks plus review times approvals, is the candidate dominant term. It binds if this lifecycle’s gates stop work routing around review (D-065), which is the pressure valve the review-capacity note found. It is human work by rule, and the regime class multiplies the review part. It dominates if directing hours per change fall below it. That is unmeasured: the strongest support for review being the bottleneck is Google’s AI migrations, where directing cost is low by design (E-465), and Anthropic’s idle-waiting figure counts waiting on any human input, not review alone (E-573). If the condition holds, class A and B teams’ hours scale almost linearly with change count, since every change carries an independent human approval, while a class D team with a proven tier-0 class can move part of its intake to zero approvals.
  • Where demand stays flat: the floor dominates. Directing hours fall, and the team shrinks to the largest of the distinct-human floor for its paths, the on-call rota, and the people needed to hold the theory of what it owns. The one team-level precedent was offset by more branches for a time and then was not (E-585).
  • Codebase size and legacy share set the number of teams, not their size.

What the rules fix (the floor, checked against D-048, D-049 and D-052).

Tier 0 (non-executable content, D-072; class D, or class C’s preapproved types)1: the directing engineer merges under the class owner’s policy
Tier 12: the directing engineer plus an approver who is neither requester, producer, directing engineer nor pusher
Tier 23, at least one from outside the producing team

In class D the tier-1 and tier-2 approvals are the program’s default, not a regime requirement (Plan 07’s governance table); in classes A to C they are required.

The rules give no team size. They give two people per owning team for tier 1, an outside pool for tier 2, and three distinct people wherever an irreversible class exists. A two-person team with a second human required for non-reversible ops writes has its whole headcount on call, so the on-call rota sets a higher practical floor. No source sizes it.

“Size reviewers”, “scoped, not staffed up” and “pools sized by class”: a partition, not a reconciliation (D-060). The defect null behind “do not add reviewers” (E-007) concerns defect yield. No approval in this lifecycle rests on defect yield: tier-1 and tier-2 approvals rest on G1 and G3 in every class, and on regime text in A and B. So the null lowers approver hours nowhere. What differs by class is tier 0 and the release-owner approvals. This note’s reading:

  • Everywhere, the approver duty is held by existing owners and by another team’s engineers for tier 2, not by a separately staffed review team, and Plan 05’s per-item cost cuts and triage apply.
  • In classes C and D, a proven tier-0 class takes changes out of the approval term, and Plan 05’s “scoped, not staffed up” holds with intake capped to owner hours. Checkpoint (D-072): tier 0 now holds only copy and translations under the escape-rate condition, so this takes little out of the approval term. In classes C and D the approval term is close to one approval per change, and the C/D side of this partition is re-read in that light (D-080).
  • In classes A and B, there is no tier 0, so approver hours are a floor per change that grows with change count. Plan 05’s “not staffed up” is overridden there: if intake grows, owner hours for approval must grow with it.
  • Untested on either side: review may also be how a service’s theory spreads (review-capacity note; extract 08-r0 §0.17), which would argue for review hours in every class. No evidence either way.

What the team-size range stays. Team Topologies’ five to nine (E-546, C) is kept as the upper bound for a trusting human team, with agents not counted, and labelled untested: it comes from social-group research, not software. Its lower bound of five is not kept. This note’s lower bound is the rule-derived floor above plus the on-call rota.

Step 6 — Skills and the junior rung

What the lifecycle needs people to be able to do. Every role in step 4 rests on judgement: cutting a unit, reading a diff against intent, saying no to a change, holding the theory of a service. Plan 04 found review and unit-cutting judgement to be the scarce inputs, “whoever runs the agent”. The criteria note’s V1 requires that people “keep the ability to judge and own the output, and grow it”.

What erodes it, on the evidence.

  • When the AI does the learner’s cognitive work, later unaided performance falls. Learning a library with AI cut comprehension 17% (E-219, A, n=52). Unguarded GPT-4 raised assisted maths grades and cut later unassisted grades 17%, and a tutor configuration largely removed the harm (E-563, A).
  • Practice that substitutes AI for the work predicts smaller skill gains, and “organizations screening on that output cannot tell them apart” (E-565, B; the telemetry half is A provisional, abstract only).
  • Experts lose skill too: endoscopists’ unaided detection fell within three months of AI arriving (E-564, A, medicine, observational).
  • The supervision channel thins. Copilot users shifted away from project-management activity and collaborative work, which the authors read as less help-seeking (E-558, A, vendor co-authored); in Lambert and Schindler’s model juniors consume senior supervision time, so anything that raises its cost pushes firms toward seniors (E-554).
  • Peripheral contributors merge agent PRs without passing CI more often than core contributors (E-561, A), which fits judgement over agent output tracking experience in a project (project tenure, not career seniority).

None of this is measured for professional programmers over a career. No 2026 replication of E-219 for programmers was found.

The junior rung (drafter’s design, untested). The class says the bottom rung goes first (E-586, E-579), the AI-era data match (E-551, E-552), and no organisation has shown a working replacement. So the lifecycle treats junior intake as a decision, not a by-product:

  • Intake with a mentor budget. Each owning team that takes juniors budgets senior hours for them. Of the two firms expanding intern intake, Shopify names senior engineers’ time as the bottleneck and Cloudflare calls mentors essential (E-567, E-568).
  • A learner mode. A junior’s agent runs in a tutor configuration on designated learning units: it explains and asks rather than writing the change (the E-563 guarded arm). Juniors direct normal tier-1 work too, always with a senior approver, which the tier rules already require.
  • AI-free reps and assessment. Progression is judged on work done without the agent, because AI-aided output cannot show skill (E-565). Shopify tests candidates with no AI, optional AI and mandatory AI, and says nobody yet knows whether high scorers perform well (extract 08-r3 §3.3).
  • Review as apprenticeship. Juniors read and explain changes as a second reader before they are approvers of record.
  • Seniors and the team keep hands-on reps. The practitioner sources name four practices for keeping skill: deliberate no-AI practice (Anthropic engineers), “explain the layer two or three below” (Shopify), craft leaders kept as a separate line (LinkedIn), and senior coaching paired with agent onboarding (a Swedish telecom). None is measured (extract 08-r3, T-skill summary). The lifecycle adds one of its own, untested: on-call diagnosis is practised without the agent, since that is when the agent’s reading of the service may be wrong.

What would change this. A firm reporting outcomes (retention, time to approver, escaped defects) for a deliberate junior design, or a career-length study of skill under agents. Until then this section is a design, not a finding.

Step 7 — Adoption with mixed fluency

What does not break. The gates are at the forge and platform, so a person who uses agents little cannot weaken them. Human-written changes go through the same tiers. Plan 04’s stance holds: the fluency spread is a design input, and the boundary is set at the platform for everyone.

What breaks when people use agents little.

  • Review. A low-fluency reviewer facing agent-rate intake either stalls the queue or waves changes through. Small PRs were approved without comment most often (E-028). This is the failure the drafter expects to matter most (an inference, untested), and it sits on the term step 5 names as the candidate to dominate.
  • The intake cap. A cap set from the fastest directors’ throughput overloads the team’s slower reviewers.
  • The expectation gradient. In a mandated, appraisal-linked rollout, expected gains grew “with hierarchical distance from the code” (E-576, B). Productivity pressure without learning time stops people learning the tools (E-575, B).
  • Theory concentration. If a few heavy users produce most changes, the theory may concentrate in them, and the service loses it when they leave. This is the drafter’s inference. No source measures it.

What helps, on thin evidence. Team-specific demos of the team’s own tasks, champions, and protected learning time (E-575, one B). Skip-level peers’ use, which was the strongest predictor of adoption at Microsoft (E-560). Agent work made public by construction, so colleagues learn from transcripts (E-567, B). No outcome comparison of mandated and voluntary adoption exists (E-576). The lifecycle does not tie appraisal to agent use, and does not size anyone on perceived speed-up (Plan 04, E-337).

The designer case. The vault’s one record of a stalled adoption is a single-vantage account, with an undefeated rival explanation that the tools were built for programmers first (design-side-ai-adoption-stall). Read as adoption: designers do not need to direct agents to run this lifecycle. Their artifacts of record are the token file, the registry and accepted baselines (Plan 03), and the design engineer is the handoff role. The onramp in design-side-ai-onramp, which separates AI use from developer tooling, fits this, and is not validated.

The eight questions (the subset that applies)

1. Does the phase survive? — skipped

Team topology is not a phase. Plan 08 does not feed program trigger 2’s count.

2. Who does the work?

Step 4’s table. Agents produce; directing engineers in owning teams direct and answer; owners approve; a platform team, a small verification function, a compliance owner and a security function sit outside the owning teams.

3. Artifact of record — skipped

Plan 08 adds none. Each role answers for an artifact another plan defined.

4. The gate

No new gate. The separation floor in step 5 is enforced by existing gates: approver distinctness computed from F-sourced identities (D-048), tier-2 outside membership at approval time, and dual authorisation by distinct persons (D-052).

5. Failure modes

  • The approver becomes a rubber stamp when review hours do not grow with change count (E-028). Criteria V2 asks the synthesis to name any role designed this way. The approver duty is at risk of it in classes A and B whenever intake is not capped.
  • Owners hold too much. One person as requirement owner, area technical owner and release owner across several services loses the theory of some of them.
  • The entry rung disappears silently, through hiring that does not happen (E-552, E-570), with no line item to notice it.
  • The platform team becomes the bottleneck, since every envelope, lock and credential change is a tier-2 check file it owns.
  • Collapse past the rules. A small organisation lets the directing engineer administer the substrate their sessions run on in class A to C (step 4 table).
  • Enterprise-specific: approver pools drawn from a central group that knows none of the services, so “outside the producing team” becomes outside any understanding of the change.

6. Team shape implications

This note is the answer.

7. What would change this

  • An enterprise study of team, reviewer or approver headcount against agent adoption. It replaces the outside view in step 3.
  • A measured review cost per change under agents, in hours, with a comparison to human-authored changes. It turns step 5 into numbers.
  • A study comparing AI review with tiered human review on the same changes by escaped defects (Plan 05’s trigger). If AI catches as much, the approver term falls outside classes A and B.
  • A supervisor admitting tier 0 in class A (Plan 07). The approver floor there falls.
  • Evidence that demand for enterprise software does not absorb agent output. Then teams shrink to the floor, and the relocation reads as reduction.
  • Outcomes for a junior-rung design. Step 6 becomes a finding or is dropped.

8. Best guess and alternatives

Best guess.

  • Owning teams (stream-aligned) per service or area, each holding requirement ownership, area technical ownership, release ownership and on-call for its services through their support period, sized by step 5 and never below the separation floor and the on-call rota. Five to nine people is kept as the upper bound, untested (E-546, C).
  • One platform team owning every boundary control from Plans 03 to 07.
  • A small verification function owning locks, held-out suites and tier policy, with per-change check work (acceptance checks, ratification) inside owning teams.
  • A compliance owner per regime scope outside the delivery teams in classes A to C, and a security function.
  • Size the human side on the human work per change (directing, checks, review times approvals) and per deployment, and cap intake to it. Do not size on the number of agent runs or on perceived speed-up.
  • Junior intake kept as a decision with mentor hours, a learner mode and AI-free assessment.

The headcount direction is relocation if demand absorbs agent output, and shrinkage toward the floor if it does not. The outside view (A and B sources on earlier shifts) supports the first branch only through a demand response nobody has measured for software. The sizing function and which term dominates, the role consolidation, the theory-holding floor (C only) and the junior design are untested: no A or B source measures any of them under agents. The pilot is told.

Alternatives and when they win.

  • One small team, everyone holds every role. Wins in class D with no tier-2 path, which is not enterprise software as Plan 00 defines it. With a tier-2 path it needs an external approver pool.
  • StrongDM’s shape: no human review, humans own held-out scenarios (E-574). Wins only in class D, only for a class with Plan 05’s own-history escape condition met. It is never a separation of duties (D-048).
  • A central review team instead of owners approving. Wins where approvals must be independent of the delivery organisation and the reviewers can hold enough context. It loses the theory channel, and no source shows a central team keeping up with agent-rate intake.
  • No junior intake. Cheaper now. It wins only if the organisation can keep hiring its future seniors from outside. Whether that market holds when many firms cut their entry rung at once is the drafter’s concern, not a finding; the outside view shows only that the bottom rung goes first (E-586, E-579).

Handoffs this plan does not settle

  • On-call rota size under ops agents. No source either way (Plan 06).
  • Reviewer hours per change, and the usable review share of a week. Plan 09 owns the cost and Plan 10 should measure both.
  • Whether demand absorbs agent output. The hinge of step 3, unmeasured.
  • Whether theory concentrates in heavy agent users. Step 7 inference.
  • Plan 09 inherits the step 5 function as its cost model’s labour side.

Findings against the vault’s own notes

Recorded as findings, not rewrites, with one line in each note linking here (D-062).

  • skill-valuation-in-the-ai-age: the “roughly 20%” junior decline was never read at source. The current Canaries revision (August 2026) reports a 19% descriptive gap for 22–25-year-olds in exposed occupations; the 13% and 16% were earlier regression estimates (E-551).
  • ai-job-displacement-timescales-mechanisms: the “−16% relative” is one revision’s estimate; the August 2026 revision reports 19% (E-551). Cite the version.
  • review-capacity-org-design: “Do not add reviewers” rests on defect nulls (E-007). The program’s approvers in classes A and B rest on separation of duties, so the null does not lower approver hours there.
  • agentic-sdlc-release-operate-phase: “size reviewers and owners, not producers” holds as a sizing rule and fails as a forecast (step 3).
  • design-side-ai-adoption-stall: its “What this trace did not do” section says it ran no forward trace and no adversarial pass, but the note contains both; and “assumption 5 below” sits above.
  • Plan 08’s brief said the 45% rule and rubber-stamp problem in multi-agent-diminishing-returns “transfer to human teams”. The note does not claim that, and the program had already declined its numbers. The premise is dropped.

What the pilot must exercise

  1. Count the floor. Run one tier-1, one tier-2 and one irreversible change. Record how many distinct people each needed, and whether the forge refused a collapse the rules forbid.
  2. Measure review hours per change for agent-authored and human-authored changes of the same tier, so step 5 gets a number.
  3. Try the forbidden collapse. In a class A to C configuration, have the directing engineer hold admin on the substrate their session runs on. The G4 run test must catch it.
  4. Run one learner-mode unit with a tutor-configured agent and an AI-free check afterwards.

A solo pilot cannot test points 1 and 3 beyond refusal. Plan 10 recruits a second person or lists them as untestable in advance.
