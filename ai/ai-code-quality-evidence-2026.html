Improving Code Quality with AI — What the Evidence Supports (Oct 2026)

The question this note answers is narrower than “what are the best practices”: which AI uses have measured evidence of raising code quality, which have evidence of lowering it, and which are reasonable but unmeasured. It is a dated evidence ledger for the cluster whose reasoning already lives in ai-for-quality-not-quantity, deterministic-gates-agent-loop and ai-assisted-code-review. Those notes argue the why; this one records what 2025–Oct 2026 studies found.

Verification status. Four research passes ran on 2026-10-23, and each read its primary sources (mostly arXiv abstracts, not full papers). Claims marked † were re-checked at the source on the same day by a second fetch. Unmarked claims rest on one research pass’s read of the abstract or page. Anything seen only in a search snippet is labelled (unverified), or left out.

The short answer

The recurring pattern across sub-areas: AI output does better where an independent, executable check that the AI cannot edit sits in the loop. It does worse where output volume outruns the checks. That is a reading of mostly observational and case-study evidence, not a tested law. The candidate practices differ a lot in how much evidence they have:

PracticeEvidence grade
Feed deterministic findings back into generation, rather than relying on prose rulesOne small controlled generation test; uncontrolled migration case studies; an observational adherence-decay result
Aim AI-written tests at faults (mutants, properties, diff-catching), not coverageFiltered real-bug yields in industrial and red-team deployments; no 2026 head-to-head against coverage-aimed generation found
Keep context files short and limited to what the agent can’t inferOne controlled benchmark (no general success gain, more cost)
Use AI review as a measured first pass, never the only reviewerLarge observational acceptance and merge-rate data, confounded
Keep part of the oracle hidden from the agentMeasures a risk (visible-vs-held-out gap), plus a null result for TDD-by-prompt. Untested as a quality intervention.
Keep changes small enough that a human still reviews themNot established by the 2026 sources (see §6). It rests on prior DORA-era consensus.

The evidence base is mostly hyperscaler monorepos (Google, Meta, Airbnb). How each practice carries over to a product-team codebase without provenance tracking or mutation infrastructure is untested.

What degrades: the baseline you are correcting for

  • Google, AI-generated C++ in production (Tran et al., arXiv 2608.06640, 6 Aug 2026)†
    • Scale: 3.52M code changes, Apr 2025–Apr 2026, with AI authorship taken from provenance recorded at authoring time.
    • Quality profile: more interface and coupling burden, more copy and allocation overhead, and a 5–8% increase in compute consumption†.
    • Review cost, per the research pass’s read of the PDF (unadjusted descriptive contrasts): about 1.9× the blocking review threads and about 1.3× the build and sanitizer failures, but a lower median revert rate (~0.9×).
    • Caveats: the study is observational, and the authors are from Google.
  • Silent debt in AI-authored commits (arXiv 2603.28592, v3 13 Oct 2026)†
    • 302.6k commits across 6,299 repos and five assistants.
    • 7.5–14.8% of each assistant’s commits introduce at least one static-analysis issue. Code smells are 89.3% of those issues, and 22.7% of them survive at HEAD.
    • The v2 abstract (Apr 2026) gave 17.4–29.1%. Cite v3, since secondary coverage still carries the older range.
    • Caveats: there is no human baseline, and a static-analysis warning is not a defect.
  • Agents skip reuse (arXiv 2601.21276, MSR 2026): agent PRs “frequently disregard code reuse opportunities”, and reviewers rated them neutrally or positively anyway. This is the duplication mechanism ai-code-latent-failure-modes describes, now with a dataset behind it.
  • Faros AI Engineering Report 2026 (telemetry from about 22,000 developers): bugs per developer up 54%, median review time up about 5×, and 31% more PRs merged with no review.
    • Caveats: the analysis is correlational, comparing each team’s low- and high-adoption quarters, and Faros sells the analytics product. Treat the figures as directional only.

What the controlled evidence says, and why it complicates the above

Borg, Hewett, … Farley, “Echoes of AI”, Empirical Software Engineering, 2026 (preprint arXiv 2507.00788)†

  • Design: preregistered two-phase experiment with 151 participants†. In phase 2, an RCT, new developers evolved the phase-1 code without AI.
  • Result: no significant difference in CodeHealth between AI-co-developed and control code, and the speed advantage in manual evolution was “small and statistically unreliable”† (Springer text). Phase-1 completion was 30.7% faster with AI†.
  • Caveats:
    • It was run in late 2024, before coding agents. The authors state this themselves†.
    • It used a single Java task. One research pass reported that it missed its preregistered sample-size target; that is unverified at source.
    • CodeHealth is CodeScene’s metric, and the authors cite it as “our previous work”†.

This is the strongest counterweight in the ledger. The one controlled maintainability test found no harm, and every large degradation dataset above is observational and confounded by task mix, adoption timing and how authorship was detected. See The case against below.

The practices, section by section

1. Close the loop with deterministic feedback, not prose rules

  • Targeted static-analysis feedback in context cut the targeted warnings by 11.1% and improved efficiency in Google’s controlled generation test† (50 functions, same paper as above). It is the only intervention result these research passes found in the degradation literature, and it is small and lab-bound.
  • Gated pipelines carry migrations. In Airbnb’s Enzyme→RTL migration, each file passed a state machine of refactor → Jest → lint/tsc, advancing only when a step validated. A first bulk run migrated about 75% of 3.5k files, 4 days of tuning raised that to 97%, and the long tail took 50–100 retries (airbnb.tech, ~Apr 2025). Google’s migration paper reports 74.45% of 595 changes LLM-generated across 39 migrations, with a self-estimated 50% time saving (arXiv 2504.09691). Both cases have an executable oracle at every step. Neither has a control arm, so they show that a gated pipeline can carry a migration, not that the gates are what made it succeed. Neither generalises to code without such an oracle.
  • Prose rules decay. In 1,650 Claude Code sessions, compliance with a config-file instruction fell about 5.6% in odds per additional generated function. File size, instruction position and file structure showed no detectable effect (arXiv 2605.10039). Caveats: the trend was found in post-hoc analysis, and the instruction was a trivial annotation. It is consistent with the hooks-over-instructions argument in claude-code-enforcement-hooks, but too weak to settle it.

2. Keep agent context files small and non-obvious

“Evaluating AGENTS.md” (arXiv 2602.11988, v3 29 Sep 2026)†

  • Providing context files “does not generally improve task success rates” and adds over 20% inference cost.
  • Agents follow the instructions well, but repository overviews “are not helpful”.
  • The files are useful for “non-standard coding practices”†.

A Codex-only study found a 28.6% shorter median runtime with AGENTS.md present (arXiv 2601.20404v2, 124 PRs). The two measure different things (inference cost vs wall-clock time), so they don’t strictly conflict. Neither study measured maintainability.

The practical rule: write down only what the agent can’t infer from the code. A census of 2,303 context files found security in 14.8% and performance in 14.5% (arXiv 2511.12884), which is where rules that are hard to infer would earn their keep.

3. Point AI-written tests at faults, not coverage

  • Mutation-guided generation. The coverage-vs-mutation-score gap and Meta’s ACH are already covered in mutation-testing-ai-generated-code. New in 2026 is JiTTest (Meta, arXiv 2601.22832, 30 Jan 2026)†. It generates catching tests aimed at the diff, which yielded 4× more candidate catches than hardening tests, and its assessors cut human review load by 70%. Of 41 candidates reported to engineers, 8 were true positives and 4 of those would have caused serious failures†. Read that as about a 20% hit rate on what reached humans. It is useful precisely because the triage is filtered.
  • Agentic property-based testing (Anthropic Frontier Red Team, 14 Jan 2026; paper arXiv 2510.09907)†
    • Of a reviewed sample of 50 out of 984 reports†, 56% were valid bugs and 32% were worth reporting.
    • After ranking with a rubric, 86% of the top reports were valid and 81% reportable†.
    • Merged fixes landed in numpy, aws-lambda-powertools and tokenizers†.
    • Cost: about 1 hour of expert review per candidate.
  • PBT-Bench (arXiv 2605.15229, NeurIPS 2026): bug recall of 42–83% across eight models with a scaffolded prompt. Scaffolding helped mid-tier models by 20+ points and made two models worse. The bugs are injected, not organic.
  • LLM-written fuzz targets found 26 new vulnerabilities in already-fuzzed OSS-Fuzz projects, including OpenSSL CVE-2024-9143 (Google Security Blog, Nov 2024). That source is from 2024. No 2026 OSS-Fuzz-Gen figures were found. FuzzingBrain V2 reports 36 of 40 on AIxCC 2025 plus 29 zero-days, but those numbers are self-reported (arXiv 2605.21779).

4. Keep part of the oracle out of the agent’s reach

This section’s evidence measures the risk of an agent-visible oracle. Nothing here tests withholding tests as a quality intervention.

  • SpecBench (arXiv 2605.21384, v2 9 Sep 2026)†: frontier agents pass the tests they can see but fall short on held-out composed tests. The gap “grows by 28 percentage points for every tenfold increase in code size”†. One run produced a 2,900-line “compiler” that memorises test inputs. This is a dated measurement of the co-authorship failure described in oracle-problem-verification.
  • Escalation channels (arXiv 2608.29460): giving agents a structured way to report broken tests, plus a policy against gaming them, cut reward hacking from 23.6% to 5.3% across 8 models, with no detectable performance cost. The setting was narrow: broken-test scenarios only.
  • TDD-by-prompt doesn’t buy this. Böckeler (martinfowler.com, 2026)† found “no clearly discernable difference” in quality and “no meaningful difference in mutation scores” between TDD and non-TDD agent runs. TDD cost 8.5×, 3.0× and 4.9× the tokens on small, medium and large tasks†. Caveats: a very small sample, an LLM judge, and greenfield tasks only, all of which she states herself. One explanation is that writing tests first inside the same agent doesn’t separate the oracle from the generator. That is consistent with the human-approved scenarios in clause-loop, but a null result for TDD is not evidence that approved scenarios work. Nothing tested them.
  • Oracles from requirements. A 10-bug pilot found that LLM oracles generated from natural-language requirements agreed more with the intended behaviour than with the buggy code (arXiv 2607.10277). It is suggestive only, given its small sample and weak correlations.

5. AI review: a filtered first pass, measured locally

  • Real-world acceptance is about a third. On CodeRabbit, 31,073 review–feedback pairs across 239 repos showed 36.4% accepted, 7.3% discussed and 56.3% rejected (Lin et al., arXiv 2607.03316)†. Rejections were mostly false positives, redundant comments, out-of-scope suggestions or “misalignment with developer intent”†. A lightweight classifier predicted which comments would be rejected at up to 76% F1. Noise is learnable, so per-repo filtering is feasible.
  • Agent-only review underperforms. PRs reviewed only by code-review agents merged at 45.20%, against 68.37% for human-only review (Chowdhury et al., arXiv 2604.03196, MSR 2026)†. 12 of 13 agents averaged a signal ratio below 60%†. The comparison is confounded, because agents weren’t randomly assigned to PRs, but it still argues for augmenting humans, not replacing them.
  • The benchmarks disagree too much to pick a tool from.
    • Entelligence’s benchmark (vendor-run, ~Sep 2026, 67 bugs) and Martian’s offline set (50 PRs, Feb–Mar 2026, as reported by CodeAnt) rank the same tools differently. Greptile’s F1 is 36.9 on one and 40.6 on the other.
    • Each vendor’s own metric favours that vendor. Cursor’s resolution-rate table (LLM-judged, public repos only) puts Bugbot at 78.13% and Greptile at 63.49%† (Cursor blog, 8 Apr 2026).
    • Your own accept rate remains the only transferable measure. That is the ai-assisted-code-review position, and these 2026 tables don’t overturn it.
  • Configuration patterns with published support, from vendor docs as of Oct 2026 and therefore unmeasured:
    • Verify before posting, as in Claude Code Review’s find → verify → rank → post pipeline. Anthropic reports that “less than 1% of findings are marked incorrect” at $15–25 per review† (via Help Net Security, 10 Mar 2026). That is a vendor figure with no independent replication.
    • Severity tied to consequence, with a cap on nits. The REVIEW.md example limits a review to five nits.
    • Excluded paths, for generated code and anything CI already enforces.
    • Evidence for behaviour claims, in the form of a file:line citation.
    • Rules read from the base branch, not the PR, so a PR can’t weaken its own review.
    • Learned-rule loops (Bugbot learned rules, CodeRabbit learnings) exist, but no published before/after data was found for them.
  • Correlated reviewers. On 248,641 agent-authored PRs with an AI review, same-product reviewers left 58–65% more comments per PR than cross-product ones for three of four dual-role reviewers. The authors say “effect sizes were small or negligible and the difference was concentrated in the upper tail”† (arXiv 2608.21311). It is a weak, non-causal echo of the “two different reviewers on high-risk paths” advice in Osmani’s Agentic Code Review (15 Jun 2026).

6. Keep the change small enough that a human still reviews it (not established here)

Faros reports PR size (+51.3%), PRs merged without review (+31.3%) and bugs per developer (+54%) as separate trends that each rise with adoption. It explicitly leaves open “whether larger PRs account for the majority of the quality deterioration” for future editions†. So the 2026 data does not show that small PRs moderate the damage. DORA’s 2026 ROI report, read via InfoQ’s 11 May 2026 summary only, recommends small batches, automated testing and CI, and adapted approval processes. Those are recommendations, not measured moderators. The practice rests on the pre-AI review-capacity argument in review-capacity-org-design and vigilance-decrement-in-ai-oversight, not on new evidence.

Reasonable but unmeasured

  • Formal verification as a quality lever. LLMs are now strong on benchmarks: about 82% on Dafny and 44% on Verus (vericoding, arXiv 2509.22908), and 92.7% on DafnyBench (AxDafny, arXiv 2606.32007). No production use was found. AxDafny’s own caution is that verification success and runtime tests measure different aspects of generated code, so the risk of a wrong specification remains.
  • AI autofix for SAST findings. The only primary data is GitHub’s 2024 beta: median 28 min vs 1.5 h to fix. That measures speed, not acceptance or regressions. Figures from Semgrep (95% agreement) and Sonar (80% “fixable”) are vendor claims about different quantities (unverified at source). No independent regression study was found.
  • Linter and typecheck feedback inside the agent loop. It is widely recommended (Thoughtworks Radar, “Feedback sensors for coding agents”, Trial, Apr 2026), but no 2026 ablation isolating its effect on quality was found. The Google 11.1% result and the Airbnb pipeline are the closest evidence.

The case against

The strongest objection to this note’s short answer: the practice evidence is mostly single-company case studies by the teams that built the system (Google, Meta, Airbnb, Anthropic), plus vendor benchmarks. Against that, the one preregistered controlled test of maintainability found no harm from AI at all.

On that reading, there are two possibilities. The degradation datasets may be measuring more AI-touched code, not worse code per line: Google’s lower revert rate fits that, and the silent-debt study has no human baseline. And “put a deterministic check in the loop” may be the advice the field already believed, now echoed back by studies designed around it.

Three more points strengthen the objection:

  • Publication bias. Gated pipelines that failed don’t get written up as engineering blog posts.
  • Research framing. The four research passes behind this note were scoped around these practices, so they were more likely to find support than refutation.
  • Cost. The best numbers carry real cost. JiTTest’s hit rate on what reached engineers was about 20%, agentic PBT needs about an hour of expert review per candidate, and TDD-by-prompt costs 3–8.5× the tokens.

What survives is narrower than the short answer’s framing:

  • Only two items are interventions with a comparison: Google’s taxonomy feedback (−11.1%, 50 functions, lab) and the escalation-channel policy (23.6% → 5.3%, broken-test scenarios).
  • Everything else is case studies without a control (migrations, JiTTest, PBT), risk measurements (SpecBench), or null results for alternatives (TDD-by-prompt, generated context files). A null for an alternative is not a win for the favoured move, because no study compared them head-to-head.
  • The Borg et al. null predates coding agents, but so do the Airbnb and Google migration cases, so that discount cuts both ways.

The honest grade: the direction is plausible and nothing found contradicts it, but it is mostly untested. The note’s own echo concern (“the field’s prior, echoed back”) is not answered by this evidence. Treat the short-answer table as a set of hypotheses to measure on your own codebase, using the accept-rate and rework metrics in measuring-own-ai-leverage, not as settled practice.

Gaps worth watching

  • An ablation of lint and type feedback inside an agent loop, measured on maintainability rather than task success.
  • An independent study of regressions introduced by AI autofix.
  • Any study measuring reviewers deferring to bot approval in 2026. “3100 Opinions on Code Review in an AI World” (arXiv 2607.07980) looks relevant, but the research pass could not read it.
  • A Borg et al.-style controlled maintainability test repeated with agents.
  • GitClear’s 2026 figures. Secondary sources disagree with each other, and none were read at the primary source, so they are omitted here (see deterministic-gates-agent-loop for the earlier directional-only treatment).
