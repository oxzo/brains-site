Team Size and Coordination Under Coding Agents

What it is

When coding agents write much of the code, does a software team get smaller, larger or differently shaped? This note collects four kinds of material on that question and keeps them apart:

  1. Classic claims. Conway, Brooks, two-pizza teams, Team Topologies and social loafing: what each says and what supports it.
  2. Measured evidence on team size, all from before agents.
  3. Measured evidence from the agent era on firm size, output per engineer, review and junior hiring.
  4. Reference classes: what earlier tooling shifts did to headcount.

Every claim carries a grade:

  • Measured means a study with a disclosed method.
  • Survey means self-report.
  • Practitioner means an experience essay or framework.
  • Vendor means a firm reporting on its own product or practice.
  • Inference is this note’s own reasoning, labelled where it appears.

Sources were checked at source on 2026-11-01 unless a line says otherwise. The agent-era evidence is weeks to months old and moves fast.

Why it matters

Forecasts that agents mean “smaller teams” are common and usually run on arithmetic: output per engineer goes up X%, so you need X% fewer engineers. This note checks that arithmetic against three things: the classic mechanisms, the few measurements that exist, and what earlier tooling shifts actually did. The short answer is that no study measures enterprise software team size, or reviewer headcount, against coding-agent adoption (as of 2026-11-01). Everything said about agent-era team size is either a measurement of something nearby or an inference.

Classic claims

Conway (1968), an essay. Conway’s thesis is that “organizations which design systems … are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations.” He also made a size argument that is usually forgotten. The first step in his account of how large systems disintegrate is “the temptation to assign too many people to a design effort.” He notes that possible communication paths grow at “approximately half the square of the number of people,” and he concludes that “a design effort should be organized according to the need for communication” and that managers should be rewarded “for keeping their organizations lean and flexible.” The evidence is anecdote. The measured tests of mirroring are covered in ai-function-topology: mirroring is common in firms and weak in open collaborative software projects.

Brooks (1975), an experience essay. Brooks’s law: “Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later.” He gave three mechanisms:

  • Training, which “varies linearly with the number of workers.”
  • Intercommunication, which grows as n(n−1)/2 when every part must be coordinated with every other.
  • Sequential work such as debugging, which more people cannot shorten.

On size, he cites “the small sharp team, which by common consensus shouldn’t exceed 10 people.” His proposed shape is Mills’s surgical team: ten people, with the system “the product of one mind or at most two.” He also concedes that the small sharp team “is too slow for really big systems.” Steve McConnell’s column Brooks’ Law Repealed? reports that Brooks, twenty years on, still called the law “the best zeroth order approximation to the truth.” McConnell’s own view is that the point where adding staff backfires “occurs later than Brooks’ law states and in limited circumstances.” That is practitioner opinion. The Brooks quote reaches us second-hand through McConnell.

Two-pizza teams, a vendor account. An AWS whitepaper quotes Bezos: “We try to create teams that are no larger than can be fed by two pizzas.” It links the rule to collaboration and to “the concept of ownership.” No outcome data is offered. The rule is best read as a proxy: one small team owns one service end to end.

Team Topologies (Skelton & Pais, 2019), a practitioner framework. A team is “a stable grouping of five to nine people.” “An organization should never assign work to individuals; only to teams.” Teams of up to about fifteen are possible only in very high-trust organisations. The size figures come from Dunbar’s number, and the five-to-eight single-team figure is labelled by the authors themselves “based on industry experience.” The second constraint is scope: organisations “should not allow a software subsystem to grow beyond the cognitive load of the team responsible for the software.” When a team is overloaded, it “starts to behave like a loosely associated group of individuals.” No independent test of either constraint is known; see ai-function-topology. multiplatform-team-topology covers the team types and the inverse Conway manoeuvre.

DORA, a survey. The 2017 State of DevOps Report (3,200 respondents) found that “loosely coupled architectures and teams are the strongest predictor of continuous delivery.” The single biggest contributor was a team’s ability to change its system’s design “without permission from someone outside the team.” The 2015 report (4,976 respondents) looked only at those who deploy at least daily. As developer count rose, low performers deployed less often per developer, medium performers held steady, and high performers deployed “at a significantly increasing frequency.” This is the only classic that says size need not cost output. It rests on a self-selected, snowball-sampled survey, and it puts the binding cost between teams, not within them.

Social loafing, measured. Karau & Williams (1993) meta-analysed 78 studies and found loafing “robust” across tasks and populations. The strongest moderators were “evaluation potential, expectations of co-worker performance, task meaningfulness, and culture.” Most of the studies are lab tasks, not software.

Measured evidence on team size (pre-agent)

Per-person output falls as open-source teams grow. Gote et al. (ICSE 2022) studied 201 GitHub projects sampled across the full range of team sizes. They found that “doubling the size of a development team” cuts average per-member productivity “by between 22% and 30%.” That figure holds the team’s collaboration-network measures constant. Editing other people’s code is separately and strongly linked to lower productivity. The authors call earlier positive findings “a specific instance of Simpson’s paradox.” On optimal size, the model shows “some evidence for an optimal team size of 7 or 19” on two of eight measures, but “for six out of eight productivity measures, the optimal productivity per team member is reached for a team size of one.” They treat the exact numbers as artefacts. Caveats: open source only, and productivity is measured as code volume and complexity, not value.

The superlinear counter-result is mostly a measurement artefact. One ETH Zürich group found superlinear scaling in open source; another found sublinear. Gut & Goldman (SBES 2024) replicated both. Sampling explained part of the gap: 81.7% of the superlinear study’s projects had fewer than 50 contributors, while the sublinear study sampled projects with more than 50. But “instrumentation biases contributed more” than sampling did.

Commercial benchmarks point the same way, with confounding. ISBSG (2010) analysed 1,681 projects and found that “PDR gets worse as team sizes increase” (PDR is hours per function point). Results also get more variable: for small projects, the spread between the 10th and 90th percentile was 21.7 hours per function point for teams of 1 to 4 and 45.4 for teams of 9 or more. The report says itself that larger projects tend to have larger teams. QSM, a vendor analysing its own database, reports that “smaller teams of 3-7 people were optimal” for medium systems in a 1997 study. In a 2012 analysis of about 1,060 IT projects from 2005–2011, large teams on small projects were “3 times more costly” with “2 times as many defects.” A later revisit found added staff cut schedule about 30% while cost rose 350% (blog post, 2019; underlying studies not read). None of these isolates late projects, so none tests Brooks’s law as stated. They test the broader cost of team size.

Coordination that matches dependencies helps in one firm and shows nothing in open source. Cataldo, Herbsleb & Carley (ESEM 2008) studied 114 developers in eight teams at one company. When coordination matched coordination needs, “the resolution time of modification requests was significantly reduced.” Mauerer et al. (arXiv 2021, accepted to IEEE TSE) studied 25 large open-source projects and found congruence “not related to project quality measures” (bugs and churn) “in any temporal scenario.”

Distribution adds people per item, and people add calendar time. Herbsleb & Mockus (IEEE TSE 2003) found distributed work items took “about two and one-half times as long” as co-located ones. The suggested mechanism is that distributed items “involve more people,” and headcount per item “is strongly related to the calendar time.” They replicated this in a second organisation.

The classic review ratio. At Google, 2014–2016, across 9 million reviewed changes (Sadowski et al., ICSE-SEIP 2018), the median reviewer count was 1. The median developer authored about 3 changes and reviewed about 4 per week, and median review latency was under 4 hours. Often only one reviewer was required “since changes are often authored by someone with ownership and/or readability for the files in question.” This is descriptive data from one firm, before agents.

Measured evidence from the agent era

Firm size: only startups have been measured. Kim & Koning (INSEAD working paper, June 2026) compare AI-native startups with others founded in the same industry and year. AI-native startups are “25% smaller” in the Y Combinator sample and about 12% smaller in the PitchBook sample. They have a higher share of engineers, about 15% lower shares of entry-level workers and managers, and flatter hierarchies. Naming worker-facing AI tools (ChatGPT, Copilot, Cursor) in job postings, the authors’ proxy for using AI internally, “does not predict smaller firm sizes.” The size gap goes with AI in the product. The authors warn that if AI produces more startups, “the overall demand for labor could well rise.” Bena, Bian & Giannetti (NBER conference paper, July 2026) find “20% more startup formation” in GenAI-exposed industries and new firms about 25% smaller (from an average of 4.6 employees). They also find that “higher entry rates more than offset smaller firm size,” so employment in new firms rises 7%. Both studies measure startups, describe rather than establish cause, and are not enterprise teams.

One person with AI versus a pair (measured, not software). In a preregistered field experiment with 776 professionals at Procter & Gamble (Dell’Acqua et al., NBER WP 33641, 2025):

  • Pairs without AI scored 0.24 SD above individuals without AI.
  • Individuals with AI scored 0.37 SD above that baseline.
  • Pairs with AI scored 0.39 SD above it.

So “individuals with AI matched the performance of teams without AI.” The task was a one-day product-ideation workshop, and the “team” was two people. The authors note that it did not capture “extended coordination challenges and iterative rework cycles.” The firm funded the research lab.

Coordination activity falls for individuals. In a regression-discontinuity study of open-source maintainers given Copilot code completion in 2022–23 (Hoffmann et al., HBS WP 25-021), coding rose by 5.4 percentage points of activity and project management fell by 10 points. Effects were larger for lower-ability developers. GitHub and Microsoft co-authored it (vendor). The study predates agents.

Output per engineer rises, measured as volume. Microsoft studied its own early-2026 rollout of Claude Code and Copilot CLI across “tens of thousands of engineers” (Murphy-Hill, Butler & Savelieva, arXiv 2607.01418). It estimates “a +24.0% lift in PRs/engineer/day” (95% CI +14.5% to +33.7%), with no fade over 16 weeks. Junior ICs were less likely to try Copilot CLI, but “more junior ICs and more senior managers experience a larger PR lift” once they adopted. Peer use was the strongest adoption predictor. This is a vendor measuring its own rollout, and one of the two tools (Copilot CLI) is its own; and merged PRs measure volume, not value, quality or headcount.

Review: the share of PRs that get any human review looks unchanged, but its form changes. In open-source repositories that host both agent and human PRs (Duma et al., EASE 2026), human participation was “nearly identical: 30.12% for agent-authored PRs and 30.83% for human-authored PRs.” So about 30% of both see any human review, and most get none. 28.37% of human comments on agent PRs were spent steering the agent. Review effort on agent PRs concentrates in a predictable tail: a model trained on static features “captures 69% of the high-effort PRs” at a 20% review budget (Minh et al., MSR 2026, abstract only). Neither study gives a reviewer-to-author ratio. review-capacity-org-design covers the review queue itself.

Junior hiring fell; the cause is contested. On the ADP payroll data in Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen’s Canaries in the Coal Mine? (August 2026 revision), employment of 22–25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations “now stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace” with less-exposed peers. The decline came “through reduced hiring of young workers rather than increased separations.” Earlier versions headlined a regression-adjusted estimate (13%, then 16%); on the current descriptive measure the gap was 15% in July 2025 data. Cite the version and the measure. The authors report that the gap persists when excluding technology firms and computer occupations and when controlling for interest-rate exposure and remote work, and concede that it shrinks with education controls and shows “some divergent trends predating generative AI.” The data are US payroll records. The authors call these “early, descriptive indicators … rather than causal estimates.” Hosseini & Lichtinger (May 2026 version) find junior employment at firms they identify as GenAI adopters (through GenAI-integrator job postings; US data through 2025) falling “by about 9 percent after six quarters,” mainly through slower hiring. They add that it “may ultimately prove temporary.” Against an AI cause:

  • Lambert & Schindler (Warwick, May 2026) find that when remote-work exposure enters the model, “the GenAI coefficient attenuates sharply.” Software developers sit at the top of both exposure rankings, which correlate at 0.77.
  • Frank et al. (arXiv 2601.02554) find risk in exposed occupations rose “beginning in early 2022, months before ChatGPT was released.”
  • Humlum & Vestergaard (Denmark, data through 2024, chatbots) find “precise zeros” on earnings and recorded hours across 11 exposed occupations. That is a different outcome from junior hiring, and the tools are pre-agent.

For software in particular, AI exposure, remote work and the 2022 tech correction are close to the same variable in some of these designs (Lambert & Schindler). The wider labour picture is in ai-job-displacement-timescales-mechanisms.

Reference classes

Earlier shifts that raised per-worker output in a white-collar function, read for what happened to headcount.

Compilers. Brooks’s No Silver Bullet (1986) reports that “most observers credit” high-level languages “with at least a factor of five in productivity.” That is a consensus, not a measurement. Programmer demand rose anyway. By 1967 there were “at least 100,000 programmers” in the US and “an immediate need for at least 50,000 more” (Ensmenger, 2003, relaying industry estimates). Census counts show IT workers rising from 450,000 in 1970 to 781,000 in 1980 (Census ACS-35, 2016). Ensmenger argues that tools meant “to eliminate the need for skilled programmers” had the effect of “elevating their status.” Demand from cheaper hardware confounds all of this.

CAD, the closest structural case (measured, multi-country case synthesis). Ebel & Ulrich (International Labour Review, 1987) report output per draughtsman up 200% to 6,000%. Yet “the corresponding labour-saving effects are nowhere near so high,” partly because a draughtsman spends “about half of his time” on non-drawing work. Employment in user firms’ design offices had not fallen. Detail draughtsmen and tracers declined, and firms employed “slightly fewer trainees”, a trend the authors attribute “probably … primarily to the latest economic downturn”. Firms hired new staff to maintain the CAD systems, raised entry education, and took two to four years to reach stable productivity.

ATMs: smaller units, more of them, then a decline. Tellers per urban branch fell from 20 to 13 between 1988 and 2004 while urban branches rose 43%, so “teller jobs did not disappear” (Bessen, IMF F&D, 2015). This is the one pre-agent case in the reference class with a per-unit headcount figure. The offset did not last: BLS CPS counts tellers at 413,000 in 2011 and 224,000 in 2025, a 46% fall (own arithmetic, crossing a 2020 classification break). The cause is not established.

Occupational relocation. In a census study, Bessen (BU working paper, 2016) finds occupations that use computers “grow faster, not slower” and that “computers create about as many new jobs as are eliminated” by substituting between occupations (“fewer telephone operators, but more receptionists”). The software version, in BLS CPS:

  • Computer programmers fell from 459,000 (2011) to 381,000 (2025).
  • Software developers rose from 1,044,000 to 2,254,000.

Both cross the 2020 code break. In the offshoring era, BLS reported IT employment up about 8% over 2001–07 while programmers and support specialists each lost more than 6% (Wright, BLS, 2009). Its 2025–35 projection repeats the pattern with a new cause: programmers “projected to decline 7 percent” as firms use “artificial intelligence (AI), to automate repetitive programming tasks,” and software developers, QA analysts and testers are projected to grow 10%. what-survived-machine-conquest covers the general pattern of routine roles disappearing while judgement roles survive.

Merges get re-specialised. DevOps merged development and operations. Google’s SRE model then re-split them with “a 50% cap on the aggregate ‘ops’ work for all SREs” and claims SRE headcount “scales sublinearly with the size of the system” (Site Reliability Engineering, introduction; vendor, no headcount data).

What the class shows, read cautiously. In the cases with data:

  • In the cases chosen, total headcount in the function held or rose during the shift. Occupations that vanished with their tooling (keypunch operators, typing pools) are not in the class, so this partly reflects case selection.
  • The routine, translating slice of the work shrank, sometimes years later.
  • A support or platform role appeared around the tool.
  • The bottom rung was hit first.
  • Realised labour savings were far below claimed task speed-ups.

The attribution is weak throughout: series cross classification breaks and macro shocks, and the cause BLS states for the same programmer decline has moved from offshoring and automation (2009) to AI (2025–35 projections). The class mostly measures occupations, not teams.

What this does and doesn’t imply

What the evidence supports:

  • Per-person output falls as a human team grows, on pre-agent open-source data (measured).
  • Output per engineer measured as PR volume rose about 24% at one vendor (measured, vendor).
  • AI-native startups are smaller, and naming AI tools in job postings, the authors’ proxy for internal use, did not predict it (measured, startups only).
  • Early-career hiring in AI-exposed occupations fell, with the cause contested (measured).

What it does not support: “enterprise teams will shrink in proportion to the output gain.” No study tests it, the reference class argues against proportionality, and the startup evidence has the wrong population and the wrong channel.

How each classic mechanism may bind differently under agents (all inference, not tested):

  • Brooks’s three terms behave differently. Training is re-paid as context in every agent session. Intercommunication becomes merges and supervision links rather than conversation: one engineer directing N agents has N links, not N(N−1)/2, but only if the agents do not coordinate with each other. The sequential term (debugging, review, verification) does not shrink as agents are added. The surgical team, where one mind holds the design, is the classic form of “one engineer directing agents,” and Brooks’s own warning that it is too slow for very big systems is its ceiling.
  • The headcount axis and the output axis both break. Team-size studies use people as the x-axis and code volume as the y-axis. Agents add output without adding members and inflate exactly the volume metrics. A rerun of Gote-style analysis would need a different denominator.
  • The one-reviewer norm rests on a premise agents remove. At Google one reviewer was often enough because the author often held ownership or readability. An agent author holds neither, so review load may concentrate on code owners.
  • Team Topologies’ scope cap gains a second term. Agents may remove routine load, which argues for wider scope. The load of reviewing and holding the design of agent-written change grows with the rate of change, which argues for narrower scope. The 5–9 team-size figure concerns people, so agents do not count against it.
  • Evaluation potential drops. “The agent wrote it and someone approved it” weakens the moderator that most reduces social loafing, unless one answerable person is named per change.
  • Conway’s over-staffing temptation returns in a new form. Cheap parallel agent streams are, in Conway’s terms, “a design organization in need of work.”

The strongest counterargument. Agents span far more of the job than drawing (CAD) or cash handling (ATMs) did. If so, the “task is a minority of the job” brake that held labour savings down in CAD may be much weaker, and the reference class may understate the effect. Against that: the demand response that kept headcount up in every “held” case (more branches, more installed computers, more analysis asked of accountants) is unmeasured for enterprise software, and nothing yet shows it present or absent. Both directions remain open, so the honest status of “agents shrink teams” is unmeasured.

What needs more research

  • Any enterprise study of team size, or reviewer and approver headcount, against coding-agent adoption.
  • Productivity measures that are not code volume, applied to agent-assisted teams, ideally with quality and rework outcomes.
  • Whether the demand response (more projects, more teams) appears for enterprise software, which is the hinge of every reference-class case.
  • An industrial test of Brooks’s law as stated (adding people to a late project). None was found.
  • Independent validation of Team Topologies’ size or cognitive-load caps. None is known.
  • Separating AI from interest rates, remote work, the tech correction and tax changes in software-developer hiring. No study yet does all at once.
  • Hackman’s team-size work and Brooks’s 1995 anniversary chapters were not read for this note.
