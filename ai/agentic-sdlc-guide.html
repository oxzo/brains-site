Agentic SDLC — Current Best-Guess Guide

As of November 2026. This is the practical, standalone guide distilled from the vault’s enterprise Agentic SDLC research program (starting from agentic-sdlc-best-guess and its phase notes). For full source tables, evidence grades, and decision logs, follow the phase links at the bottom.

Scope. This guide targets enterprise-level software: long-lived systems shared by multiple teams, bound to legacy integrations, owned by many stakeholders, and subject to outside audit, regulation, or customer security review. If you are a solo developer shipping a small greenfield app, you do not need most of this — use clause-loop or spec-driven-development-operating-model instead.

The Core Bet in One Paragraph

Do not try to build an AI-native SDLC out of prompt instructions, multi-agent persona chains, or AI self-approval. Instructions decay within a session, prompt-only guardrails fail against injection and fatigue, and current enterprise data shows coding agents raise change volume and review wait, not proven net delivery speed. Our best guess keeps five event-triggered engineering phases, moves every safety boundary out of prompts into the forge and platform, treats human verification and intent ownership as the binding constraint, and pairs model-free automated gates with risk-tiered human approval.

Six Non-Negotiable Rules

Every choice below rests on six structural rules. Break one and the lifecycle loses its safety properties regardless of how good the model gets.

  1. No self-certification. The producing agent (and anything sharing its session or context) never approves its own change and cannot edit or weaken the checks that judge it.
  2. Boundaries live outside the agent. Permissions, sandboxes, branch rules, egress limits, and credentials are enforced by the forge, cloud IAM, or an org-operated harness — never by CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md, or interactive confirmation pop-ups.
  3. An answerable human stands behind every production change. Either a named human approved the change directly, or a named human owns the admission policy that allowed it.
  4. Irreversible changes get a separate, stricter gate. Data migrations, deletions, breaking shared contracts, and external sends cannot rely on rollback. They require explicit classification, a dry-run diff against the target, and two distinct human approvers.
  5. Artifacts and gates outlive the model. Every artifact of record is plain text, every blocking gate is model-free and re-runnable without the producing model, and audit records are exported at write time to a write-once store.
  6. Protect review capacity by cutting per-item cost, not by rubber-stamping. Shrink what reaches humans where regulations allow, give reviewers a structured evidence bundle instead of a raw diff, and throttle agent PR intake before review thins into silent approval.

Lifecycle at a Glance

A change does not march through all five phases blindly. Phases are event-triggered stretches of work: a routine bug fix enters Build → Verify → Release; only changes that alter requirements or architecture enter the upstream phases. Phase-by-Phase Operating Guidance

1. Requirements & Intent (per requirement change, not per project)

Agents almost never ask clarifying questions on their own (failing to ask on 44–100% of underspecified benchmark tasks), and test-passing agent PRs still miss intent (maintainers rejected ~24 percentage points more PRs than automated graders passed in one benchmark study). Treat requirement ownership as a first-class engineering boundary.

  • Keep a living plain-text baseline in the repo, one file per area: SHALL statements, WHEN/THEN scenarios, non-functional and design constraints (not just behavior), owner, source (stakeholder, regulation, or contract clause), and risk/irreversibility class.
  • Lock the baseline at the forge. Put baseline paths under CODEOWNERS excluding all agent identities, with admin bypass disabled. Agents may draft deltas on branches; only the human Requirement Owner (plus a Compliance Owner for regulated/contractual items) lands them. Assign one accountable owner per area plus a named deputy so leave does not trigger bypasses.
  • Time approval by change class (untested design rule):
    • Approve before building: regulated or contract-sourced requirements, irreversible changes, shared/consumed contracts, or multi-owner changes.
    • Approve on the built change (prototype-first): everything else — only if the Requirement Owner writes acceptance scenarios before seeing the prototype, to guard against anchoring on polished output.
  • Run an advisory ambiguity pass before building. Use a separate-context agent to surface ambiguities and send questions to the Requirement Owner. For stakeholder conflicts, log the conflict explicitly: the ticket wins locally for the current build, and one named arbiter fixes the baseline.
  • Trace at write time, not after the fact. Checks annotate which requirement ID they verify; CI generates the requirement→check map automatically (so routine test churn doesn’t touch the owner-locked baseline) and fails any requirement that has no check or named invariant owner.

2. Architecture & Design (event-triggered only)

Prose architecture rules decay within a session, and across real repositories LLM-judged compliance with them levels off around 72% even after rule edits; compiling the same rules into executable checks reached ~88% vs. ~67% for prose in one controlled study. Meanwhile, module-level design belongs inside Build.

  • Skip this phase for normal changes. Enter only on four observable triggers: (1) an executable architecture rule fails and the builder wants to keep the change, (2) a consumed contract would break, (3) the change is irreversible, or (4) a human flags it as pattern-setting.
  • Make the executable rule set + linked ADRs the artifact of record. Encode layers, allowed dependency edges, cycle bans, naming/element rules, and contract-break checks (e.g., Buf, oasdiff, Pact) as required forge checks. Pair each accepted decision with a short ADR referencing its enforcing Rule ID. Keep CLAUDE.md / AGENTS.md short (~100 lines, as a pointer map into docs and rules), never as the enforcement mechanism.
  • Lock rule files and self-exemption hatches. Most static architecture tools allow one-line self-exemptions in-repo (warn defaults, frozen-violation stores, tag edits). Put all rule files, thresholds, violation stores, and contracts under Area Technical Owner code owners.
  • Separate erosion from drift. Executable rules catch erosion (broken rules), not drift (creeping loss of coherence or duplicated behavior). Address drift with periodic, small, human-reviewed cleanup PRs — never unreviewed autonomous refactors (one study found aggressive agent cleanup introduced 140 new smells alongside a 63% detector false-positive rate).
  • UI & product design handoff. Treat Figma as upstream input, not an artifact of record (it is seat-gated and supports agent writes without documented review gates). Make the design token file (e.g., DTCG JSON), component registry, design-system lint, accessibility floor (WCAG / EAA / ADA), and human-accepted visual regression baselines the record, locked under the Design-System Owner. Never let an agent or vision-language model approve visual baselines (pixel diffs catch single-CSS changes reliably; tested VLMs missed >59%).

3. Build (one writing agent per unit, inside a platform envelope)

Published enterprise pipelines (Stripe, Spotify, Google, Airbnb) all converge on one writing agent per unit inside a deterministic frame. Controlled evidence does not yet show multi-agent persona chains beating a single agent on coding at matched tokens.

  • Use one writing agent per unit; fan out only on reading and judging. Run parallel units only across disjoint write sets; serialize units touching the same module. Use best-of-N candidate generation only when a validator the producer did not write picks the winner.
  • Size units by files touched and verifiable end state, not hours. Benchmark performance drops sharply with file count and drops steeply on private repos versus public ones (on SWE-Bench Pro, GPT-5 (high) fell from 41.8% public to 15.7% on commercial repos). Split large outputs into stacked PRs so each layer stays small enough to review.
  • Bound runs in code and restart clean. Cap CI/fix rounds (e.g., Stripe’s two CI rounds) and spend in workflow code, never in prompts. When a run hits its cap or spirals, restart from a clean context rather than accumulating failed attempts (errors compound in long contexts).
  • Cap build autonomy at a draft PR on an agent-only branch. Build agents hold zero credentials to merge, deploy, publish packages, or touch production data. Worktrees isolate working files, not blast radius (.git and standing approvals can leak across them).
  • Harden the platform envelope — never trust vendor defaults. Every major coding-agent vendor ships sandboxes off, fail-open, user-loosenable, or excluding surfaces like MCP servers. In regulated/contractual scopes (Classes A–C below), run change-producing sessions on org-operated substrate (managed VM or CI runner) so controls hold against both the agent and the directing engineer:
    • Admin-pinned sandbox failing closed with bypass modes disabled.
    • Protected configuration paths (.git, hooks, agent settings, CLAUDE.md/AGENTS.md, MCP configs — the dominant sandbox-escape vector is writing a file a privileged process later runs).
    • Credential-bound egress proxies (not mere domain allowlists; treat the forge itself as an exfiltration channel and keep MCP servers inside the boundary).
    • Separate caches and runners from release workflows (shared caches enable PR-to-release token theft).
    • Platform-pinned approved models, endpoints, and data-residency regions (zero personal accounts; re-approve when models or retention terms change).
  • Record write-time provenance. Capture agent identity (distinct from the directing human), directing engineer identity (from forge/platform auth), dated serving model ID + provider + inference region, inputs, tool-call metadata, and touched-path flags.

4. Verification (cost-ordered stack, strict check locks, tiered human review)

Under a real enterprise throughput-doubling mandate, human review coverage fell from 89% to 68%, substantive review fell from ~39% to ~21%, and silent approvals roughly doubled. Do not try to fix review by adding more reviewers — cut per-item review cost, automate everything model-free first, and throttle intake before assurance collapses.

  • Run verification cheapest and most independent first — with the forge run as the gate of record:
    1. Deterministic gates (blocking): compile, types, lint, SAST, architecture and contract rules, and required CI run under a workflow the repo cannot edit — on both the pushed commit and the integrated result (a merge queue up to queue-wait crossover; post-merge milestones + pre-deploy integration past it). Keep static-analyzer false positives below ~10% or engineers ignore them.
    2. Oracle strength & held-out suites (blocking / diagnostic): run held-out checks the producer cannot see or edit, plus mutation/property checks on changed code so 100%-coverage weak tests cannot mask behavioral drift.
    3. AI review (advisory finding generator — never a gate or approver): on real PRs, measured AI review recall sits between about a quarter and three-fifths (~23% on one benchmark; 45–59% LLM-judged on another), models share errors within a provider, and producer PR text can steer them (getting 15 of 17 security-fix reverts past one reviewer). Run the AI reviewer in a separate context, reading instructions from the base branch, with producer PR text and commit messages stripped. Prefer a cross-family model. Apply “answered, not obeyed”: high-severity findings block only until the directing engineer records a reasoned answer (the producer never closes them). Do not present an AI first pass ahead of human review — in one controlled study, reviewers given one found no more issues and inspected less code.
    4. Tiered human approval (blocking):
      • Tier 0 (zero human approval): Non-executable content only (e.g., plain copy and translations — never code, rendered markup, or docs/instructions read by agents), only in Regime Class D (or expressly preapproved non-executable types in Class C), under a named Class Owner and a clean escape-rate history.
      • Tier 1 (1 independent human approver): Default for ordinary changes. Approver cannot be the requester, producing agent, directing engineer, or anyone who pushed to the branch (computed from forge identities, not self-declared).
      • Tier 2 (2 independent human approvers, ≥1 outside the producing team): Mandatory for security/auth paths, CI and deploy workflows, check files, architecture rules, and irreversible changes.
    5. Intent acceptance: Required from the Requirement Owner bound to the head commit only when the change edits the requirement baseline (not for routine PRs implementing existing requirement IDs).
  • Enforce strict check-file locks (custom, untested): Any edit to tests, test setup/fixtures, snapshots, visual baselines, rule files/thresholds, CI/deploy workflows, package-manager configs, release policies, reviewer instructions, ownership files, or inline suppressions requires separate approval from the check’s owner (distinct from whole-PR approval).
    • Exemption: A brand-new test file that only adds cases needs no separate lock approval, counts as evidence, and joins the gate once ratified by a non-producer. New behavior still needs a check the producer did not write.
    • Run base check files against the PR (revert-and-rerun) and verify per-test execution identity so skipped or deleted tests fail the gate.
  • Give reviewers an Evidence Bundle, not just a raw diff: link to approved intent, checks executed with per-test identity, check-file changes + revert-and-rerun result, mutation score, answered AI findings, risk tier + inputs, and computed approver independence.
  • Throttle intake when review thins. Track team-level review coverage, silent-approval share, and a monthly sampled review-substance rating. Never turn these into individual metrics; when pool substance drops past your threshold, cap agent PR intake into that pool.
  • Reshape QA into a standing Verification function. Retire per-change QA handoffs and manual system-test phases. The Verification function owns held-out suites, check-file locks, tier policies, oracle ratification (delegable per area outside the producer/directing engineer), and the AI reviewer’s regression set.

5. Release, Operations & Legacy Evolution

Change advisory boards (CABs) fail at scale — a UK FCA review of 23 firms and over a million production changes found CABs approved >90% of major changes — and configuration pushes trigger nearly as many outages as code pushes (31% vs. 37% in Google’s 2010–2017 postmortems). Gate deployments with automated policy over evidence, progressive rollout, and strict credential boundaries.

  • Gate releases with a policy check over evidence bundles (custom, untested): Before deploying the exact built artifact, re-run release-level integration/contract suites and run a policy check across every included change’s bundle:
    • Admit automatically if every change carries its required tier approvals (or is an admitted Tier-0 class) and none is irreversible (in Classes C–D).
    • Route to the Release Owner in Classes A–B (every deployment) or when policy demands it, and to two distinct humans for any irreversible change.
    • Refuse any incomplete bundle or stale artifact digest (except pipeline-initiated rollbacks).
    • Include configuration changes in the same gate and evidence trail as code.
  • Lock down the deploy credential surface. Deploy only from protected branches through protected environments using OIDC whose cloud trust policy pins the protected environment. Disable admin bypass; keep zero production secrets at repo or org level; isolate deploy runners and caches from PR workflows; deny agents dispatch, re-run, tag, or admin rights.
  • Roll out progressively with an external signal. Use staged canaries or flags reading at least one health signal outside the changed service (load balancer, client, or downstream error rate) so a change cannot corrupt its own telemetry. Auto-abort on failure; pause for a human on inconclusive results. Schedule automated/Tier-0 rollouts during the owning team’s working hours.
  • Gate irreversible changes with two humans + dry-run diff (custom, untested): Require two distinct human approvers (verified as different individuals) reviewing a dry-run diff against the target environment plus a rehearsed restore or forward-fix plan. Pre-wire a separate two-human break-glass path (incident commander + second human, time-bounded credential, mandatory post-hoc review) so emergency fixes do not stall during an outage.
  • Harden the software supply chain. Never allow agent-added dependencies in Tier 0; require at least Tier 1 for version bumps (agents pick vulnerable versions more often and still hallucinate ~5% of package names in bare generation). Enforce package-manager install controls (preinstall scripts drove 99% of Shai-Hulud 2.0 infections), force installs through a registry proxy, and emit an SBOM per release.
  • Keep Ops Agents read-only with per-action write credentials. Give ops agents their own identity class with standing read-only access to telemetry (customer data gated). Grant zero standing writes: issue single-operation, single-resource, time-bounded credentials on approval — 1 human for pre-classified reversible actions (rollback, restart, bounded scaling), 2 distinct humans for any other write or external send, and refuse deletes even if approved. Route code fixes back through Build/Verify as PRs. Treat agent root-cause analysis as advisory and require it to show its causal path.
  • Fold maintenance into the loop and protect legacy with characterization oracles. Assign one owning team for the service’s full declared support period and reserve a standing share of capacity for preventive cleanup (flag removal, upgrades, complexity repair — open-source studies show complexity rising after agent adoption). Before letting agents refactor legacy code without tests, build characterization tests outside the producing agent (golden outputs from running legacy behavior + independent inputs), and batch migration PRs to reviewer capacity.

Three cross-cutting compliance rules:

  • Release-path separation overlay: Wherever MAS TRM / APRA CPG 234-style rules apply, the person who merges or dispatches any change (even Tier 0) cannot be its directing engineer.
  • Fall-back column: Wherever DORA Title II or PCI DSS applies, every change record must include rollback/fall-back steps and a security-impact statement.
  • Export a joined write-once record at write time (custom, untested): Do not rely on git commit signatures (lost on GitHub squash/rebase merges), git notes (lost on rebase), or native forge audit logs (7–180 days on GitHub/Azure DevOps as of Nov 2026). Join the Change Record (push) + Evidence Bundle (merge) + Release Record (deploy) on PR ID + commit SHAs, and export at write time to a write-once enterprise store neither the agent nor the directing engineer can alter. Retain for the service’s lifetime plus the longest post-retirement duty (e.g., 10 years for EU CRA technical docs).

Team Topology, Roles & Skills

Do not plan on shrinking teams. No study measures enterprise team size falling with coding agents; historical automation shifts relocated headcount when demand absorbed output and cut the entry rung first.

Size around human verification work and the separation floor. Per week, a team’s human load is changes × (directing + human-authored checks + review × approvals) + deployments × release approvals + standing duties. Whenever demand absorbs agent output, human verification work is the candidate bottleneck. Regardless of throughput, never drop below the separation floor:

  • Tier 1 change: ≥2 distinct humans (directing engineer + independent approver).
  • Tier 2 / irreversible change: ≥3 distinct humans, with ≥1 outside the producing team (meaning the smallest viable org is two owning teams, or one team plus an external approver pool), plus a sustainable on-call rota.

Protect skill formation and mixed-fluency teams. Controlled studies show unguarded AI assistance hurts subsequent unaided comprehension and performance (~17% drop), while tutor-style scaffolding preserves learning.

  • Treat junior hiring as a deliberate investment with budgeted mentor hours, tutor-mode agents on learning units, second-reader review apprenticeships, and AI-free skill assessments (AI-aided output cannot distinguish skill from delegation). Keep seniors sharp with periodic no-AI on-call/diagnostic reps.
  • Never tie performance appraisals to AI usage or token volume. Help low-fluency reviewers adapt with team-specific demos and protected learning time — otherwise agent-rate PR volume makes them either stall the queue or wave PRs through.

Economics, Metrics & Model Change

Plan for more volume landing on review, not a proven speedup. Enterprise studies show higher PR volume (+24% to +36%), much smaller delivery gains (+13.7% resolved issues on deployment, borderline), and +56% longer review wait. Human review time (pre-agent rough anchor ~40 min/change) likely dwarfs token cost (cents to ~$1.50/task on minimal scaffolds, though frontier costs and 30× run variance can push heavy tiers higher). The highest-leverage cost cut is lowering human verification minutes per change, not pinching tokens.

Track eleven service-level metrics (freeze definitions up front, compare each service only against its own past, report raw counts beside rates, and never use as individual performance targets):

  • Quality & rework: 7/30-day follow-up fix rate per change; change-fail & deployment rework rate; defect escape by phase (90-day window).
  • Review & flow: human verification effort per change (minutes + CI compute); team-level review substance (coverage, silent-approval share, monthly sampled quality); wait to approval & deploy; end-to-end lead time (recorded request → production); delivery rate (items resolved on deployment per engineer).
  • Cost & adaptation: total cost per accepted change and per delivered unit; AI-authored share from forge provenance (stratifier only, never a target); adaptation hours per model change.

Do NOT target: PR/commit counts, lines of code, autocomplete acceptance rate, token consumption, CI-green rate, or MTTR trends at low incident counts (high statistical noise).

Treat model change as an annual, budgeted maintenance event. Generally available models retire every ~12–22 months (with 45-day to 6-month notices), and serving stacks drift silently even under pinned IDs.

  • Pin dated model IDs in managed settings (never unpinned aliases) and log the serving model ID returned by the API.
  • Treat any model swap (producer, AI reviewer, or classifier) as a Tier-2 check-file change.
  • Maintain an execution-graded golden set for every model-specific component (prompts, harness, AI reviewer, classifiers) and measure per-item flips (not just averages) before promoting a new model. Re-verify held-out and incident-regression coverage with the new producer.

When to Pick a Different Lifecycle (Alternatives & Counterargument)

No single lifecycle wins every enterprise context. Our best guess above is the strongest default for multi-team, evolving enterprise products — but two alternatives genuinely win in narrower regimes, and adopting the full model carries real costs.

The strongest case against adopting this full best guess today:

  1. Three load-bearing mechanisms are custom and unbuilt by vendors. Strict check-file add-vs-edit locking, the unified write-once joined change record, and irreversibility dual-authorization with dry-run diffs do not ship out of the box in any surveyed forge or lifecycle. Building and maintaining them is a non-amortizing platform tax that breaks whenever forges or agent vendors change interfaces.
  2. Classification gaming can recreate self-certification one layer up. If Tier-0 relief or lighter substrate rules depend on a Compliance Owner’s regime classification, organizational pressure shifts from bypassing PR review to lobbying for a cheaper regime class.
  3. Dual authorization can collide with incident recovery. If an urgent production forward-fix is itself irreversible, requiring two humans on a dry-run diff slows recovery right when minutes matter — which is why the two-human break-glass path must be pre-wired and rehearsed before go-live.

What Is Evidenced vs. Untested (and What Would Change Our Mind)

Be explicit with stakeholders about epistemic confidence — the program’s empirical pilot was cancelled before running, so this guide combines well-evidenced failure modes with untested operational fixes:

  • Solidly evidenced (high confidence): Agents fail to ask on ambiguous tasks; tests pass while violating intent and design constraints; prose rules decay (compliance levels off around 72%); instruction-only security controls fail against prompt injection and click-through fatigue; agent adoption raises PR volume and review wait without proven net enterprise delivery speedup; blanket CAB review degrades into rubber-stamping; GA models retire roughly yearly; unguarded AI erodes unaided skill formation.
  • Current best guess, untested in production: The three custom gate mechanisms (strict check-file add-vs-edit detector, unified write-once joined change record, irreversible-class dual authorization on dry-run diffs); the undefined class-level escape-rate metric required before admitting any executable Tier-0 class; exact human review minutes per agent change; intake throttling when review thins; the junior-rung learner design; and whether the full control stack catches more risk than it costs.

Watch list — re-open this guide when any of these land:

  • Forge defaults: GitHub, GitLab, or Azure DevOps ships native check-file locking (distinguishing test edits from pure additions) or forge-signed model-ID provenance — turning our custom platform glue into configuration.
  • Regulatory rulings: An EU supervisor/ESA Q&A clarifies whether DORA Art. 17(1)(b) allows policy-admitted Tier-0 changes, or the AICPA 2027 SOC 2 criteria tighten rules on single-user development and deployment.
  • Measured verification & delivery data: Enterprise studies publish human review minutes per agent change, AI-vs-human review escaped-defect comparisons on the same PRs, or an enterprise RCT showing net delivery speedup after review and rework.
  • Vendor defaults: Major coding-agent vendors ship fail-closed, admin-pinned sandboxes with protected config paths by default.
