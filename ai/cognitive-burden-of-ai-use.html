Cognitive Burden of AI Use

The mental cost of working with AI is not what the productivity narrative assumed. Generation got cheap; judgment, verification, integration, and stewardship got expensive. The net effort is often comparable to or greater than doing the work manually — just distributed differently, and in ways the human cognitive system is less well-adapted to sustain.

This note is the hub for how that burden shows up, why it is structurally worse than ordinary computer work, and what design and workflow patterns actually reduce it. It sits alongside ai-automation-biases (which covers the trust side) and hitl-progressive-autonomy (which covers the oversight architecture). As of April 2026.

Why this matters

If AI verification is a candidate for a durable discipline (see ai-verification-stack-web, evaluating-ai-code-beyond-tests), then cognitive burden is the load curve anyone in such a role rides for a living. If the work of verifying, integrating, and stewarding AI output is structurally exhausting and structurally underestimated, then (a) the role has durable economic value because organizations cannot easily scale it by simply “adding more reviewers,” and (b) the personal sustainability question — how to do this work for years without burning out — is a first-class engineering problem, not a wellness afterthought.

For day-to-day developer work that uses AI as a tool (without verification being the explicit job), the same load curve still applies in compressed form: every PR review of agent-generated code, every “trust this output or check it” decision, draws on the same cognitive budget.

The shift: from generation to verification-as-default

The most carefully-sourced empirical picture comes from Lee et al. at Microsoft Research / CMU (CHI 2025), who surveyed 319 knowledge workers and analyzed 936 real-world examples of GenAI use. Their central finding, paraphrased:

  • AI does not replace the need for critical thinking in knowledge work; it relocates it.
  • Effort moves from information gathering and problem solving toward information verification, response integration, and task stewardship — the downstream, evaluative modes in Bloom’s taxonomy.
  • The worker’s relationship to the output shifts from author to critical integrator: editor, guide, constrainer, validator.
  • Higher self-reported trust in the AI correlates with less critical thinking enacted; higher task-specific self-confidence correlates with more.

That last pairing — the confidence-effort inversion — is the practical crux. The more you trust the AI, the less you check it; the more you trust yourself on the task, the more you push back. Both are problematic on their own but in opposite directions, and the bias fingerprint note (ai-output-bias-fingerprints) catalogs why polished AI output preferentially exploits the first.

Longitudinal confirmation (2026). Lee et al. is a cross-sectional survey; Vella & Blincoe (2026) followed professional software engineers over six months (two surveys, 158 → 101 respondents, matched cohort of 95) and found the shift playing out in time. Productivity perception held stable — 84% reported improvement at both time points — while the proportion reporting worsened developer experience nearly doubled, 14% → 27%, with flow state and cognitive load named as what specifically eroded. 82% reported spending less time writing code, and the authors propose a name for what replaced it: supervisory engineering work — the direction, evaluation, and correction of AI-generated output.

That is this note’s thesis measured over a real interval, by a group that did not stop believing it was more productive. It is self-report, so it measures experienced load rather than performance; that limitation is also what makes it the right instrument for this particular claim.

The burden is a slope, not a level

The sharpest experimental result on this note’s core claim also complicates it. Fan et al. (CHI 2026) held the LLM backend fixed (GPT-4o, fixed decoding, no tools) and varied only the interface — Inline completions, Chat, or Structured prompting — across N=60 participants and three Python tasks, with a no-AI control cohort. Per task, AI helped on every conventional measure: workload fell 18.2 RAW-TLX points, completion time fell 22% (25.0 vs 32.1 min), correctness improved (OR 1.71).

Fatigue nonetheless accumulated faster with AI than without it: KSS rose Δ +0.70 from task 1 to task 3 in the AI cohort versus Δ +0.48 in the control (Condition × Index interaction, p=0.041). Their behavioural verification-load composite — compile/test failures, time-to-first-compile, code churn, pauses, context switches — statistically mediated about 26% of that fatigue rise and 24% of the stress rise.

Two consequences matter more than the effect sizes, which are modest:

  • The load is in the accumulation, not the instance. A per-task workload measure can fall while the day still costs more. Studies that measure one task cannot see this, which is one reason the burden literature disagrees with itself.
  • Checking and repairing is the mediator, not generating. The design implication is not “generate less” but “schedule the checking” — cap it, batch it, and recover after it.

The same study found interaction mode governs the slope, and inversely to how cheap each mode feels: Inline had the steepest fatigue slope (Δ +1.18) despite being the fastest and lowest-load mode on simple work, with Chat shallowest (+0.92) and Structured between (+1.05); Mode × Index p=0.033. It also isolated a behavioural trigger — rejection streaks of ≥5 within 3 minutes, and pause clusters, predicted mode-switching (OR 2.36, 95% CI [1.33, 4.20]) — the “rejection spiral” participants described unprompted. And it located a calibration point: an Inline acceptance rate near 0.48 was associated with peak correctness, with both over- and under-acceptance degrading outcomes.

Scope discipline. Three ~25–32 minute lab tasks, capped at 40 minutes with 15–20 minute breaks. Extrapolating the slope to a workday is an inference, not their finding. The backend is a 2024-generation model without tool use, and the modes studied are completions and chat — not agent runs, where the ratio of machine output to human attention is far higher. Whether that steepens or flattens the slope is open. The operational schedule derived from this treats the mode and trigger findings as the transferable parts and the timings as conventions to tune.

Mechanisms that make supervisory work harder than it looks

“Supervisory” is not the same as “easier.” Four features of AI oversight consistently push load upward:

Cognitive decoupling. When you write something, you understand it as a byproduct of writing it. When you read something an AI wrote, you must reverse-engineer the reasoning the AI never actually did — and the output’s fluency gives you a false sense that you already grasp it. This is the same trap as the illusion of understanding, applied specifically to reviewing instead of reading for learning.

Accountability without authorship. You carry professional, legal, and in some domains moral responsibility for outputs you did not produce line by line and cannot fully inspect. The legal-accountability version of this is Elish’s moral crumple zone; the daily experienced version is a low-grade, draining background anxiety.

Fragmentation across multiple agents. Modern stacks route work through several specialized tools — IDE autocomplete, chat, code review bots, search-grounded agents, deployment assistants. Each hand-off costs working memory. Context management is a second-order problem that eats first-order attention. Multi-agent systems amplify this (multi-agent-token-efficiency, multi-agent-failure-modes).

The humanness amplifier. Conversational and agentic interfaces trigger the brain’s social-cognition machinery whether you want them to or not. Evaluating a chatty, confidently-worded assistant is not the same cognitive operation as evaluating a JSON response from a microservice, even when the information content is identical. Anthropomorphic design bypasses analytic filters and adds a layer of relational management on top of instrumental evaluation.

Vigilance decrement — the well-documented collapse of sustained attention when a system is usually-but-not-always correct — makes all four worse, and gets worse as the system improves, because rarer defects are missed at higher rates. See hitl-progressive-autonomy for the oversight-architecture response to this (autonomy reduction before escalation, anti-rubber-stamping UX).

The capacity-state axis — the burden is not a constant

Everything above describes the burden as a property of the work. It is also a function of the operator’s current executive capacity, which varies by hour, by day, and by illness. This axis is missing from most of the literature because studies recruit willing participants and measure them once.

Why AI work degrades faster than manual work

The load-floor argument, derived from cognitive decoupling above:

When you write something yourself, understanding is a byproduct — it arrives free with the act of producing. When you review AI output, understanding is a separate deliverable: you must hold the intended spec, construct a model of what the output actually does, and diff the two. Three working-memory objects where manual work needed one.

At full capacity the difference is invisible. As capacity drops, the byproduct path still works and the three-object path collapses. This predicts something the productivity framing misses: AI-assisted work has a higher load floor than manual work for small, well-understood tasks. Below some capacity threshold, writing the function yourself is genuinely cheaper than specifying it, reading it, and verifying it. “Use AI less when depleted” is correct triage rather than a failure of discipline.

The degradation curves are real and modelled

Three independent literatures put numbers on operator-state decay:

  • Vigilance decrement — measurable decline in sustained monitoring, worse for repetitive work, and reliably faster than intuition suggests: Mackworth’s founding experiment lost 10–15% of detection inside the first half hour, and later work finds decrement within 15 minutes. There is no fixed onset time to schedule against — it moves with signal salience, target prevalence, and task difficulty. The oversight version has a name: out-of-the-loop unfamiliarity, where an operator supervising a usually-correct system disengages and loses sensitivity to anomalies. vigilance-decrement-in-ai-oversight is the full treatment, including the finding that the obvious remedies (cap the block, batch the queue, micro-break) are much less supported than the problem is.
  • Fatigue-aware deferral — Zhang et al. (2026) argue that human-AI deferral systems fail because they assume static expert performance, and model it instead as a two-phase curve: quadratic warm-up to a peak, then sigmoid decay toward a floor, driven by cumulative workload. Their design detail is the load-relevant one: only tasks routed to the human accumulate fatigue. They validate against mammography data where radiologist recall declines from 78% to 66% over 100 continuous readings.
  • Acute state suppression — sidi-stress-cognition covers stress-driven dlPFC suppression and decision-fatigue the cumulative version. Illness belongs on the same axis: sickness behaviour is a cytokine-mediated syndrome (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) that includes cognitive impairment, and working memory depends on frontoparietal networks vulnerable to neuroinflammation. (The mechanistic chain from a common respiratory infection to measurable dlPFC hypoactivation is inferred from adjacent literatures — much of the causal work is LPS-challenge studies in rodents — so treat the mechanism as plausible and the phenomenology as the reliable part.)

The practical consequence is that capacity state should route the task, not just pace it. Low-capacity windows suit mechanical work with strong external verification signal — green CI, tight refactors, well-specified changes. They do not suit architecture decisions, “is this agent plan right,” or irreversible commits. This is the operator-side counterpart to the machine-side deferral policy Zhang et al. formalize.

The design implication for tooling and training

Every AI workflow is designed and tested by people at peak capacity, and used by people at median capacity. Tool designers, internal champions, and the person writing the onboarding doc are all in an unrepresentative state. A workflow that only works when the operator is sharp does not work. Design and evaluate against the median state, not the demo.

Why the problem doesn’t self-correct — the broken feedback channel

The burden is largely invisible to the person carrying it, which removes the signal that would normally drive adaptation.

METR’s randomized trial (2025) is the sharpest illustration. Sixteen experienced open-source developers completed 246 real issues on mature repositories they knew well (averaging 22k+ stars, 1M+ lines). With AI tools allowed, tasks took 19% longer (CI +2% to +39%). They had predicted a 24% speedup — and after experiencing the slowdown, still estimated AI had made them 20% faster. Among the contributing factors METR proposes is one squarely in this note’s territory: cognitive distraction from working with the tool.

Vella & Blincoe’s longitudinal cohort (above) shows the same channel from the other side: productivity perception stable at 84% while experienced load worsened.

Read together — the benefit is perceived and stable; the cost accrues on an axis nobody is measuring. Learning-by-doing needs a feedback signal, and here it is inverted for productivity and absent for load.

Scope discipline on METR. Its own February 2026 follow-up contextualizes rather than retracts: a late-2025 re-run gave −18% (CI −38% to +9%) for returning developers and −4% (CI −15% to +9%) for new ones, which METR itself calls “only very weak evidence” because 30–50% of developers declined to submit tasks they wouldn’t attempt manually, systematically excluding the highest-value AI cases. The experiment is being redesigned. The slowdown magnitude is uncertain and probably shrinking with tool generation; the perception gap is the robust part, and it is what this section rests on.

How it actually shows up

Workplace — “AI brain fry”

The term was coined by BCG in a 2026 Harvard Business Review article (Bedard, Kropp, et al., March 2026) based on a survey of roughly 1,488 US workers. It is a consulting-firm coinage, not a peer-reviewed clinical construct — useful as a label, but worth flagging as such. The study’s own reported differentials: AI-heavy oversight work associated with roughly 14% more mental effort, 12% more fatigue, 19% more information overload, and among the most affected (“brain-fry”) cohort, 33% higher decision fatigue and 39% higher intent to quit.

The driver in enterprise contexts is not AI per se, it is what an industry commentary has called the efficiency paradox: AI generates more options per unit time than a human can meaningfully evaluate, expanding the “sphere of accountability” faster than it contracts the sphere of execution.

A 2026 scale estimate, with a vendor caveat. Harness’s State of Engineering Excellence 2026 (700 engineering practitioners and managers across five countries, fielded by Sapio Research in April 2026) puts 31% of a developer’s day on what it calls AI-related invisible work — reviewing AI output, deeper quality scrutiny, and trust/override judgment calls — with 81% of engineering leaders reporting that code-review time has risen sharply since deploying AI. Developers named the top friction sources as reviewing AI code for accuracy (53%), fixing subtle bugs (52%), and explaining AI-generated code to teammates (48%). This is a vendor-published survey supporting a product category that sells into exactly this pain, so treat the direction as informative and the magnitude as unaudited.

The honest counterweight. DORA’s State of AI-assisted Software Development 2025 found no correlation between AI adoption and increased burnout or friction in its survey data — the largest developer dataset addressing the question, returning a null. DORA’s own reading is that strain is accumulating where surveys do not yet look (rising stalled work, restarts, and sharply higher parallel-thread counts), and that AI functions as an amplifier of existing organizational strengths and weaknesses rather than an independent load source. That reading is an inference from operational metrics, not a measurement of burden, and it should be held as one. Anyone citing this note for “AI is exhausting people” needs to also carry the null.

Education — atrophy vs. scaffolding

Two pieces of carefully-cited work:

  • Gerlich, M. (2025), Societies 15(1), 6 — survey of 666 UK participants using the Halpern Critical Thinking Assessment. Reported correlations: AI use ↔ cognitive offloading r ≈ +0.72, offloading ↔ critical thinking r ≈ -0.75, total effect of AI use on critical thinking r ≈ -0.42. The relationship is mediated through offloading, and the total effect is meaningful but not catastrophic.
  • Zhai, Wibowo & Li (2024), Smart Learning Environments 11, 28 — PRISMA review of 14 studies on AI dialogue systems and cognitive effects.

The caveat worth holding onto: heavy offloading is not the same as moderate, bounded use. The Gerlich effect is mediated, not direct — the risk is a usage pattern (delegating the productive struggle), not the presence of the tool. Beware the moral-panic register here; the popular press runs with it and the data is softer than the headlines.

Healthcare — ambient integration wins, intrusive CDSS loses

The clearest real-world dichotomy. Ambient scribes (listen passively, generate notes in the background) show strong effects in peer-reviewed work: a multi-center study published in JAMA Network Open (2025) across 263 clinicians and 6 health systems reported burnout dropping from 51.9% to 38.8% in 30 days; Stanford’s Tierney et al. study in JAMIA (2025, 32(2), 375+) found task load down 24 points and measurable burnout reduction.

In contrast, clinical decision-support systems that interject recommendations mid-workflow impose verification friction that can slow care and induce moral distress when AI and clinician disagree — the friction of cross-referencing against EHR and clinical judgment. Cognitive load here is dictated by integration posture, not by how capable the underlying model is.

Personal life — digital housekeeping

Managing a smart-home mesh or a consumer AI stack is increasingly a form of unpaid cognitive labor: onboarding devices, debugging integrations, re-auth’ing services, curating recommendation feeds. Sociology work on “digital housekeeping” shows this load distributes unevenly within households (typically onto the most tech-literate member). Recommendation-driven analysis paralysis is the same phenomenon in miniature: AI multiplies the options presented faster than it improves the decision.

The confidence-effort inversion — the actionable lever

Lee et al.’s finding that trust in AI and enacted critical thinking move in opposite directions is the single most useful design-level insight in this literature. It predicts:

  • The most fluent, best-marketed AI tools will produce the least critical engagement from their users. Polish is a load-reducer and a verification-reducer.
  • Domain experts push back more, even when fatigued, because task-specific self-confidence is a separate variable. Non-experts accept more.
  • Interventions that build earned skepticism (structured disagreement drills, forced counter-drafts, independent re-derivation on critical paths) are more valuable than generic “critical thinking training.”

See bias-aware-verification-protocol for the four-pass protocol that operationalizes this, and ai-automation-biases for the bias-cluster view.

Anthropomorphism as a hidden tax

There is a real and replicated empathy effect: anthropomorphic chatbots elicit longer, deeper, more personal user interactions than neutral ones, and users rate them as more trustworthy. Experimental work on perceived empathy in AI (e.g., Mousavi et al., AAAI 2025, arXiv:2411.12877) has documented the behavioural asymmetry. A Nature Human Behaviour 2025 study on human-vs-AI empathy comparison is another reasonable entry point. Specific numeric claims in popular summaries of these studies should be treated cautiously — the literature is young and the effect sizes vary.

The cost is that empathetic interfaces bypass analytic filters. Affect-laden engagement recruits social-cognitive resources that neutral, tool-like interfaces do not. For instrumental work — code review, factual verification, high-stakes decisions — the conversational register is a load multiplier, not a friendliness feature. The design implication is not “make AI cold” but “match interaction register to task register”: warm for support, neutral for instruments.

What actually reduces the burden

Pulled from HCI research and oxzo’s existing notes. Reduction comes from design changes, not from demanding more human resilience.

Direct manipulation over prompt-juggling. Shneiderman’s 1983 direct-manipulation principles — visible objects, reversible actions, rapid incremental feedback — have been re-applied to LLM interfaces. Masson et al.’s DirectGPT (CHI 2024) layered UI affordances over a chat model and reported users were ~50% faster, used ~50% fewer prompts, and those prompts were ~72% shorter than baseline chat. Prompt-engineering fatigue is a symptom of broken HCI, not an inherent cost of LLMs. See also agentic-vs-autocomplete-coding for the mode-selection version of this argument.

Ambient integration, not intrusive assistance. The healthcare scribe pattern generalizes. AI that fades into the workflow and produces artifacts reviewed at natural breakpoints imposes far less load than AI that interjects mid-task. For web development, the analogue is async code-review bots vs. in-editor autocomplete in a high-stakes file.

ISO 9241-210 principles applied to AI surfaces. Suitability, self-descriptiveness, controllability, error tolerance — the HCI fundamentals predate LLMs and still apply. Self-descriptiveness (clear audit trails, visible reasoning) and error tolerance (undo, prompt-iteration history) are the two most commonly violated by current AI UX.

Structured verification that scales with stakes. Not every AI output needs deep scrutiny. A tiered protocol — light heuristic checks for low-risk outputs, deep audit for high-stakes ones — is how you avoid burning vigilance on cases that don’t warrant it. The bias-aware-verification-protocol encodes this; hitl-progressive-autonomy is the agent-fleet version.

Automated reasoning / external grounding. Pushing factual verification from the human reviewer onto tool-assisted systems (automated-reasoning checks, RAG with source display, test-as-spec) reduces supervisory load by letting the machine carry the proof obligation. AWS Bedrock’s Automated Reasoning Checks advertise up to 99% verification accuracy against formalized domain policies — vendor-marketing figure, not an independent benchmark, but the architectural direction is sound.

Match accountability to authority. If you’re the named human in the loop, your review capacity needs to be real, not nominal. This is a management and staffing question as much as a UX one. Anti-rubber-stamp design is the interface side; adequate review time is the organizational side.

State-triage the task class. Per the capacity-state section: route work by current capacity, and treat “do this manually” and “defer this” as first-class options rather than concessions. The three moves that survive a bad day, in leverage order — pre-commit acceptance criteria before generating (converts depleted-state review from constructing a judgment into matching against a written list, the cheapest working-memory reduction available); externalize the spec into a plan file so it isn’t reloaded from memory on every output; and reduce the number of live surfaces, since each agent hand-off costs working memory.

Cognitive forcing functions — and the reason they need to be structural. Buçinca, Malaya & Gajos (2021) tested interventions that interrupt the reflexive acceptance of an AI suggestion and force deliberate engagement. They reduced overreliance on incorrect AI output — and participants trusted and preferred them less than frictionless explainable-AI interfaces, with the effect strongest among high need-for-cognition users, who are exactly the population most likely to believe they don’t need the friction. The design lesson generalizes past their task: the intervention that works is the one the operator will want to remove, so it has to live in a template, a checklist, a hook, or a plan file — anywhere other than in-the-moment discipline, which is the resource already depleted. This is the empirical backing for the “pre-commit acceptance criteria before generating” lever below.

Break dosing, correctly sized. Albulescu et al. (2022), a meta-analysis of 22 studies, found micro-breaks (≤10 minutes) reliably boost vigor (d=.36) and reduce fatigue (d=.35), both p<.001 — while the effect on performance was not significant (d=.16, p=.116), and a meta-regression showed longer breaks produced larger performance benefits. Micro-breaks manage subjective state; recovering capacity after a depleting verification block needs more than ten minutes. Break protocols that treat all breaks as interchangeable get the wellbeing benefit and none of the capacity one.

Scaffolds over skill. Anand & Idan (2026) report that a conceptual-roadmap scaffold reduced outcome variance across users by ~40% without lowering mean performance. Artifacts carry competence that training has to install per-person and that fatigue removes — the same logic as sidi-stress-cognition’s protocols-over-judgment counter. trainability-of-ai-use develops this into the intervention argument.

Operational counters — skills and persistent infrastructure

The patterns above describe what should exist in tools. Below are two concrete invocable skills plus one persistent file, each tied to a specific mechanism named above. Both skills rely on the AI accurately surfacing its own uncertainty, which is exactly what output-side bias fingerprints flag as unreliable — so these are partial counters, not full fixes. Their purpose is to make the invocation surface cheap so the deliberate act of reviewing happens at all.

They complement each other on different granularities: /reviewers-map runs per substantive output, /commitment-ledger runs per session. The persistent log file attacks the mechanism directly by converting ephemeral chat state into a searchable audit trail over time.

Reviewer’s-map skill — counters cognitive decoupling

Invoke. /reviewers-map [path] — if a path is given, maps that file; otherwise infers the most recent substantive output in the session. Skill definition at .claude/skills/reviewers-map/SKILL.md.

When to use. Any time the AI’s output will be acted on, committed, or published — code diffs, note drafts, research synthesis, PR descriptions. Skip for throwaway queries.

What it does. Produces a verification map alongside the output, so the reviewer does not reverse-engineer the reasoning from fluent text. Fluency decouples “understood the text” from “understood the logic”; the map reattaches them by handing the reviewer a ranked target list instead of an undifferentiated wall of prose.

Underlying template (what the skill tells Claude to produce — reproduced here for reference):

After producing your primary output, append a "Reviewer's map":

1. Load-bearing claims — bullet list of assertions the output depends on
   (names, numbers, dates, API methods, file paths, causal claims).
   Tag each [V] verifiable from provided context, [U] unverified, [I] inferred.
2. Least-confident spots — three places you are least sure, ordered by how
   much the rest of the output leans on them. One sentence each on what
   would falsify them.
3. What to check first — if the reviewer has five minutes, the three
   highest-leverage verifications.
4. What I did not do — files not read, sources not fetched, tests not run,
   steps deferred.

Do not pad. If a section would be empty, say "none."

Failure mode to watch for. A confident-sounding map that mirrors the output’s confidence rather than genuinely surfacing uncertainty. If everything is tagged [V] and nothing is [U] or [I], the map is probably sycophantic rather than honest — re-run with an explicit instruction to tag at least one claim as unverified or inferred, or accept that the output may not warrant the map at all.

Commitment-ledger skill — counters accountability without authorship

Invoke. /commitment-ledger — no arguments; operates on session state (git status, conversation history). Skill definition at .claude/skills/commitment-ledger/SKILL.md.

When to use. End of a working session that produced changes or decisions. Run it before closing the laptop, not at the start of the next day — the point is to lock in the ledger while context is still warm.

What it does. Converts diffuse “what did I just agree to” anxiety into an explicit list of files touched, claims implicitly accepted, and deferrals. The accountability mechanism is that you carry professional responsibility for outputs you did not produce line by line; the ledger makes the responsibility surface concrete rather than a low-grade background load. At the end of its output, the skill offers to append to [[commitments-log|journal/commitments-log.md]] for a persistent audit trail — see the next subsection.

Underlying template (reproduced for reference):

Produce a session closeout. Be factual and terse.

1. Files touched — each file changed or created, ≤10-word description
   of what changed.
2. Claims I stated that you accepted without external verification —
   assertions I stated as fact where you did not independently check
   (library behavior, API shape, benchmark numbers, "this pattern is
   standard"). If none, say "none."
3. Decisions recorded — choices made this session (architecture,
   naming, scope cuts). Name the rejected alternative if one was
   discussed.
4. Deferred items — anything flagged "will need to check later,"
   "assuming for now," or "TODO." If none, say "none."
5. What to check first tomorrow — the two or three highest-stakes
   items from sections 2-4.

Do not rehash the conversation. Do not editorialize. Empty section → "none."

Failure mode to watch for. The ledger sliding into a sycophantic progress narration. The point is load transfer, not accomplishment summary. If section 2 is consistently “none” across sessions, either the work was genuinely low-claim or the AI is underreporting — the latter is more likely, and the fix is to re-prompt with examples of the kinds of implicit claims that got accepted (e.g., “you said Zod’s .parse throws on invalid input — did you actually check?”).

Commitments log — persistent infrastructure

File. journal/commitments-log.md, append-only. Each /commitment-ledger invocation optionally appends under a timestamped section header.

What it does structurally. Converts ephemeral session state into a searchable audit trail. Ledgers that live only in chat history evaporate the moment context clears; a rolling log file makes last week’s assumptions, decisions, and deferrals walkable. This is the move that directly attacks the accountability without authorship mechanism rather than mitigating it per-session — the assistant can grep the log before starting a new session to see what was deferred, and the user has a real audit surface if anything produced a regression.

Rules, summarized (full format in the file’s header):

  • Append-only. Never rewrite or edit past entries. Corrections go in a new dated entry.
  • No de-duplication. Repeated claims across sessions are themselves a signal.
  • Terse, not reflective. The daily note is where reflection lives; this is an audit log.
  • Per-session opt-in. Not every session warrants persistence.

Failure mode to watch for. A log that grows into wallpaper. Two signs: (a) the user stops reading past weeks when starting new sessions, (b) the ledger gets appended automatically on every trivial session out of habit. Countermeasure: the skill asks per-session before appending; if you find yourself saying “yes” to everything, the threshold is too low.

Counterpoint worth holding onto

The burden framing can overstate the case in two ways. First, the empirical record on aggregate productivity with AI remains mixed-but-positive across most developer-tool studies; the burden is real but it does not wipe out the gains. METR’s slowdown is the sharpest counter-example on record and it is narrow by construction — experienced contributors, mature codebases with high quality bars, early-2025 tooling — with wide confidence intervals and a selection problem METR itself flags. Do not read it as “AI makes developers slower”; read it as “the cost is real, it is context-dependent, and it is not perceived.” Second, the cognitive-atrophy literature is still young, and its effect sizes (Gerlich’s r ≈ -0.42 total effect) are meaningful without being catastrophic. The risk is heavy, unreflective substitution, not any use.

The stronger framing: this is an ergonomic problem with a design solution, not a moral problem requiring users to “use AI less.” Treating it as the former keeps the intervention surface pointed at tools, workflows, and staffing; treating it as the latter tends to collapse into guilt and optimization anxiety, which are themselves forms of cognitive load.
