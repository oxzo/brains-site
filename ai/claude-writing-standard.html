A Research-Grounded Writing Standard for Claude

The staged pipeline in ai-writing-workflow steers style with a voice card distilled from writing samples you own. That works when your own writing is the target. It fails when it isn’t: if the person briefing the model writes worse than the model does, imitating them caps the output at their level. This note covers the other case. The model writes to an external standard — reading-comprehension findings plus vendor prompting guidance — and the person supplies substance and judgment, not style.

Model-specific guidance and the benchmark reading below are as of 2026-10-14. The clarity rules rest on older, slower-moving research.

Split the job by what each side is good at

The person suppliesThe model supplies
What they know: facts, decisions, the raw material, messy notes in any languageSentences, paragraphing, transitions
Who the reader is and what that reader already knowsWord choice against the standard
What the reader should understand or do afterwardsA first draft, then a critique of that draft
Yes/no on each proposed edit: “clearer” or “changed my meaning”The fixes

Drafting ability is usually not the bottleneck. On Surge AI’s Hemingway-bench, which professional writers judge blind (a single vendor’s benchmark, not independently reproducible), Claude Fable 5 ranked first (1110) when read on 2026-10-14. Gemini 3.7 Flash (1105), Fable 5.1 (1095) and Gemini 3.8 Flash (1093) followed, all inside overlapping ranges. The page carries no date, so freshness is unknown. Per-job model choice lives in writing-model-selection-2026-08. The more common failure, on this note’s reading rather than any measurement, is upstream: a vague brief produces generic prose, and the person’s sense of what the reader knows is miscalibrated (the curse of knowledge, below).

The clarity rules and their evidence

The research behind these rules measures comprehension — reading time, recall, felt ease — not beauty or persuasion. Each rule names its evidence tier. The full treatment lives in the linked notes; this table indexes them and does not restate them.

Name the referent instead of “this/it” across a sentence boundary; reuse one term per conceptBritton & Gülgöz 1991; rare-word cost fades by about the third encounter, so rotating synonyms pays it againDirectNone found for expository text
Keep connectives (because, however, therefore)Discourse-comprehension research: markers cut reading time and help recall, most for struggling and L2 readersConsistent finding, no single canonical citation (readability-mechanics)—
Prefer the common word; use a technical term only if it does real work and recursRayner & Duffy 1986 (rare words fixate longer); Oppenheimer 2006 (needless complexity lowers judged author intelligence); Bullock et al. 2019 (jargon hurts fluency even when defined)Mechanism plus perception studiesJargon raises credibility expert-to-expert (Zimmermann & Jucks 2018)

What the evidence does not support: most Strunk-and-White-style injunctions (“omit needless words”, “never use passive”), an optimal heading depth or section length, and readability formulas as targets rather than signals. The rules make prose clear. They don’t make it elegant, and no reading study measures elegance. Taste stays with the editor pass and real readers.

Levers specific to current Claude models

All from Anthropic’s prompting documentation, read 2026-10-14.

  • Define mannered prose. Fable 5.1’s prose uses few stock phrases but can run denser than Fable 5’s, with longer sentences and fewer paragraph breaks. Anthropic recommends naming the anti-pattern, preferably in the user message:

    Mannered prose substitutes metaphor and flourish for direct statement. Instead of “a parameter worth varying,” the mannered writer produces “a dial worth turning.” Instead of “this point still matters,” they write “this point earns its keep.” The phrases exist to display the writer, not to convey the idea, and readers can tell. That is why mannered prose irritates: it makes the reader work harder so the writer can perform. It is also imprecise. Metaphors drag in connotations the writer did not choose and cannot control. The fix is to say what you mean. When a literal phrase is available, use it.

    The documented short form: “Please remove all mannered prose.”

  • Drop legacy anti-formatting rules on Fable 5.1. Earlier models overused bullets and bold, and prompts accumulated rules against it. Fable 5.1 leans the other way, so Anthropic advises replacing those rules with a statement of when formatting helps.

  • Ask for length on Opus 5. Its replies and the documents it writes run longer than earlier Opus models’. Lowering effort cuts thinking, not visible length. Anthropic’s sample: “Match the length of written documents to what the task needs: cover the substance, but do not pad with filler sections, redundant summaries, or boilerplate.” Opus 5 also verifies its own work unprompted, so remove “double-check” instructions.

  • General levers for all current models: say what to do rather than what to avoid (“write in smoothly flowing prose paragraphs”, not “no markdown”); give the reason behind an instruction; match the prompt’s own formatting to the output you want; and use 3–5 examples wrapped in <example> tags.

Borrowed exemplars instead of your own samples

To aim above the standard’s clarity floor, take 2–3 pieces of the same genre that you think are excellent. Have the model extract observable traits — sentence-length spread, paragraph openings, how much it explains, how it ends — and write to that trait list. This is the voice-card step of ai-writing-workflow pointed at someone else’s work. Ask for traits rather than “write like [living author]”: traits are easier to control, and imitating a named writer raises its own concerns.

The standard as a prompt

Reader: <who, what they already know>
Goal: <what they should understand or do after reading>
Length: <roughly>

Raw notes (messy is fine; use only these facts; mark gaps as [NEED INFO]):
<notes>

Clarity rules:
- Start each sentence with what the reader already knows; put new info at the end.
- Name things explicitly instead of "this/it" across sentences; use the same term
  for the same concept every time.
- Keep connectives (because, however, therefore) that show how ideas relate.
- Prefer common words. Use a technical term only if it does real work and recurs.
- Keep subject and verb close. Vary sentence length; combine choppy short sentences.
- Point first, with one line of context if the reader is new to the topic.
- Assume the reader knows less than you think.
Please remove all mannered prose.

If something important is missing, ask up to 3 questions first. Then draft.

Then run a critique before accepting anything:

Act as a demanding editor who did not write this. List the 5 weakest passages:
quote each, say what is wrong for the target reader, and propose the smallest
fix. Do not rewrite yet.

The person accepts or rejects each fix, then asks for the accepted ones to be applied. For the full critique protocol, cross-family review and claim audit, see ai-writing-workflow. The vault’s skills run the same rules mechanically: skill-readability-scan and skill-density-scan diagnose, skill-prose-surgery applies the sentence-level fixes, and skill-unslop strips AI register.

Failure modes

  • The prompt is untested as a whole. Each rule has its own evidence. Whether this bundle beats a bare prompt on a given model and genre has not been measured. The blind bake-off in writing-model-selection-2026-08 is the way to find out, and until then treat the prompt as a reasoned default.
  • Rules flatten prose. Writing to a checklist produces competent, flat text — the rubric-homogenization failure named in ai-writing-workflow. The rules set a clarity floor, and they don’t guarantee anything above it.
  • Rules have counter-cases. Every row above has one. Apply a rule where its symptom appears, not across a whole text.
  • The judge can’t judge style. If the person can’t tell good prose from bad, the editor pass checks meaning, not taste. LLM judges agree with human creative preferences only about 73% of the time (LitBench, via writing-model-selection-2026-08), so a model grading its own style is a weak substitute. Showing the draft to a real reader from the target audience settles what neither can.
  • The model-specific levers expire. They describe specific model versions. Re-read Anthropic’s per-model pages when the model changes.
