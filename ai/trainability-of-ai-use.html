Trainability of AI Use

“Train the team to use AI effectively” is sold as one problem. It is at least three, with training economics that differ by orders of magnitude, and the industry prices all three at the cost of the cheapest one.

This note answers how hard is it to train people — the question cognitive-burden-of-ai-use raises but doesn’t address, and the question the design-adoption cluster (design-side-ai-adoption-stall, design-side-ai-onramp) hits from the organizational side. As of August 2026.

The three-component split

ComponentWhat it isTraining horizonWho teaches it
Interaction literacyPrompt formulation, tool selection, mode choice, context managementDays–weeksThis is what workshops teach
Verification capacityDomain expertise sufficient to judge whether the output is rightYearsNobody, as “AI training” — it is the domain
State managementRecognizing your own current capacity and matching task class to itNever taught—

Almost all commercial AI training targets row 1. Row 2 is the binding constraint. Row 3 is invisible.

The split matters because the three fail differently. Bad interaction literacy produces frustration and abandonment — visible, self-correcting, cheap to fix. Missing verification capacity produces confident acceptance of wrong output — invisible, self-reinforcing, and the failure lands downstream of the person who caused it.

Row 1 is real and separable — the AIC evidence

The strongest case that interaction literacy is a genuine, distinct skill comes from Anand & Idan (2026), who name it AI Interaction Competence (AIC): the ability to elicit, filter, and verify model outputs, decomposed into prompt formulation, output evaluation, and iterative refinement.

Their reported finding: GenAI access improved mean performance ~17%, but the distribution of gains was highly uneven — and the gains were not predicted by GPA or prior knowledge. They were predicted by AIC. High-AIC participants realized outsized gains; low-AIC participants saw limited or negative marginal returns.

Two things follow, and they point in opposite directions:

  • Optimistic: if the predictor is a skill rather than a trait or a credential, it is in principle trainable, and the population that gains most is not the population that already had the most.
  • Pessimistic: the negative-marginal-returns tail means there is a competence threshold below which AI subtracts. Deploying AI to an untrained population is not “some gain for everyone, more for some.” For part of the distribution it is a loss.

Caveat that matters. This is a working paper, in education and knowledge-work settings, and AIC is a construct its own authors defined. “People good at using AI gain more from AI” is close to tautological unless AIC is measured independently of the outcome it predicts. Treat the shape as informative and the effect size as provisional. This is the note’s weakest load-bearing evidence.

Row 2 is the constraint, and it isn’t AI training

Verification capacity is domain expertise. You cannot workshop it.

The distributional evidence makes this concrete. Dell’Acqua et al.’s pre-registered field experiment with 758 BCG consultants (~7% of the firm’s individual-contributor workforce) found that on 18 realistic tasks within AI’s capability frontier, consultants below the average performance threshold improved 43% against their own baseline while those above it improved 17%. The same inversion appears across the literature — the call-centre and writing studies in model-access-inequality report the same direction.

The uncomfortable reading: training returns are highest exactly where verification capacity is lowest. The people who gain most from AI are the people least equipped to catch it when it’s wrong. That is not an argument against training novices; it is an argument that the gain and the risk arrive on the same people at the same time, and the interventions have to be designed together.

The same paper supplies the reason this can’t be solved by task selection alone. Its central concept is the jagged technological frontier: AI performs excellently on some tasks and poorly on others that look, to a human, comparably difficult. The boundary is irregular and it is not visible from the outside. Deciding which side of it a given task falls on is itself a verification-capacity judgment — which is why “just use AI where it’s good” is not advice a novice can act on.

This is also the mechanism under the design-adoption stall. The designers’ quality objection isn’t resistance — it’s the correct output of people who can verify visual quality being handed a tool whose output they can’t verify in the code domain. design-side-ai-onramp’s step 0b (does the constraint substrate exist?) is asking the row-2 question.

Row 3 — the capacity-state component nobody teaches

The burden is not a constant. It’s a function of the user’s current executive capacity, and it has a floor. See cognitive-burden-of-ai-use for the mechanism and the load-floor argument; the training implication is what belongs here:

Every AI workflow is designed and tested by people at peak capacity, and used by people at median capacity. Tool designers, internal champions, and the person writing the onboarding doc are all in an unrepresentative state — engaged, rested, motivated by novelty. The workflow that only works when you’re sharp doesn’t work.

The training move this implies is not more training. It is scaffolding that survives low capacity — see the intervention section below.

The feedback channel is broken

The most damaging finding for any self-directed learning model: people cannot perceive their own AI-induced cost.

  • METR’s RCT (2025) — 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 real issues on mature repositories they knew well. With AI tools allowed, tasks took 19% longer (CI +2% to +39%). The developers had predicted a 24% speedup, and after experiencing the slowdown still estimated AI had made them 20% faster.
  • Vella & Blincoe (2026) — longitudinal, two surveys six months apart, matched cohort of 95 professional engineers. Productivity perception held stable at 84% reporting improvement at both time points, while the proportion reporting worsened developer experience nearly doubled, 14% → 27%, with flow state and cognitive load specifically named as what eroded. 82% reported spending less time writing code, and the authors name a new category — supervisory engineering work — for the direction, evaluation, and correction that replaced it.

Read together: the benefit is perceived and stable; the cost accrues on an axis the person isn’t measuring. Ordinary learning-by-doing requires a feedback signal. Here the signal is inverted for productivity and absent for load.

Honest scope on METR. Its own 2026-02 follow-up contextualises rather than retracts the result: a late-2025 re-run gave −18% (CI −38% to +9%) for returning developers and −4% (CI −15% to +9%) for new ones, which METR itself calls “only very weak evidence” because 30–50% of developers declined to submit tasks they wouldn’t attempt manually, systematically excluding the highest-value AI cases. The experiment is being redesigned. The slowdown magnitude is genuinely uncertain and probably shrinking; the perception gap is the robust part and is what this section rests on.

The pipeline erodes itself

The training problem has a second-order version. The capacity that row 2 requires is degraded by the tool that requires it.

  • Gerlich (2025) — AI use → cognitive offloading → reduced critical thinking, mediated rather than direct (see cognitive-burden-of-ai-use for figures and the moral-panic caveat).
  • Liu et al. (2026) — participants given AI assistance showed reduced persistence and worse independent performance on similar tasks afterward. The proposed mechanism is that immediate availability of a solution atrophies willingness to struggle, which is the process that builds durable capability. (Preprint; direction reported, effect sizes not extracted.)

If verification capacity is the binding constraint, and heavy unreflective AI use erodes the struggle that produces it, then scaling AI adoption faster than you build verification capacity is a self-limiting strategy. augmented-vs-trained-thinking is the personal-practice version of this trade; higher-order-thinking-under-ai is the skills version.

What actually works — scaffold, don’t train

The single most actionable finding in this literature: Anand & Idan’s scaffolding intervention (conceptual roadmaps) reduced outcome variance by ~40% without lowering mean performance.

That is the intervention shape. Not “make everyone competent” — make the workflow tolerant of varying competence. It converts a skill problem into an artifact problem, and artifacts survive states that skills don’t. The same logic drives sidi-stress-cognition’s counter to stress-degraded decisions: pre-built protocols are the cognition still available when capacity is low.

Ordered by leverage:

  1. Ship the roadmap, not the workshop. A written scaffold — acceptance criteria, a review checklist, a worked example of a good and a bad output — carries competence that training has to install per-person and that fatigue removes.
  2. Pre-commit verification criteria before generating. Written while fresh, the depleted-state task becomes matching against a list (recognition) rather than constructing a judgment. Cheapest available working-memory reduction.
  3. Assign first tasks where the person’s existing verification capacity already reaches. This is design-side-ai-onramp’s “first exposure from the High column” restated in row-2 terms: never make someone’s first AI task one they cannot judge.
  4. Teach the abstain condition explicitly. “Don’t use it below this capacity, above this stakes level, or outside your verification range” is a teachable rule and is almost never taught, because it doesn’t look like adoption.
  5. Build the org-side oracle. Tests, linters, gates — machine-carried proof obligations reduce how much verification capacity each individual must personally hold. dora-2025-ai-capabilities-model’s amplifier effect: AI magnifies existing organizational strengths and weaknesses, so the substrate has to exist first.

Note the direction: every item moves load out of the person and into an artifact or a system. None of them is “be better at prompting.”

What the market is selling instead

DataCamp’s 2026 survey of 500+ enterprise leaders (US/UK) reports that organisations with a structured upskilling programme are roughly twice as likely to report significant AI ROI (42% vs 21% overall), that only 35% have a mature organisation-wide AI-literacy programme, and — the interesting pair — that 77% offer some kind of AI training while 59% still report an AI skills gap. (Vendor survey; DataCamp sells AI training. Treat the ROI correlation as interested, and note it cannot separate “training causes ROI” from “organisations that are good at this do both.“)

That last pair is what the three-component split predicts: most organisations are buying row 1 and needing row 2, so the training is delivered, absorbed, and the gap stays open. The survey’s own explanation is different — it blames training models built for slower-moving skills — which is a claim about delivery format where this note’s is about which capacity was the constraint. Both could be true; they are not the same diagnosis, and only one of them is fixed by buying more courses.

Counterargument

The strongest objection is that this is a snapshot artifact. Every study cited runs on 2023–2026 tools. The whole framing rests on verification being expensive. If models improve to where verification collapses to spot-checking — or where self-verification is reliable enough to carry its own proof obligation — then row 2 stops being the constraint, the training problem becomes row 1 only, and it is a workshop. ai-capability-watch-log tracks the indicators that would signal this; self-verification is the one to watch. METR’s own follow-up already suggests the 2025 measurement may not describe 2026.

Second objection: consensus echo. “Verification and judgment are what survive” is simultaneously this vault’s position and the mainstream consulting position. skills-to-train-for-ai-future flags that agreement as a yellow flag rather than confirmation. The three-component split is vulnerable to the same critique — it is a satisfying frame, and satisfying frames get adopted for reasons unrelated to being true.

Third: measurement quality. Two of the four load-bearing studies are preprints, one key survey is self-report, one is vendor-interested, and the strongest construct (AIC) has a circularity risk named above. The direction is consistent across independent designs, which is the main reason to credit it. No individual figure here should be quoted as settled.
