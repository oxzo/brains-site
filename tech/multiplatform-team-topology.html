Multiplatform Team Topology: Who Owns the Shared Parts

The companion question to multiplatform-app-architecture-options. That note frames the architecture as five independently choosable axes; this one covers the constraint that couples them: every sharing decision creates a shared artifact, and a shared artifact needs an owner your org chart can actually produce.

Why this is not a sixth axis

The five axes work as a frame because they are independent technical choices. Org structure is not a sixth choice alongside them — it is the medium the other five are chosen in. Conway stated the mechanism in 1968:

“Any organization that designs a system (defined broadly) will produce a design whose structure is a copy of the organization’s communication structure.” — Melvin Conway, “How Do Committees Invent?”, Datamation, April 1968 (quote as given on melconway.com, retrieved 2026-08-29)

Martin Fowler’s gloss names the consequence for sharing decisions specifically: “Conway understood that software coupling is enabled and encouraged by human communication,” and where architecture clashes with org structure, “module interactions that were designed to be straightforward become complicated, because the teams responsible for them don’t work together well” (martinfowler.com/bliki/ConwaysLaw). A shared core is deliberate coupling between platforms; it holds only if the communication structure behind it holds.

The inverse Conway maneuver — “change the communication patterns of the designers to encourage the desired software architecture,” coined by Jonny LeRoy and Matt Simons in the December 2010 Cutter IT Journal (attribution via Fowler’s bliki) — is the org-side lever: pick the architecture, then reshape teams to fit it. The bound on the maneuver is the labor market. You can redraw the org chart; you cannot redraw who is hireable and retainable — which is why multiplatform-app-architecture-options ranks hiring as driver #1 and why the failure evidence below is mostly hiring evidence.

Vocabulary: the four team types

Team Topologies (Skelton & Pais, 2019) is the standard vocabulary. The four types, as defined on teamtopologies.com/key-concepts (retrieved 2026-08-29):

  • Stream-aligned team — “aligned to a flow of work from (usually) a segment of the business domain”
  • Platform team — “a grouping of other team types that provide a compelling internal product to accelerate delivery by Stream-aligned teams”
  • Enabling team — “helps a Stream-aligned team to overcome obstacles. Also detects missing capabilities.”
  • Complicated Subsystem team — “where significant mathematics/calculation/technical expertise is needed”

Applied to the three-client problem (the mapping is authored here, not from the book):

  • A shared TS/KMP core consumed by client teams is a platform in this sense — an internal product with the client teams as customers, served “X-as-a-Service.”
  • A Rust core behind UniFFI bindings leans complicated-subsystem: the point of that lane is concentrating scarce expertise behind a boundary so client teams don’t need it (rust-shared-core-enterprise).
  • Design-system and build-tooling teams are platform teams whether or not anyone calls them that.

Team Topologies’ own warning applies directly to shared cores: the “Thinnest Viable Platform” concept exists because “most internal platforms become bloated monstrosities that slow teams down rather than accelerate progress” (same page). A shared core is subject to the same bloat gravity as any internal platform.

The implied team shape per archetype

The combination archetypes from multiplatform-app-architecture-options each imply a team topology. This column is the authored core of this note:

ArchetypeImplied team shapeThe org bet you’re making
JS everywhereStream-aligned feature teams over one codebase; no sharing boundary between teamsEveryone is a TS generalist; web-platform and native-platform depth atrophies
Native pair + webPer-surface stream-aligned teams + a core-owning teamYou can fund the core team permanently, not just for the migration
All native, share the coreThree platform silos + a complicated-subsystem core teamYou can hire and retain for four skill pools, including the rarest one
Thin clientsBackend team owns all logic; client teams consume a contractThe API spec is the coordination point; client teams accept being downstream
Web-first companionOne web team; native shells are packaging workMobile stays a companion — if it becomes the product, the topology is wrong

Two structural observations fall out of the table:

  1. Sharing inside one team is cheap; sharing across teams is what Conway taxes. The JS-everywhere archetype works for small teams not because JS is special but because there is no team boundary running through the shared code. The moment the org grows enough to split teams, the boundary has to land somewhere — and wherever it lands becomes the interface that degrades first.
  2. The pure states are stable; the hybrid state is where org costs concentrate. Airbnb, from direct experience: “The path of a world that is 100% native or 100% React Native is relatively straightforward. However, once you have a mix within your codebase, many new issues arise. How do you split up your teams? How do teams collaborate? … How do you hire and allocate resources across your organization?” (Building a Cross-Platform Mobile Team, Gabriel Peal, 2018-06-19, read in full 2026-08-29). Every migration between archetypes passes through that hybrid state — so the transition cost is an org cost more than a technical one, and it is paid even if you eventually retreat.

Who owns the shared core

The practical question every sharing decision on axis 2 reduces to. Three ownership options recur; the failure modes are the same ones the design-system cluster has already documented one layer up (design-system-governance).

1. Dedicated core team (platform model). The core is an internal product with an SLA and a roadmap. This is the only model with named production evidence in this cluster’s research passes — but it needs standing funding, not project funding. The design-system parallel is exact: Nathan Curtis, across 80+ design systems, reports the number thriving without centrally allocated people as “Zero. Zero. Zero percent.” A shared code core is the same economics one layer down: unfunded, it rots into the thing product teams route around (design-system-failure-modes gives the rule-of-thumb 12–24-month timeline for the design-system version). Risk: the core team becomes a bottleneck and loses touch with client-team friction — the same centralized-model weakness as in the governance table.

2. Strongest consumer owns it. The team that uses the core most (usually the largest client team) stewards it. Cheap to start; drifts predictably — the core evolves toward the owner’s platform, and the other clients become second-class consumers whose PRs wait. (Failure mode reasoned from the Conway mechanism and the design-system parallel; no named public postmortem for this specific shape found in this pass.)

3. Federated stewardship (“everyone owns it”). Contribution from all client teams, no central owner. This is the model Curtis publicly retracted for design systems — “Favoring federated was and is wrong … federated is not a choice, it’s a facet. In practice, it’s never pursued first and never without central investment” — and the mechanism is the same here (an unfunded shared artifact, contribution without an owner), so the correction should transfer: federated contribution is a maturity phase a funded core earns, not a way to avoid funding one. See design-system-governance for the full retraction.

What the documented failures were actually about

The two best-documented shared-core walk-aways are org failures wearing technical clothes:

Dropbox (shared C++ mobile core, abandoned by 2019). Eyal Guthmann’s postmortem (dropbox.tech, 2019-08-14, re-read 2026-08-29) itemizes the costs: a custom build system that “was a big drag on our resources as it needed to be constantly updated to support changes in two build systems”; a hiring-and-retention wall — a year-long specialist search failed, and “mobile developers simply did not want to work on a C++ project”; and the summary judgment, “this overhead ended up being more expensive than just writing the code twice.” Toolchain maintenance and hiring are both team-shape problems: the architecture assumed a standing C++-platform team the labor market wouldn’t staff.

Airbnb (React Native, sunset June 2018). The org half of the five-part series (part 3, cited above) lists the mechanisms: the technology was polarizing (“wildly different opinions varying from praising it as a silver bullet … to being wholly against any use …”); root-cause attribution blurred across the native/RN boundary (“it wasn’t clear which team an issue should be attributed”); hiring perception suffered (“many Android and iOS engineers were hesitant to apply”); and split teams fragmented expertise (“the code became fragmented … engineers no longer had the expertise to work across the entire flow”). Note the caveat Airbnb later appended to the post, still on the page: the technical points reflect end-of-2017 React Native and should be revalidated; “[a]ny organizational points should also be considered within the context, size, and culture of your organization.”

Both are big-company stories with parallel native teams already in place — the hybrid state from the table above. Neither says shared cores fail; both say shared cores fail when the team shape they imply can’t be staffed or funded. Which is the note’s claim in one sentence.

The checklist form

Before committing to a sharing decision, answer for the shared artifact it creates:

  1. Who owns it in eighteen months? A named team with standing funding, or “the team that built it” (which is a migration crew, not an owner)?
  2. Can you hire for the implied skill pools — including the rarest one — at your location, comp band, and brand? (Dropbox’s veto.)
  3. Which team boundary does the sharing boundary cross? If the same team owns both sides, the coupling is cheap. If not, that interface is where drift starts.
  4. What happens to the org during the migration, given the transition passes through the hybrid state even on a later retreat? (Airbnb’s cost was concentrated there.)
  5. Is the core scoped as a thinnest-viable platform, with a mechanism for saying no to scope, or will it accrete until client teams route around it?

What we couldn’t establish

  • Any named public postmortem for the “strongest consumer owns it” model. Its failure mode below is reasoned from the Conway mechanism plus the design-system parallel, not observed in a published case.
  • Any multiplatform-native evidence for the federated-ownership correction. Curtis’s retraction is design-systems evidence, transferred here on the argument that both are unfunded shared artifacts. The transfer is an argued claim, not a measured one.
  • Team sizes, funding levels, or cost figures for any of the three ownership models. No source in this pass gives numbers, so the note can say which shapes exist and how they fail, but not what one costs.
  • How often the hybrid state is actually transited versus avoided. The claim that transition cost concentrates there rests on one company’s account of its own migration.
  • Recent walk-away evidence. Both documented cases are 2018–2019, from large orgs that already had parallel native teams — the same recency and selection problem multiplatform-app-architecture-options flags for the technology-side evidence. Airbnb’s own appended caveat says the organizational points “should also be considered within the context, size, and culture of your organization.” Treat both as existence proofs of a failure mode, not as base rates.

Scope guard

This note covers the org structure implied by sharing decisions — ownership, staffing shape, governance of the shared artifact. General org design (reporting lines, funding models, team sizing) is out of scope here; if that material ever earns a note it belongs in knowledge/general/, not this cluster.
