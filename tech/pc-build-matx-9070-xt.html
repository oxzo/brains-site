Why mATX and not Mini-ITX

The 9070 XT is a 304 W part. In ITX volumes that heat leaves via fan RPM, and quiet cards are physically fat cards — so silence pushes volume back up. The premium ITX cases are also thermally compromised: the Fractal Terra caps the CPU cooler at 48 mm once a ≥50 mm GPU forces Max GPU mode, the Mood’s fabric restricts airflow, and the Ncase M1 is discontinued. mATX also returns ~€150–250 in ITX board and SFX PSU premiums.

Design rule: large volume, many fan mounts, everything spinning slowly. Airflow beats sound-damping foam for a 300 W GPU — foam muffles a fan working hard, airflow means it never has to.

Why not a “premium quiet” case instead (considered and rejected 2026-07-27)

The category barely exists at this size, and the reason is physical. Dampened cases work by solid foam-lined panels, restricted intake, and enough volume to run big fans slowly — a formula that needs volume (Define 7 ≈ 45 L, Silent Base 802 ≈ 55 L). At 26 L with a 304 W GPU, restricted intake makes the fans spin faster, and fan noise at source exceeds what foam removes downstream. The one dampened mATX case, Fractal’s Pop Mini Silent, is documented as running hot with powerful parts and suited to efficient mid-range components.

The A3-mATX’s real weakness is cable management, not acoustics — GN: “no channels or covers and there isn’t really any space behind the motherboard. Tie-down points are limited,” while otherwise rating it easy to work with and solidly built. A premium swap buys assembly experience, not quiet.

Fractal Design North (€174.90, Jimm’s) is the only serious alternative and fails on three counts: it is 45.1 L vs 26.3 L (there is no mATX North — it is an ATX mid-tower that accepts mATX boards), its cooler clearance drops to 145 mm with the front fan bracket fitted against the NH-U12A’s 158 mm, and it is itself a mesh airflow case rather than a damped one — the same philosophy for €88 more and nearly double the volume.

The build’s two loudest parts are already handled (NITRO+ at 26 dBA measured; NH-U12A with two NF-A12x25). GN’s zero-case-fan run landed within 1.5 °C of their best config, which bounds how much the chassis is contributing. Case swapping is the lowest-leverage money in this build; the open value problem remains the €519.90 RAM.

Fan layout

Resolved 2026-07-27 against GamersNexus’s bench test of this chassis. The case ships with no fans at all — all three are a purchase.

3× 120 mm, all exhaust: 2× top (rear-most positions) + 1× rear. Side and bottom stay empty.

PositionFansDirectionWhy
Rear1ExhaustSits behind the NH-U12A’s rear fan and continues the cooler’s front-to-back path out of the case. Highest-value single fan.
Top, rear two slots2ExhaustLifts the hot column off the CPU/VRM area.
Top-front0—Sits above the front-mounted PSU at F2; would recirculate front-panel air instead of pulling from the CPU region.
Side0—Feeds the GPU intake passively — see below.
Bottom0—Forced by the 64 mm card.

Side intake is the trap. GN measured ~11 °C worse GPU temperature with 2× side intake versus none, and traced why: under top/rear-exhaust-only negative pressure the GPU pulls near-ambient air through the bottom mesh and inward through the unused rear PCIe slot covers. Side fans form a competing pressure system that shoots air above the card, stops the lower third of the side panel feeding it, and reverses the PCIe slot covers from intake to exhaust. GN names this build’s situation directly: a GPU this large “will effectively cut the case in half… will only be able to pull from the bottom and rear PCIe slot covers, meaning side intake will significantly hurt its performance.” Side fans do help the CPU substantially — it is a real trade, decided for the 304 W part over the ~120 W one.

GN’s own optimal config is unavailable here. Their winner was wood front + top exhaust + 2× bottom intake (GPU 47 °C over ambient, CPU 44 °C, 32 dBA). A 25 mm bottom fan caps GPU thickness at 61 mm; the NITRO+ is 64 mm.

Caveat on transferring GN’s numbers: they tested with a 240 mm AIO up top, which exhausts CPU heat directly. The NH-U12A dumps it into the case instead, so this build depends on top exhaust more than theirs did — hence two fans there rather than one.

Why not more. GN’s “wood stock” run — zero case fans, cooler only — finished just 1.5 °C behind their best CPU configuration. The chassis is ventilated enough that marginal fans buy very little thermal headroom while each adds a noise source. Three is the sweet spot; two (1 top + 1 rear) is defensible; four or more is spending acoustics for nothing.

Fan choice — 3× Noctua NF-A12x25 PWM chromax.black.swap, €34.70 each (€104.10) at Gigantti (oxzo’s brand preference, 2026-07-27). Matches the NH-U12A chromax.black, which already carries two of the same fan. Cheapest of the three retailers for this SKU (Verkkokauppa €34.99; Jimm’s does not stock the chromax at all) and it joins the Gigantti order, so no third shipment. Supports Zero RPM, so the fans stop entirely at idle; ships with Low-Noise Adapters.

Priced alternatives: NF-S12A ×3 €77.97 (Verkkokauppa); NF-A12x25 G2 ×3 €104.70 (Jimm’s, brown/beige); NF-A12x25 brown ×3 €101.97 (Verkkokauppa); NF-P12 redux-1300 ×3 €50.97 (Verkkokauppa); Corsair RS120 PWM 3-pack €29.90 (Gigantti); ARCTIC P12 Pro PST ×3 €32.70 (Jimm’s).

Open counterpoint worth revisiting if noise disappoints: Noctua splits its range by application — the S-series is airflow-optimised for unrestricted positions, the A- and F-series are pressure-optimised for radiators and heatsinks. All three positions here are free-air exhaust, which is exactly the S12A’s design case, at €26 less for the set. The chromax A12x25 was chosen on aesthetic match and order consolidation, not on blade-type fit; at the low RPMs expected the difference should be small, but this is the assumption to test first if the build is louder than hoped.

Traps checked while pricing Corsair (recorded in case the brand question reopens): iCUE LINK QX120/RX120 need a System Hub that only the “Starter Kit” SKUs include, so “Expansion” fans bought alone will not run; RS120 MAX is a 30 mm-thick static-pressure fan wrong for free-air exhaust; and the AF120 Elite appears discontinued (0 kpl at Verkkokauppa, Jimm’s page 404s).

Assembly constraints

These are build-defining, not preferences:

  • PSU mounts at the front-offset bracket position F2.
  • No bottom fans. The case’s GPU thickness limit drops to 61 mm when a 25 mm bottom fan is fitted; the NITRO+ is 64 mm, so a bottom fan is now ruled out by clearance, not just by preference. (The PURE at 61–61.6 mm was already over the line.) The GPU instead draws fresh air directly through the filtered bottom mesh, with its own fans sitting close to that intake. The limit only binds with a bottom fan — Lian Li’s baseline clearance is a 4-slot GPU up to 415 mm — but every extra millimetre of card is a millimetre less intake plenum above the mesh, which is the cost of the NITRO+ over the PURE.
  • Top-front position: leave empty.
  • Rear and top: exhaust. All-exhaust negative pressure is the point, not a compromise — it is what lets the GPU feed off the bottom mesh and rear PCIe slot covers. See Fan layout.
  • No side fans. Bench-measured ~11 °C GPU penalty in this chassis. Helps the CPU, costs the GPU more.
  • If bottom intake later proves necessary, use 15 mm slim fans only — and test GPU temperature and noise without them first. With the 64 mm NITRO+ even this needs re-measuring before ordering fans.
  • Cooler ceiling is 165 mm. be quiet! Dark Rock Pro 5 and Noctua NH-D15 G2 are both 168 mm and do not fit. Verified fitting: NH-U12A chromax.black (158 mm), Dark Rock 5 (161 mm), Thermalright Phantom Spirit 120 SE (155 mm).
  • Case accepts ATX/SFX/SFX-L PSUs up to 220 mm. Radiators if switching to liquid later: top 360/240, side 360/280/240, bottom 240.

Why not the Pulse, despite looking like the value pick. TechPowerUp measured it at 25.5 dBA / 57 °C and called it the quietest 9070 XT they had tested. GamersNexus, testing in a hemi-anechoic chamber, found what buys that figure: memory at 90 °C (“90 becomes 95 inside a case pretty fast”), and a fan that cycles 1560 → 1400 → 1300 → 1390 → 1100 → 1258 → 1000 → 1170 → 1130 RPM under a fixed workload — “an irregular and annoying ramp/de-ramp rapidly cycling within a 2-3 minute period.” The VBIOS chases core temperature, so the fans never learn the memory is cooking. Perceived annoyance tracks variation, not absolute level: a steady 28 dBA beats a cycling 25.5 dBA. This also explains TechSpot’s independent finding of the Pulse running the warmest average GPU temp (62 °C) and hottest VRM (86 °C) — same relaxed curve, different instrument.

Two caveats on the PURE. No top-tier lab published a sound-pressure figure for it — TechPowerUp reviewed the Pulse and Nitro+ but not the PURE. Its acoustic standing rests on TechSpot’s noise-normalized results (strong) rather than a measured dBA number; the 31.9/33.6 dBA figures cited under Sources are from a weaker outlet on a non-comparable methodology. And it is white (black PCB, silver heatsink, non-white bracket) against a Wood-edition case.

Defensible upgrade — Sapphire NITRO+ OC, €955.99 (+€56). TechPowerUp measured 26 dBA under load and an 83 °C hotspot, and rated its cooler the strongest of the four tested. The 12V-2x6 connector hides behind a magnetic backplate and routes toward the motherboard — worth real money in 26.3 L with the PSU at F2, and the Seasonic ships a native 12V-2x6 cable so no adapter is involved. Against it: 64 mm vs 61 mm costs 3 mm of the bottom-mesh intake plenum this build’s cooling depends on, 1894 g vs 1233 g, and the RAM line already strains the budget.

PSU selection

Resolved 2026-07-27 against Verkkokauppa stock. The two hard requirements — a zero-RPM (semi-passive) fan mode and two independently usable PCIe cable runs, meaning two separate cables rather than one daisy-chained pair — eliminate most 850 W ATX 3.1 Gold units on the shelf.

Pick: Seasonic FOCUS GX-850 ATX 3.1 (2024), €140.90 at Jimm’s. 80 PLUS Gold, ATX 3.1 / PCIe 5.1, fully modular, selectable semi-passive mode, 135 mm FDB fan, 10-year warranty. Ships 3× PCIe 6+2 on three separate cables plus a 12V-2x6 600 W cable. At 140 mm deep it is 20 mm shorter than the Corsair — real cable-bend room at the front-offset F2 bracket in 26.3 L. Stock was thin (3–4 units).

Alternative: Corsair RM850x ATX 3.1 (2024), €163.99. Same boxes ticked, plus Cybenetics Gold, a 140 mm FDB fan, and a manual fan-override knob; also 3× PCIe 6+2 all on separate cables. 160 mm deep, €21 dearer, but deep stock (>25 units) — the safer order if buying is not immediate.

Rejected and why:

  • ASUS Prime 850W Gold ATX 3.1 (€118.99) — no zero-RPM, one PCIe cable in box, not shippable at time of check. Fails both requirements.
  • MSI MAG A850GL PCIE5 — no semi-passive operation; 4× PCIe on two daisy-chained cables; 5-year warranty.
  • Cooler Master MWE Gold 850 V3 ATX 3.1 (€122.99) — has zero-RPM, but a 120 mm ball-bearing fan at 2300 RPM max; reviewed with “high fan noise at max load” and thermal stress. Wrong unit for a silence-first build.
  • Asus TUF Gaming 850W (€104.90) — cheapest, but the fan profile is tuned for component temperature rather than noise.
  • Corsair RM850e (€148.99), ASUS ROG Strix 850W Platinum (€161.99) — both advertise zero-RPM / 0 dB; PCIe cable-run counts not verified, and neither undercuts the RM850x enough to justify chasing.

Both shortlisted units supply three independent PCIe runs, so the choice holds whether the ordered 9070 XT variant takes 2× or 3× 8-pin.

Value problems to resolve before ordering

In priority order:

  1. RAM (€500–520). The single largest value problem. Replace or postpone. The AI-datacenter DRAM shortage tripled DDR5 prices through 2026 and mid-2026 forecasts point to continued tightness — so waiting is a gamble, not a plan. If 6000 CL30 EXPO is unavailable, a 5600–6000 CL36 kit in stock beats waiting.
  2. SSD. Substitute a better-priced 2 TB TLC drive for the KC3000. Resolved 2026-07-27 — dropped to WD_BLACK SN7100 1 TB at €169.90, saving €129 against the 2 TB option and €155 against the rejected KC3000 2 TB. Accepted tradeoff: 1 TB is roughly a five-or-six-game working set once Windows or Bazzite is on it, so installs get managed rather than hoarded. Expansion is deliberately cheap — the B850M-PLUS WIFI has 3× M.2 slots and the case has 3 drive bays, so a second NVMe later needs one drive and a side panel, no rebuild.
  3. Case fans — deliberately not economised. The original concern was that three premium fans are over-spec, and on thermal grounds that still holds: cheaper sets were priced at €29.90–€50.97 and GN’s zero-fan run was within 1.5 °C of their best config. Three NF-A12x25 chromax.black at €104.10 were chosen anyway, on brand preference and match with the chromax cooler. This is the cheapest line to revisit if the budget tightens — roughly €55 recoverable. See Fan layout.
  4. Cooler. A cheaper AM5 air cooler is fine if acoustics and long-term Noctua mounting support are not priorities.
  5. Windows. Add its cost to the finished-system budget if needed. (Not required if running Bazzite/Fedora — see Linux notes.)
  6. Retailers. Consolidate only after weighing component savings against added shipping and return complexity.

Undervolting

The 9070 XT undervolts well — around −100 mV with a reduced power limit trades roughly 10 % performance for a substantial power drop (figures reported for the non-XT 9070; XT is directionally similar, not separately verified). At 1440p there are frames to spare, so this is close to free acoustic headroom. Tune after assembly rather than buying more cooling.

Linux

See wine-proton-gaming-linux, bazzite-brainhost-ops-runbook.

  • RDNA 4 is well supported on current kernels/Mesa (RADV); Bazzite and Fedora 42+ handle the 9070 XT out of the box, avoiding NVIDIA driver-packaging friction on atomic distros.
  • GPU undervolting needs amdgpu.ppfeaturemask set for full overdrive access; LACT is the usual tool (unverified against current Bazzite releases).
