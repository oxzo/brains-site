Change Management Before Coding Agents

As of 2026-11-01. Regulatory texts were read at source on that date. The SEC rule’s status, the CRA application dates and the NIST catalog version are the claims most likely to move.

What it is

Change management is how an organisation decides which changes reach production, when, and with what evidence. Before coding agents, the toolkit had two families. The ITSM family, codified in ITIL, sorts changes into classes by risk and urgency, routes the riskier ones to a change authority (often a change advisory board, CAB), and uses release windows and freezes to limit exposure at sensitive times. The SRE family, as Google documents it, gives up on preventing every bad change and limits how many users a bad change reaches through progressive rollout, automated detection and rollback.

Regulations sit on top of both. They mostly require that changes be recorded, tested, assessed and, in some regimes, approved. They rarely say how.

Why it matters

  • Changes are the dominant outage trigger in the data that exists. In Google’s sample of postmortems from 2010–2017, binary pushes triggered 37% of outages and configuration pushes 31% (Google SRE Workbook, Appendix C). These are triggers, not root causes, and the sample size is given only as “thousands of postmortems”. In the UK FCA’s data, 17% of the nearly 1,000 material incidents reported to it in 2019 were attributed to change activity.
  • Many familiar controls (the weekly CAB meeting, the holiday freeze) are not what the law asks for. Separating the regulatory requirement from the organisational habit is the first step in any redesign, including one driven by much higher change volume.

Change classes

ITIL’s own text (AXELOS, ITIL 4 “Change Enablement” practice guide) is paywalled. The account below is secondary, from a practitioner article on itsm.tools (Danby, 2026-01-02, updated 2026-08-01).

  • Standard changes are “low-risk, repeatable, and pre-authorized”, follow a documented procedure, and are “typically automated or documented in runbooks”. The human decision moves from the individual change to the class: someone approves the procedure once.
  • Normal changes “require risk assessment and approval” and follow different workflows by risk level.
  • Emergency changes are urgent changes to prevent major incidents. They are expedited and “typically require a post-implementation review”.

ITIL 4 replaced the assumption that every change goes to a CAB with a change authority that “decentralizes decision-making”. The same article lists “Automated approval processes in CI/CD pipelines” among the forms a change authority can take. That is the article author’s gloss; this note did not read an AXELOS sentence saying so.

Binding texts echo the emergency class without using ITIL’s vocabulary. The DORA RTS on ICT risk management requires “procedures to document, re-evaluate, assess, and approve emergency changes after their implementation, including workarounds and patches” (Delegated Regulation 2024/1774, Art. 17(1)(g)). The NIS2 implementing regulation requires that, when “the regular change management procedures could not be followed due to an emergency”, the entity document the result and “the explanation for why the procedures could not be followed” (Implementing Regulation 2024/2690, Annex 6.4.3).

Change advisory boards

A CAB is a standing group that reviews and approves proposed changes, typically the higher-risk normal changes. NIST SP 800-53 names it as one way to meet configuration change control: “Processes for managing configuration changes to systems include Configuration Control Boards or Change Advisory Boards that review and approve proposed changes” (CM-3 guidance). The control itself asks for a “configuration change control element” that convenes on an organisation-defined schedule. It does not require a board.

The case for CABs, as concessions in the critical sources state it (no source written in defence of CABs was read for this note):

  • DORA’s (DevOps Research and Assessment) own guidance, which is critical of CABs as approvers, says the CAB “still has a vital role” in continuous delivery: “Facilitating notification and coordination between teams”, helping with process improvement, and “Weighing in on important business decisions that require a trade-off and sign-off at higher levels of the business”. It also concedes “CABs are good at broadcasting change”.
  • The FCA’s review found “a positive correlation between the longevity of governance arrangements and higher change success rates”. Its workshop participants did not propose abolishing CABs. They “emphasised that CAB members should be carefully selected” and that a range of subject-matter experts should review changes from technical and business angles.
  • Several regimes require an approval that is independent of whoever implements the change (DORA RTS, below). A board outside the delivery team is the most legible way to show that independence to an auditor. This is an inference about why CABs persist, not a sourced finding.

The evidence against CABs as an assurance mechanism:

  • FCA, Implementing Technology Change (2021). The review covered 23 firms and over a million production changes in 2019. Overall, 1.6% of changes caused an incident. Major changes (high-risk by definition; per the FCA they “usually require management and CAB approval”) failed at 3.8% (about 2,600 incidents from nearly 68,000 major changes). “CABs approved over 90% of the major changes they reviewed and in some firms, they had not rejected a single change during 2019.” The FCA says this “may indicate that many CABs perform more of a ‘flight control’ role rather than an assurance function”. An approval rate is not a catch rate: the figure shows that CABs rarely say no, not that they catch nothing. Firms self-classified “major”, which may explain part of the higher failure rate.
  • DORA (DevOps Research and Assessment), Streamlining change approval (page last updated 2025-10-30, citing the 2019 State of DevOps Report). Approval by “people external to the team proposing the change: a change advisory board (CAB) or a senior manager” has “a negative impact on software delivery performance”, and “no evidence was found to support the hypothesis that a more formal, external review process was associated with lower change fail rates”. DORA proposes a mechanism: heavyweight approval produces “larger batches less frequently”, and so higher-impact changes. It recommends peer review “captured in the team’s development platform” to meet segregation of duties. This note did not read the 2019 report’s method or sample. The finding is from survey data and is correlational.
  • The FCA’s data agrees on the batch-size mechanism: “firms that deployed smaller, more frequent releases had higher change success rates than those with longer release cycles”. That finding is correlational.

Release windows and freezes

A release window restricts deployment to agreed times. A freeze halts non-essential change for a period, such as a holiday peak or a recovery. None of the regulatory texts read for this note mandates either one. The nearest is NIST CM-3(2) guidance: if a system must go offline for testing, “the tests are scheduled to occur during planned system outages whenever possible”.

Two documented cases show the range:

  • A freeze as a regulatory sanction. On 2023-11-01 the Monetary Authority of Singapore directed DBS Bank “to suspend all changes to the bank’s IT systems except for those related to security, regulatory compliance and risk management for a six-month period”. It cited shortcomings that included change management, after repeated disruptions in 2023. MAS also kept a 1.8× multiplier on the bank’s operational-risk capital. This is one enforcement case, not a general rule.
  • A freeze turned into a risk gate. Meta does trunk-only development, so “branch-specific freezes do not apply”. During a freeze, diffs to expert-designated paths are blocked (“red-zone”) or gated (“yellow zone”). A gated diff needs a written rationale and possibly approval from an area expert. With a diff-level risk model in the winter 2023 freeze, “only 16% of changes are gated”, and “42% more changes landed … compared to the prior year, there was a 52% decrease in outages” (Mockus et al., ACM TOSEM, 2025). The authors are Meta employees evaluating Meta’s tool, and the comparison is an uncontrolled year-over-year comparison.

The FCA also notes the human cost of the window model: “Production deployments are often not routine, requiring weekend working and overtime when incidents occur.”

What Google SRE does instead

Google’s SRE book says “SRE has found that roughly 70% of outages are due to changes in a live system”. The chapter gives no method or denominator for the figure; the workbook’s postmortem table above is the better-specified number. Its answer is automation for three things: “Implementing progressive rollouts”, “Quickly and accurately detecting problems”, and “Rolling back changes safely when problems arise”. In the book’s words, “This trio of practices effectively minimizes the aggregate number of users and operations exposed to bad changes. By removing humans from the loop, these practices avoid the normal problems of fatigue, familiarity/contempt, and inattention to highly repetitive tasks.”

The rest of the SRE material shows that this is change control, not its absence:

  • Gated operations. In Google’s release engineering, “Approving source code changes” and “Deploying a new release” are separate gated operations under access control. Tests are re-run on the release branch to create “an audit trail showing that all the tests passed”, and a “report of all changes contained in a release” is archived with the build artifacts.
  • Risk-fitted rollout. The goal is “to fit the deployment process to the risk profile of a given service”. Pre-production environments may push automatically when tests pass. Large services start in one cluster and “expand exponentially until all clusters are updated”. Sensitive infrastructure may extend the rollout “over several days”.
  • Canarying is “a partial and time-limited deployment of a change in a service and its evaluation”, run because “Some defects will reach production” whatever the pre-production testing. “Deployments should be performed by computers, not humans.” The workbook gives mechanisms, not a measured catch rate.

Progressive rollout assumes the change can be rolled back. None of these passages covers changes that cannot be undone, such as data migrations or messages already sent to third parties.

Four observations from reading these texts side by side (the drafter’s reading, not legal advice):

  1. No text read names a human approver per change, and none forbids an automated one. DORA says changes are “approved” and the RTS adds functional independence. NIST CM-3 leaves the approver to the organisation; the HIGH-baseline enhancement CM-3(1) names organisation-defined “approval authorities”. PCI says “authorized parties”. Whether an automated gate owned by an independent function counts as the approving function is not addressed in any of them.
  2. Independence is the hard requirement, not the meeting. DORA’s RTS explains it: separating approval from request and implementation is needed “to prevent conflicts of interest, and to ensure that ICT changes are evaluated objectively”.
  3. Rollback is a required control in some regimes. The DORA RTS (law) and PCI DSS (an industry standard enforced by contract) both require a defined way back. Neither distinguishes reversible from irreversible changes.
  4. NIST baselines differ. In the SP 800-53B profiles, CM-3 is absent from LOW. MODERATE adds CM-3, CM-3(2) and CM-3(4). Only HIGH adds the automated approval workflow, CM-3(1).

Incident clocks that depend on change records

Once a change causes an incident, these clocks require someone to reconstruct what changed, quickly:

  • DORA major ICT incident (Delegated Regulation 2025/301, Art. 5): initial report within four hours of classifying the incident as major and no later than 24 hours from awareness. If the incident is classified later, four hours run from that classification. Intermediate report within 72 hours of the initial notification. Final report “no later than one month” after the latest intermediate report. Non-significant entities may file by noon of the next working day when a deadline falls on a weekend or bank holiday (Art. 5(4)).
  • NIS2 significant incident (Art. 23(4), as transposed nationally; Member States may be stricter): early warning within 24 hours of awareness, notification within 72 hours, final report “not later than one month” after the notification.
  • CRA (Art. 14, applies from 2026-09-11; most of the regulation applies from 2027-12-11, Chapter IV from 2026-06-11): an actively exploited vulnerability needs an early warning within 24 hours, a notification within 72 hours and a final report “no later than 14 days after a corrective or mitigating measure is available”.
  • SEC (US-listed companies): an Item 1.05 Form 8-K is “generally … due four business days after a registrant determines that a cybersecurity incident is material” (SEC press release, 2023-07-26). It covers cybersecurity incidents, not every outage. Industry groups have asked the SEC to rescind Item 1.05. A joint trade-association letter dated 2026-04-10 (File No. CLL-15) “urge[s] the Commission to rescind Form 8-K, Item 1.05”. Whether the SEC has acted on that request is (unverified) as of 2026-11-01.

Audit (PCAOB)

(unverified, whole paragraph: the PCAOB site blocked automated reading when this note was drafted, and the paragraph rests on an earlier, unrechecked reading of AS 2201 Appendix B, paragraphs .B29 and .B31.) PCAOB auditing standards bind the auditors of US issuers, not the issuers. They treat “program change” controls as one family of IT general controls. Under AS 2201’s benchmarking provisions, an auditor may rely on an automated control without re-testing it each year if program-change controls are effective, and application stability (“few changes from period to period”) is a risk factor in that judgment. If that holds, frequent change raises audit evidence demands on the change controls themselves.

Failure modes and tradeoffs

  • Rubber-stamp approval. A board that approves over 90% of what it sees may be adding little filtering (the FCA’s reading: it “may indicate” a “flight control” role); the approval rate alone cannot show it. The cost is real either way: either the board slows delivery and drives batch size up (DORA), or it approves everything and only appears to assure.
  • Treating all changes alike. DORA lists “Treating all changes equally” as a pitfall: reviewers cannot “devote time and attention to those that require true concentration”.
  • Adding process after incidents. DORA warns that answering stability problems with “more heavyweight approvals” drives up “lead times and batch sizes, creating a vicious cycle”.
  • Emergency-class inflation. The FCA saw emergency changes at 3–5% of volume overall, but some firms classified “almost 20%” of changes as emergencies, and it linked emergency changes to legacy estates. An emergency class that grows becomes a bypass.
  • Rollback-only thinking. Progressive rollout and fallback duties assume reversibility. Data and external effects need a separate, stricter path. The sources read do not prescribe one.
  • Configuration blind spots. Configuration pushes nearly equal binary pushes as outage triggers at Google, and both Implementing Regulation 2024/2690 Annex 6.4.2 and NIST cover configuration changes. A process that gates code but not configuration misses a large share of the risk.

What changes when coding agents raise change volume

This section is inference unless a source is named. No source read for this note measures change control under agent-authored change.

  • Per-change human approval stops scaling first. If agents multiply the number of changes and each change needs a human decision, either the queue grows or the decisions thin out. The FCA’s pre-agent figure (over 90% of major changes approved by CABs) suggests the second outcome is already common. More volume would likely push it further.
  • Class-level pre-authorisation is the existing scaling mechanism. Standard changes, change authorities that can be automated (per the ITIL secondary source) and NIST CM-3(1)‘s automated approval workflow all move the human decision from the change to the class. None of the texts read forbids that. EU DORA’s independence requirement still applies to the entities it binds. On this note’s reading, the function that requests and implements a change (including through an agent) and the function that owns the approving gate must be separate.
  • Some organisations have closed this route explicitly. The UK Home Office’s internal engineering standard SEGAS-00020 (last updated 2026-03-20) says “AI‑assisted outputs MUST be reviewed and approved by a human before reaching production” and that AI-assisted changes “MUST be traceable through engineering processes”. It binds that department’s teams only. It shows that per-change human approval of AI output is a live policy choice, not a legal floor.
  • Progressive rollout and automated detection scale with volume. Approval meetings do not. Google’s argument for removing humans from repetitive release steps (fatigue, “familiarity/contempt”) applies more strongly when the steps multiply. It still leaves irreversible changes uncovered.
  • Records matter more. Incident clocks measured in hours to a month, plus root-cause reporting, require tracing an incident to the causing change quickly. More changes per day make that harder unless each change carries its provenance.
  • Freezes have to be enforced, not instructed. A freeze that exists only as an instruction or a calendar entry does not bind an automated actor. Meta’s freeze already runs as a gate on landing diffs; an agent-era freeze would need the same kind of enforcement in permissions or the pipeline.
  • The CRA preference for separate security updates pulls against continuous release trains that bundle security and feature changes. A manufacturer shipping agent-generated changes continuously would need a way to ship a security fix alone.

What needs more research

  • A measured catch rate for CAB review or canarying. The sources give approval rates and mechanisms, not the share of bad changes each control stops.
  • AXELOS’s own ITIL 4 text on whether a pipeline can be the change authority, and COBIT 2019 BAI06 (both paywalled or behind a login).
  • The DORA 2019 report’s sample and method behind the finding on external approval.
  • A primary source written in defence of CABs (AXELOS, audit profession), including the pre-filtering and deterrence arguments: changes may be revised or withdrawn before reaching a board, which an approval rate cannot show.
  • Whether any regulator or auditor has ruled on an automated gate as the “approving function” under DORA’s RTS, or as “authorized parties” under PCI DSS 6.5.1.
  • PCI SSC’s own text and guidance column for Requirement 6.5 (licence-gated; read here only through a vendor mirror).
  • The current status of SEC Item 1.05.
  • Controlled evidence on freezes. Meta’s result is one company, one year-over-year comparison.
