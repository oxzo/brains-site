Micro-ATX Kids’ Gaming PC — RX 9060 XT 16 GB (Hogwarts Legacy class, 1080p/1440p)

Comparison build against pc-build-matx-9070-xt. Same form factor and same retailers, different goal: a machine for a 9- and a 7-year-old playing Hogwarts Legacy-class single-player games. The Roblox/Fortnite layer that normally comes with that age group is not this machine’s job — it lives on the household Xbox Series X, which is what makes the Bazzite decision below possible.

Verdict: build it as a 1440p machine. The resolution question has no price attached. Inside the box, 1080p and 1440p cost the same — €0 difference — because the cheapest GPU worth buying is already a competent 1440p card and the 8 GB variants are not cheaper in Finland. On the display side a 27” QHD gaming panel (€170.99) sits inside the 1080p gaming-monitor price band (€110–200). Nothing in this build gets cheaper by targeting 1080p.

Box total as planned €1717.82 from Verkkokauppa alone (2026-07-30 pricing, excl. monitor and shipping; the fan line changed at purchase — see the order sheet) — €878.58 less than the 9070 XT build’s €2596.40, at 66 % of its cost. No OS line: this is a Bazzite machine (decided 2026-07-30, see below), so the €139.00 Windows licence the anti-cheat situation would otherwise force never gets bought.

Spec upgraded 2026-07-30 to Ryzen 5 7500X3D + 32 GB (+€295.00 over the original Hogwarts-Legacy-only draft). Both changes are driven by the same thing: WoW and Dragon’s Dogma 2 are on the list, and both are CPU- and memory-bound in ways a Hogwarts Legacy-class library is not. Neither is a resolution decision.

The remaining money fork is the 9070 GPU tier-up (+€213.09), and it is the one to skip — it buys nothing in either of the two games that drove this upgrade.

PSU — the fanless-mode comparison (2026-07-30)

The spec’s Seasonic G12 GM-650 was flagged for using S2FC (Smart and Silent Fan Control), a thermal fan curve rather than a zero-RPM mode — a step down from the standard the parent build set. This is the comparison that resolves it.

The dividing line is the FOCUS series. Seasonic’s own page for FOCUS GX ATX 3.1 describes Digital Hybrid Fan Control, a patented three-phase control with a genuine Fanless Mode up to roughly 30 % load, then Silent, then Cooling. Everything below FOCUS in Seasonic’s range uses S2FC instead.

The middle option is a trap. The CORE GX-650 at €93.99 looks like the natural half-step, but Verkkokauppa’s own feature list for it reads “Smart and Silent Fan Control (S2FC)” — the same curve as the G12 GM. €12.00 more for no change on the axis that prompted the question.

What the €50.00 actually buys, stated honestly. Fanless Mode covers ~30 % of rated load: about 225 W on the GX-750, 255 W on the GX-850. This system draws roughly 285 W under a gaming load (160 W GPU TBP + ~90 W CPU package + ~35 W for board, drive and fans) and 60–90 W at desktop.

So neither unit is fanless while Hogwarts Legacy or DD2 is running — and that is fine, because under load the GPU and CPU fans are audible and mask the PSU anyway. What it buys is a PSU fan that is completely stopped at desktop, browsing, YouTube and light games, which is exactly when the rest of this machine is already silent and a slowly-spinning PSU fan is the only thing left to hear. On a box that will spend many of its hours doing homework and Minecraft rather than DD2, that is where silence is actually noticed.

The GX-850 alternative. Its wider fanless window (255 W vs 225 W) is worth something, and it is the same unit the parent build vetted — 135 mm FDB fan, three separate PCIe cables, 10-year warranty, and a spare-parts commonality across two machines. But it is cheapest at Jimm’s €140.90 or Gigantti €141.70 (50+ kpl), and Verkkokauppa’s own stock is 2 units at €142.99. Paying €8.91–€11.00 more and adding a second shipment for 30 W of extra fanless headroom is not the trade this note has taken anywhere else.

Why the current unit stays. The G12 GM-650’s fan does not run fast at these loads, it just never stops — and behind a dampened steel panel, at 60–90 W of desktop draw, that may be inaudible in practice. €50.00 is also exactly what the CPU step-down saves, which makes this the cheaper thing to skip than the X3D. The PSU is the last part in the machine you would notice, and the only one whose upgrade is a drop-in swap later.

Re-open this only if the built machine has an audible idle hum that traces to the PSU rather than the case fans. Everything needed to act on it is above; do not re-derive the comparison.

Line by line against this build’s actual needs:

  • PCIe 5.0 x16 is the headline B850 feature and is worthless here. A 9060 XT does not saturate PCIe 4.0 x16, let alone 5.0.
  • The fast M.2 slot is a tie. M.2_1 runs PCIe 5.0 x4 on both boards with a Ryzen 7000-series CPU, and the 7500X3D is Raphael/7000. Only the 8000 series drops it to 4.0. So the storage-speed argument does not exist.
  • 2 M.2 slots vs 3 — with a 2 TB drive already fitted, that is one spare versus two. The parent build valued three slots because it starts at 1 TB and plans to expand; this one does not.

The opportunity cost is the argument that settles it. €89.91 is more than the entire silence/dust package at €59.99 — filters, dampening, a dual-tower cooler and positive pressure, all of which the kids notice every day. Two fair counterarguments, recorded so this doesn’t get re-litigated:

  1. If the 9070 XT build is ordered from Jimm’s anyway, the shipping and warranty friction is already paid and the marginal cost is purely the €89.91. That removes the logistics objection but not the value one.
  2. The €79.99 PRIME price is itself a campaign against €126.99 list. If it lapses, the gap narrows to €43.00 — closer, and worth re-running the comparison at that point, though still not compelling for a machine that cannot use the difference.

CPU — 6 cores is the right answer here

Pick: Ryzen 5 7500X3D, €229.99 (decided 2026-07-30).

WoW and Dragon’s Dogma 2 — the two titles that change the CPU pick

Asked 2026-07-30, and the answer promoted the 7500X3D and 32 GB into the default spec. Both games are CPU-bound, and both are exactly the workload 3D V-Cache exists for. AMD’s spec page confirms the 7500X3D is a real 3D V-Cache part: Zen 4, 6C/12T, 96 MB L3 against the 7600X’s 32 MB, 4.0/4.5 GHz.

World of Warcraft. The GPU is enormous overkill; a 9060 XT 16 GB will not be the limiter at 1440p in any scenario. The limiter is single-thread CPU and cache in the three places WoW gets heavy — 20-man raids, dense M+ pulls, and capital cities. A 7600X is comfortable questing and in the open world; a 96 MB L3 part is the one that holds the floor when a raid boss and forty nameplates arrive at once. Linux side is proven ground here: the Battle.net/Lutris path works, and the known failure mode is already documented in voice-proxy-crash-linux-fix (rename WowVoiceProxy.exe and the ERROR #132 mid-fight crashes stop).

Dragon’s Dogma 2 is the problem child, and no CPU in this price class fixes it. GamersNexus’s benchmark piece is titled “Dragon’s Dogma 2 is a Mess: GPU & CPU Benchmarks, Bottlenecks, & Crashes,” and the shape of the problem is structural rather than a hardware shortfall:

  • Vernworth is severely CPU-limited. Reported testing found a negligible gap between Low and Max settings inside the city — the graphics settings barely move the frame rate, which is the signature of a CPU wall. A Ryzen 5 5600X + RX 6600 at 1080p on lowest settings averaged 39 fps with 20 fps 1 % lows in Vernworth, and first-pass traversal drove 0.1 % lows to about 6 fps. (Figures from secondary reporting of GN/PC Gamer coverage, not read off the source charts.)
  • Outside the cities it is a normal GPU-bound game and the 9060 XT 16 GB handles 1440p fine. DD2 is also VRAM-hungry at higher settings, which is another mark against ever considering the 8 GB variant.
  • A 7600X is a solid step up from the 5600X in those reports, and a 7500X3D more so, but the honest expectation is good frame rates in the world and compromised 1 % lows in Vernworth. That is DD2 on every PC.

Proton status differs sharply between the two. Hogwarts Legacy is Gold/Deck Verified; DD2 is ProtonDB Silver, and the reports that run well lean on custom Proton builds (GE-Proton, CachyOS Proton) plus launch options rather than working untouched. It runs — dragons-dogma-2-mods-linux already documents the REFramework mod path under Proton on this household’s hardware — but budget setup time, and do not hand it to a child as a first-run experience.

Knock-on for RAM — this is why 32 GB is now the default. DD2 is the title that makes the question serious: 16 GB is its recommended spec, before Proton overhead and REFramework mods. Add WoW’s addon memory on top and 16 GB stops being comfortable. Because the upgrade path is replace, not add (see below), discovering this later means buying RAM twice during a price surge — so the 2×16 kit goes in at the start.

Adding two more 8 GB sticks later does not work well (corrected 2026-07-30)

The board has four DIMM slots, so this looks like a free upgrade path. On AM5 it is not, for two independent reasons:

  • Four DIMMs drop the memory speed. ASUS’s own spec sheet for the PRIME B650M-A WIFI II lists 4800 as the only non-OC rated speed — every rate from 5200 to 7600+ is marked (OC) — and footnotes that the supported data rate “vary[s] depending on the CPU and memory configuration [and] number of DRAM modules.” In practice four single-rank DDR5 sticks on AM5 land near 4800 rather than 6000. Because Infinity Fabric is coupled to memory speed on this platform, that is a permanent, everywhere loss of frames traded for capacity the machine may never need. (The 4800-with-4-DIMMs figure is the consistent community finding across Tom’s Hardware, Overclock.net and PCPartPicker threads, not a lab measurement I verified.)
  • Two kits bought separately are not a validated pair. EXPO profiles are qualified for the kit, not for two kits side by side. Even an identical part number ordered a year later can be a different die revision or batch, and it is not guaranteed to POST at rated timings.

So the real upgrade is sell or redeploy the 2×8 kit and buy a 2×16, which keeps two DIMMs and keeps 6000 CL30. That makes a 16 GB kit a full purchase rather than a down payment on 32 GB — and it is the whole reason 32 GB went into the default spec rather than being deferred.

Buying 2×16 up front is not free of that logic either: it leaves the two spare slots as an equally bad path to 64 GB. But 32 GB is where this machine’s workload actually sits, so the spare slots are decoration, not a plan.

Storage — 1 TB, and the one upgrade path that actually works

Yes, an old 3.5” spinning disk fits — with two caveats

Asked 2026-07-30. Cooler Master’s spec sheet gives the S400 4× 3.5” bays, 4× 2.5” bays and one 5.25” bay, and the HDD cage is removable and mountable in three positions — forward for a longer PSU, backwards for radiator room, or out entirely with one screw. The board has 4× SATA 6Gb/s. So capacity is not the question.

Caveat 1 — cage position versus a 304 mm GPU. Reviewers report “limited clearance to the 3.5” HDD cage, even with the cage in its furthest forward position” with a long card, fitting only after cable manipulation. The ASUS PRIME 9060 XT at 304 mm is long, if inside the 319 mm limit. Expect the cage position to be dictated by the GPU rather than chosen freely, and check it before buying extra drives.

Caveat 2 — the PSU is exactly at the limit. CM lists PSU clearance as 140 mm with the HDD cage in the backmost position. The Seasonic G12 GM-650 is 140 × 150 × 86 mm — a zero-margin fit in that position, and comfortable with the cage forward. It fits either way, but there is no slack.

The real objection is noise, not fit. A spinning disk is the one part of this machine that cannot be made quiet. In a build where €59.99 bought dampening and a fan profile that idles near-stopped, spindle whine and seek chatter become the loudest thing in the room — undoing on the noise axis exactly what the case was chosen for. The Silencio series mitigates it (dampening material behind the front panel and lining the steel side panels, rubber-padded fan mounts) but cannot remove it.

Reasonable use: bulk overflow — old game installs, media, backups — on a drive set to spin down when idle, given 1 TB is tight for this library. Unreasonable use: as the drive anything actually loads from, or as a reason to skip a second NVMe later, which the free M.2 slot makes cheap and silent.

Decided 2026-07-30: the backup disk goes in a USB dock, not in the case

Asked for a drive holding image backups rather than anything in active use, and settled the same day. Not mounting is not the same as not spinning — a SATA drive that has power spins up at POST whatever the mount state, so noauto silences the filesystem, not the platters. Solving this internally means three separate mechanisms, and solving it externally means none.

The external answer is better on its own merits, not just simpler. In a USB dock or enclosure the drive is silent, cool and unpowered whenever it is unplugged — and a backup that is physically disconnected cannot be encrypted by ransomware, deleted by a curious 9-year-old, or wiped when the machine gets reinstalled. For archive media that separation is the point, not a compromise. It also sidesteps the cage-versus-304 mm-GPU clearance question entirely.

Fans — run positive pressure, which also inverts the parent build

The parent build runs all-exhaust negative pressure, deliberately, because its 304 W card had to pull air through the bottom mesh and the rear PCIe slots. That reasoning is GPU-specific and does not apply here.

For dust, do the opposite: more intake than exhaust, so the case sits slightly pressurised and air enters through the filters instead of being sucked in through every unfiltered seam. Practical layout in the S400:

  • 2× front intake (behind the front filter) + 1× rear exhaust. Leave the top empty.
  • The case ships 1 front + 1 rear Silencio FP 120, so this needs one more 120 mm fan at minimum.

Build day — what this specific parts list needs (2026-07-31)

Not generic assembly advice; these are the things that bite this combination of parts.

The full ordered build guide is pc-build-kids-assembly-runbook (written 2026-08-01, when the build started) — bench test, case prep, cabling map, BIOS settings, Bazzite install and a no-POST table. The list below is the subset that is decisions, and stays here because it belongs with the reasoning; the runbook is the thing to have open at the desk.

Before the CPU goes in

  1. Flash the BIOS first — and this is now a likely requirement, not insurance (corrected 2026-08-01). The board has a USB BIOS FlashBack button (ASUS techspec), which flashes with only the PSU connected — no CPU, RAM or GPU needed.

    An earlier revision of this note read the ASUS CPU QVL as showing 2613 for the 7500X3D and dismissed the column as suspect because “it showed the same figure for every 7000X3D part.” That was a misreading. The column varies correctly, and the 7500X3D is on the far side of the split: the QVL gives it 3263, against 2613 for the 7600X and 7800X3D (read 2026-08-01; single read of a paginated table — re-confirm at build time).

    The split is not arbitrary. 2613 is the mid-2024 BIOS that added initial Ryzen 9000 / Zen 5 support (AGESA 1.1.7.0); 3263 is the later one covering the parts that launched after that — 9850X3D, 9700F, 9500F and the PRO 9000 series all show 3263 too (this bucket structure verified directly in the QVL table). The 7500X3D launched November–December 2025, roughly a year after 2613.

    So any board manufactured before late 2025 cannot know this CPU exists, and a B650 SKU from 2024 that has been sitting in the channel is a real possibility. The failure mode is total: no video, no beep, CPU Q-LED lit, nothing that says “BIOS.” It is recoverable — FlashBack works with the CPU installed and the machine fully built — so skipping the flash risks an hour of misdiagnosis rather than the build. Flash it anyway; it costs ten minutes at a point where nothing is assembled.

    ⚠ Target ≥3263, not “latest.” The top BIOS as of 2026-08-01 is 3886 (2026-07-02), flagged Beta on ASUS’s download page. There is no reason to run a beta here. FlashBack expects the file renamed to P650MAW2.CAP — ASUS’s BIOSRenamer utility, included in the download ZIP, does it for you.

  2. Bench-test on the box — board, CPU, cooler, one DIMM, GPU, PSU — before anything is buried behind a 304 mm card in 33.9 L.

Case prep, before the board goes in

  1. Remove the 3.5” HDD cage entirely (one screw). The backup drive is external by decision, so the cage has no job, and pulling it fixes two things at once: the reported clearance conflict between long cards and the cage, and the PSU squeeze — CM lists 140 mm PSU clearance with the cage backmost against the Seasonic’s exactly 140 mm depth.
  2. Dry-fit the GPU against both front fans before routing any cable. This is the one clearance the note never closed — see the fan section.
  3. Leave the top empty. A top exhaust would make the case 2-in/2-out and throw away the positive pressure the filtered case was chosen for.

Assembly

  1. M.2 before the GPU. The SN7100 goes in M.2_1 (top slot, PCIe 5.0 x4 with a Ryzen 7000 CPU), which sits under the graphics card. If the board ships an M.2 heatsink, peel the film off its thermal pad.
  2. RAM in A2 and B2 — second and fourth slots from the CPU. Two sticks in A1/B1 is the classic no-POST.
  3. Mount the cooler on the bench; check the Peerless Assassin’s front fan does not overhang the first DIMM.

Power

  1. The card takes exactly 1× 8-pin and ASUS recommends a 550 W PSU (ASUS techspec), so the 650 W unit is comfortable. Use the dedicated PCIe cable, not a daisy-chain off a peripheral run. The G12 GM is semi-modular — 24-pin and EPS are hardwired.
  2. PSU fan-down, over the filtered bottom vent.

First boot

  1. Enable EXPO. It does not turn itself on, and 32 GB quietly running 4800 JEDEC instead of 6000 CL30 is invisible unless you look.
  2. Do not raise SoC voltage manually on an X3D part. Run EXPO and leave it — which is why step 1 comes first.
  3. Q-Fan: flat, low curves with long ramp times on all three chassis fans. This is where the silence is actually bought; slow response also avoids the ramp/de-ramp cycling that pc-build-matx-9070-xt documents as more irritating than a steady higher noise floor.
  4. Secure Boot — Bazzite needs it disabled or its key enrolled.

The resolution question, priced

The framing was “1080p or 1440p if the price difference is low.” The difference is not low — it is zero inside the box, and roughly zero outside it.

Inside the box: the same GPU, CPU, RAM and PSU serve both. There is no cheaper GPU worth buying (TechSpot: least expensive card anyone should consider), the 8 GB variants are not cheaper locally, and 16 GB of VRAM at 1080p is not waste — it is what keeps Hogwarts Legacy’s texture streaming out of trouble. A 1080p-targeted build and a 1440p-targeted build are the same parts list.

Outside the box: 27” QHD gaming panels start at €170.99 (ASUS TUF Gaming VG27AQE5A). The 24” 1080p gaming field runs roughly €110–200 — e.g. LG 24U411B 24” FHD IPS €109.99 (2–3 week lead), MSI G255F 24.5” FHD €199.99. A 1440p monitor is cheaper than several 1080p ones. Buy for panel quality and refresh rate, not resolution tier.

Recommendation: 1440p, high preset, RT off, FSR in reserve. At 1080p the card is far above any refresh rate two young children will notice, which makes 1080p a legitimate fallback if a spare monitor already exists — but nothing about the purchase changes.

Kid-specific constraints

Not derivable from the parent build, and worth deciding before ordering:

When the second one comes up, re-run the Steam Machine comparison: two of those is €2,078 against €3,435 for two of this build, and that is the scenario where it wins.

Upgrade path — keep this platform, don’t plan a rebuild

Asked 2026-07-30: is this a 2–4 year machine that gets replaced, or one that gets upgraded? Upgraded. The expensive, slow-moving parts are all adequate and the fast-moving ones are all replaceable — which is unusual for a build at this price, and mostly an accident of AM5.

The socket is the whole argument. AMD extended AM5 support through 2029 at Computex 2026, up from the previous “2027+”, covering Zen 6 and possibly Zen 7 — reported by Tom’s Hardware and TweakTown; not read off an AMD primary source here. The board already supports Ryzen 9000 per ASUS’s own spec. So in 2028 this machine takes a then-current X3D chip without touching board, RAM, case or PSU.

Clean upgrades — drop-in, no compromise:

PathWhy it is easy
CPUAM5 to 2029. The single biggest lever, and everything else stays. The Peerless Assassin 120 SE already has headroom well past a 120 W X3D.
Storage1 of 2 M.2 slots free. Adding a drive penalises nothing — see the storage section.
PSU / cooler / fansAll standard parts, all swappable in an evening.

Constrained but workable:

  • GPU — capped at 319 mm by the case. That is a real filter, not a formality: the parent build’s Sapphire NITRO+ 9070 XT is 327 mm and would not fit. Plenty of shorter mid-range and upper-mid cards do.
  • GPU — 650 W and 2× PCIe 8/6-pin, no native 12V-2x6. Seasonic lists the G12 GM-650 at 54 A on +12 V with two PCIe 8/6-pin connectors. A future card expecting a 12V-2x6 connector needs an adapter, or the PSU swap that is already costed in the PSU comparison.
  • GPU slot is PCIe 4.0 x16 (B650). Fine for x16 cards; more of a real cost on a future PCIe 5.0 x8 card, which would run at 4.0 x8.
  • RAM to 64 GB means replacing, not adding — 2×32 in two slots. Same finding as before; the four-DIMM path drops the speed grade.

What actually gets replaced in 2028 is a CPU or a GPU, for roughly €300–500 — not a €1717.82 machine. Against that, the case for a rebuild would have to be a socket change, and AM5 removes it for the whole window in question.

Considered and rejected: buying a Steam Machine instead (2026-07-30)

Fair question, since this build’s job description — Steam single-player games on a SteamOS-family Linux with the anti-cheat titles offloaded to a console — is close to exactly what Valve’s Steam Machine is for.

It is €678.82 cheaper and roughly half the GPU. As of 2026-07: launched 29 June 2026 at €1,039 for the 512 GB model, custom AMD Zen 4 CPU with a 28-CU RDNA 3 GPU and 8 GB VRAM. GamersNexus and others place its graphics between an RX 6600 and an RX 7600 — Cyberpunk 2077 at 1440p Medium with RT and FSR Quality managed 28 fps; Horizon Zero Dawn Remastered at 1440p, 48 fps. Valve’s own silicon runs at roughly half the wattage of desktop equivalents, which is the trade for the chassis. Availability in Finland is through a randomised reservation programme, with fulfilment reported as “the end of this year.”

Against the note’s original scoping, it was genuinely competitive. A Hogwarts-Legacy-class single-player library at 1080p is squarely inside what it does, and €1,039 sits close to the €1333.83 the first draft of this build came to.

Against the spec that WoW and Dragon’s Dogma 2 forced, it is the wrong machine — on all three axes that drove the upgrades:

  1. 8 GB VRAM, permanently. This note has now rejected 8 GB twice on its own merits, and TechSpot calls the 8 GB 9060 XT “the one you should avoid.” A Steam Machine cannot be moved off it.
  2. No 3D V-Cache, at half desktop wattage. The 7500X3D’s 96 MB L3 was bought specifically for WoW raids and Vernworth. This is the workload a Steam Machine is worst at, and DD2’s CPU wall is severe on far stronger chips.
  3. Nothing is upgradeable. The section above is the whole counter-argument: this build’s value is that a 2028 CPU or GPU swap costs €300–500. A Steam Machine’s answer to 2029 is another Steam Machine.

Also worth weighing: WoW is not on Steam, so it needs Lutris or Bottles in SteamOS desktop mode — doable, but more friction on a console-shaped device than on the Bazzite desktop this household already runs.

Where it genuinely wins: two of them. The unresolved “one machine or two” question below is much cheaper to answer with Steam Machines — €2,078 for two against €3,435 for two of this build. If the real problem turns out to be a 9- and a 7-year-old fighting over one PC, that is the comparison worth running, not this one.
