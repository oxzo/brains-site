Progressive Delivery — Flags, Canaries, Staged Rollouts and Automated Rollback

Last verified 2026-11-01. Vendor and tool behaviour (LaunchDarkly, Argo Rollouts) and the 2026 Spotify report are the perishable parts.

What it is

Progressive delivery exposes a change to production a slice at a time and decides, from what the slice shows, whether to widen exposure, stop, or undo it. The pieces are separable:

  • Feature flags decouple exposing a code path from deploying it.
  • Canary releases send a small, time-limited share of traffic to the new version and compare it with a control.
  • Staged (percentage, cluster or region) rollouts widen exposure in steps.
  • Automated rollback reverses exposure when the analysis fails, without waiting for a person.

None of these tries to stop every bad change from shipping. Google’s SRE book frames the goal as limiting how many users a bad change reaches: best practice uses automation for “implementing progressive rollouts”, “quickly and accurately detecting problems” and “rolling back changes safely when problems arise”, a trio that “effectively minimizes the aggregate number of users and operations exposed to bad changes” (Google SRE book, Introduction).

Why it matters

Change is the main trigger of outages in Google’s published postmortem breakdown. Across “a sample of thousands of postmortems” from 2010–2017, Google lists binary pushes (37%) and configuration pushes (31%) as the top two outage triggers (SRE Workbook, Appendix C). The SRE book’s introduction puts it as “roughly 70% of outages are due to changes in a live system”, with no method stated. Both are one company’s self-measurement. They count triggers, not root causes, and the sampling method is undisclosed.

The second reason is that pre-production testing has a ceiling. The workbook’s canarying chapter says: “you won’t be able to detect all release-related defects until real traffic is hitting the service”, because test environments and coverage are never complete, and “If a release deploys instantly everywhere, any defects will deploy in the same way.”

Configuration pushes sit almost level with binary pushes in Google’s data. A rollout discipline that covers code but not config leaves nearly half of push-triggered outages (31 of 68 percentage points) unguarded. The canary chapter makes the same point: the principles of flags and experiments apply “to any type of change to your service, not just software releases.”

Feature flags

Four kinds, with different lifetimes

Pete Hodgson’s taxonomy (martinfowler.com, 2016) sorts flags by purpose. Purpose sets how long a flag should live and how dynamic its decision is:

KindPurposeExpected lifetime (Hodgson)Decision
ReleaseShip incomplete code dark on trunk; separate release from deploy”should generally not stick around much longer than a week or two”Static per release
ExperimentA/B or multivariate tests on user cohortsHours to weeks, long enough for significancePer request
OpsLet operators disable or degrade a feature fast; long-lived “Kill Switches”Mostly short; a few kept “almost indefinitely”Must change without a redeploy
PermissioningGate features to premium, internal or beta usersCan be “multiple years”Per request

A flag’s kind tells you who owns it, when it should die, and how fast it must be changeable. Hodgson notes that ops toggles must be re-configurable “extremely quickly”: needing a new release to flip one “is unlikely to make an Operations person happy.” A permissioning flag is product entitlement logic, not delivery machinery, and lives on that timescale.

The difference between a canary and a permissioning rollout (“Champagne Brunch” in Hodgson’s term) is who sees it. A canary exposes a random cohort. A permissioning rollout exposes a chosen set of users, such as staff or beta testers.

Flag debt

Flags have a carrying cost. Hodgson: they “introduce new abstractions or conditional logic into your code” and “a significant testing burden”. “Savvy teams view the Feature Toggles in their codebase as inventory which comes with a carrying cost and seek to keep that inventory as low as possible.” His listed countermeasures are:

  • a removal task created with every release flag;
  • expiration dates;
  • “time bombs” that fail a test or refuse startup once a flag is past its date;
  • a hard cap on the number of live flags.

The Knight Capital case is the standard cautionary tale, and the SEC’s order (Release No. 34-70694, 2013-10-16) gives the facts:

  • Knight’s new order-routing code “repurposed a flag that was formerly used to activate the Power Peg code”. Power Peg was functionality Knight had stopped using in 2003, but it “remained present and callable”.
  • During a staged installation from 2012-07-27, a technician “did not copy the new code to one of the eight SMARS computer servers”. No second technician reviewed the deployment.
  • On 2012-08-01, orders carrying the repurposed flag reached the eighth server and triggered the dead code. In about 45 minutes it produced “4 million executions in 154 stocks”, and Knight “realized a $460 million loss”.

The SEC’s findings name several failures together: dead code left on production servers, a flag reused rather than retired, code not retested after a 2005 change, and no written procedure requiring a “simple double-check” of the deployment. Flag reuse is one link in that chain, not the sole cause.

Debt does not clear itself. Uber’s Piranha paper (Ramanathan et al., ICSE-SEIP 2020) defines a flag as stale “when the purpose that necessitated its introduction is resolved”. It attributes staleness partly to ambiguous flag management, “churn in the organization affecting flag ownership”, and “lack of developer incentives”. Piranha generated deletion diffs for 1,381 flags (17% of all flags) between December 2017 and May 2019 and routed each to the flag’s author. 65% landed without changes. This is a builder’s self-report on mobile codebases.

Google’s 2025 experience report on LLM-assisted migrations covers the same problem for experiment flags: experiments “become stale” once abandoned or fully rolled out, “but the flag and the associated code remains” (Nikolov et al., 2025).

Canary releases and canary analysis

Google’s workbook defines canarying as “a partial and time-limited deployment of a change in a service and its evaluation”, and says “Canarying is effectively an A/B testing process” (Warner, Davidovič et al., 2018). The design choices it names:

  • Blast radius and error budget. In the chapter’s worked example, a release that fails 20% of requests, canaried to 5% of traffic, costs a 1% overall error rate. Impact scales with canary size times duration.
  • Duration is tied to release cadence. Daily releases cannot carry week-long canaries. With continuous deployment, “your canary duration must be significantly shorter.” The authors “strongly advise running only one canary deployment at a time”: parallel canaries add mental load and contaminate each other’s signals.
  • Sample and traffic. The canary must be “sizeable and last long enough to be representative”. “Terminating a canary deployment after receiving just a handful of queries doesn’t provide a useful signal”. Homogeneous traffic needs less volume. Slow metrics such as queue depth “may need more time or a large canary population”.
  • Thresholds cut both ways. “If the idea of acceptable behavior is overly strict, we will get lots of false positives”; if “too loose, we will be more likely to let a bad canary deployment go undetected.” The chapter counts both false positives and false negatives as the measure of canary quality, and says to re-evaluate thresholds regularly.
  • Measurement traps. Whole-service monitoring hides the canary: the 1% overall error rate “might be indistinguishable from other sources of errors”. Metrics must be split into canary and control. Metric intervals must not exceed canary duration; an hourly metric judging a 30-minute canary gives “a very muddied signal”. Shared backends create shared failure domains in which a bad canary can hurt the control.
  • Before/after is weaker than side-by-side. “Before/After Evaluation Is Risky”: it confounds the change with time of day, day of week and anything else that moved. The chapter notes that blue/green deployment is effectively a before/after canary.
  • Stage it. Start small with the bluntest signals (crashes, request failures), then widen the population and add subtler metrics.

Automating the verdict. Netflix’s Kayenta post (Netflix Technology Blog, April 2018) describes how canary analysis moved from manual to automated. Canary analysis “was initially a manual process” in which engineers compared graphs and logs; the post’s further claims that this took hours per canary and limited deploys to once or twice a week are (unverified — page blocked to automated reading on 2026-11-01). Kayenta judges each metric by confidence intervals from the Mann-Whitney U test. Spinnaker then decides to “continue, rollback, or, in some cases, prompt manual intervention”. The post also describes comparing the canary against a freshly created baseline cluster rather than the long-running production cluster (unverified, same reason). At the time of writing, Kayenta ran “approximately 30%” of Netflix’s production canary judgments, about 200 a day. No catch rate is given.

The one measured gate found. Azure’s Gandalf (Li et al., NSDI 2020) correlates failure signals with ongoing rollouts and issues go/no-go decisions across staged environments. From January to November 2018 (the paper calls this an 8-month window), on the data plane, it “captured 155 critical failures at the early stage” with 92.4% precision and 100% recall against high-impact incidents. On the control plane, across 1,200+ region-level deployments, it filed 39 incidents with 2 false alarms and missed 2 true issues. False positives were higher in the Stage and Canary environments because their failure signals are noisier.

In one of the paper’s case studies, a release manager “directly requested to bypass the no-go decision”; a later no-go in Pilot regions confirmed a real regression. These are builders evaluating their own pre-agent system, and recall is measured only against high-impact incidents.

Staged and percentage rollouts

Google’s release engineering chapter states the principle as “fit the deployment process to the risk profile of a given service” (McNutt, SRE book). Its examples:

  • Pre-production services push whenever tests pass.
  • Large user-facing services start “in one cluster and expand exponentially until all clusters are updated”.
  • Sensitive infrastructure extends the rollout “over several days, interleaving them across instances in different geographic regions”.

The same chapter flags that “configuration changes are a potential source of instability” and puts them under strict review.

Batch size is the related lever. The release engineering chapter’s rationale is that “frequent releases result in fewer changes between versions”, which makes testing and troubleshooting easier. DORA’s metrics guide names “reducing the batch size of changes” as a common way to improve all five of its metrics. In the UK FCA’s review of 23 financial firms and over 1m production changes in 2019, “firms that deployed smaller, more frequent releases had higher change success rates than those with longer release cycles” (FCA, 2021). The FCA finding is a correlation; better-engineered firms may simply do both.

Knight Capital also shows the limit of staging the install rather than the exposure. The new code went out to a few servers at a time on successive days. Nothing exercised the missed server until live flagged orders arrived, so the staging caught nothing. (Inference from the SEC order’s sequence of events.)

Automated rollback

Rollback on failed analysis is now a stock mechanism:

  • Argo Rollouts (project docs, stable): a failed analysis run “causes the Rollout to abort, setting the canary weight back to zero”. In blue/green, a failed post-promotion analysis “switches traffic back to the previous stable” ReplicaSet. An inconclusive run (neither success nor failure, for example a metric with no conditions defined) “causes a rollout to become paused at its current step. Manual intervention is then needed”. Metrics can run in dry-run mode, where they “won’t impact the final state of the rollout”.
  • LaunchDarkly guarded rollouts (vendor docs): traffic to a flag variation “progressively increases” while monitored. A regression is a “statistically significant negative impact” under sequential testing, and the response is configurable: notify, or automatically roll back. If a step does not reach a minimum number of evaluated contexts, “LaunchDarkly automatically rolls back the change”. Insufficient sample fails safe rather than passing. As of 2026-11-01 the docs list the feature on the “Enterprise plan with the Guardian add-on”. This is a vendor describing its own product.

Two design points recur across these tools. First, an unclear result goes to a person (Argo’s inconclusive pause, Spinnaker’s manual intervention). Second, too little data counts as failure, not success (LaunchDarkly). Both matter most at low traffic, where a canary may never collect enough evidence to fail. (The low-traffic framing is inference. The mechanisms are documented.)

DORA’s change fail rate counts deployments needing “immediate intervention”, which it says is “likely” a rollback or a hotfix. Its fifth metric, deployment rework rate, counts unplanned deployments caused by production incidents. Rollback frequency is therefore part of the standard measure of delivery stability, not just an operational detail.

What rollback cannot undo. Every mechanism above reverses exposure: traffic weight, flag state, the running version. None of them reverses a schema change that dropped a column, data written in a new format, a message already sent, a payment already moved, or an app binary already installed on devices (mobile staged rollouts can be halted, not reversed). (Inference. None of the sources above claims coverage of these cases, and none discusses them.)

The established way to keep schema changes reversible is parallel change, also called expand and contract (Sato, martinfowler.com, 2014):

  1. Expand the schema to support old and new forms.
  2. Migrate readers and writers, optionally behind a flag.
  3. Contract only once nothing uses the old form.

“Most database refactorings follow the parallel change pattern, where the migrate phase is the transition period between the original and the new schema.” The contract step is the irreversible one. It is the step that warrants its own, slower gate. (Inference.)

Failure modes and tradeoffs

  • Flag debt. Flags accumulate faster than they are removed. Ownership churn and weak incentives leave them stale (Piranha). Reusing a dead flag can wake dead code (Knight Capital, SEC order).
  • Canary false negatives. These come from thresholds set too loosely, samples too small to be representative, metrics slower than the canary window, and whole-service dashboards that dilute the canary’s signal (Google workbook).
  • Canary false positives come from thresholds that are too strict and from noisy early-stage environments (Google workbook; Gandalf). They cost release velocity and teach people to override the gate.
  • Overriding a correct block. Gandalf’s case study records a human bypassing a correct no-go. An automated gate is only as strong as the norms around overriding it.
  • Slow regressions pass per-release gates. Spotify: “individual releases can look healthy while smaller regressions accumulate over time, affect particular phones, or sit outside the signals we are watching”. It responded by adding longer-term trend signals to release decisions (Singer, 2026).
  • Passing every check is not safety. At Spotify, “an automated dependency upgrade passed our checks, but still failed in production, impacting end users” (Singer, 2026).
  • Shared failure domains and signal contamination. These come from shared backends, overlapping canaries, and before/after comparisons confounded by time (Google workbook).
  • Irreversible changes fall outside what rollback can fix (see above, inference).
  • Cost. Blue/green needs double capacity (Google workbook). Guarded rollouts at LaunchDarkly sit on the Enterprise plan with the Guardian add-on (vendor docs, as of 2026-11-01). Canary analysis needs per-population metrics that many monitoring setups do not produce by default.
  • Survivorship. Almost all the mechanism evidence comes from the companies that built the tools. Of the sources read for this note, only Gandalf reports precision and recall. None reports a catch rate under comparison with a no-canary baseline.

When coding agents produce more changes

Sourced:

  • Spotify’s merged changes “more than doubled year over year in August, from roughly 8,100 to 17,000”. Its incident reviews found that “the volume of change increased faster than some of our verification controls could adapt”, while not identifying AI-authored code “as a material direct contributor”. That second finding is self-assessed, and the method is not disclosed.
  • Spotify’s Fleet Management merges the “vast majority” of fleet-wide automated changes “automatically after passing safety checks”, and now includes agent-driven changes. After the dependency-upgrade failure, Spotify responded by “strengthening safeguards, expanding rollback capacity, and scheduling automated changes during owning teams’ working hours”. The post’s summary: “AI increased the capacity to produce change. The next constraint became our ability to verify it.” (Singer, 2026.)
  • Google’s LLM-assisted migration report: “Code reviews and change rollouts still require a human operator and can quickly become bottlenecks … We needed to slow down some of the migration work to avoid overwhelming the teams that had to do the reviews and to make sure that the rollouts happened in a gradual and responsible way.” (Nikolov et al., 2025. An experience report, “not a research study” by its own description.)

Inference (not established by these sources):

  • Rollout capacity is a throttle, and it is serial. Google’s advice to run one canary at a time, with duration bounded below by sample needs, means each service has a limited number of canary slots per day. If agents raise change volume, the choices are to batch more changes per canary (bigger blast radius, harder attribution), shorten canaries (weaker signal, more false negatives), or queue changes. Google slowing migration work to match review and rollout capacity is one company choosing the third option.
  • Working-hours scheduling staffs the escalation path. The inconclusive-pause and manual-intervention states in Argo and Spinnaker assume someone is there to answer. Landing automated changes when the owning team is awake makes that assumption true. It also caps automated throughput at the owners’ working hours.
  • Flag inventory likely scales with change volume. If agents ship behind release flags, more changes mean more flags to retire. Piranha-style cleanup diffs routed to a named owner are the matching counter, and they need flag ownership to survive team churn.
  • No source read for this note describes a separate deploy path for agent-authored changes. Google paced its migration rollouts and Spotify timed automated changes to owners’ working hours, but both reuse the standard rollout mechanism.

What needs more research

  • Measured canary catch rates outside Gandalf: Kayenta, Google’s canary analysis, flag-platform guarded rollouts.
  • Rollback-rate or change-fail-rate series before and after agent adoption, with a comparator.
  • Whether any organisation gives agent-authored changes a stricter rollout (longer canaries, smaller first stage) and what that costs.
  • Flag-debt growth rates in agent-heavy codebases.
  • A separate, unwritten topic: ownership of entitlements, rollout flags and kill switches as three distinct server-side concerns, and how they key onto a navigation registry.
